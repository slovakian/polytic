# Moral circle

A Foundation Dimension in the Core, in the taxonomy group _Who counts_. Copied from
`docs/dimension-taxonomy.md`, which wins on any difference.

## The Dimension

- **Poles:** Near and dear first ↔ Everyone counts equally
- **Definition:** Do we owe more to those close to us (family, community, nation), or
  should everyone's interests count equally wherever they are?

## Using these skeletons

This is a Foundation Dimension: every skeleton asks what the person takes to be true or
deserved, never what the law should do. Keep it that way when you localise. Pole order
means nothing. Localise only as `docs/dilemma-skeletons.md` allows: swap generic
institutions, places, and amounts for local ones; never change the collision, a fact
either side relies on, or either Case. For "Push me harder", use the **Turn the dial**
variant that pushes against the person's answer.

## Dilemma Skeletons

### The burning house

- **Tests:** Moral circle: _Near and dear first_ ↔ _Everyone counts equally_
- **Also touches:** Outcomes or rules
- **Facet:** Family against strangers
- **Scenario:** A house is on fire. You can reach your own child or two children you
  have never met, in another room, but not both. Most people would save their own. Is
  that the right thing to do, or an understandable failing?
- **Localise:** Nothing needs localising. Never change the numbers in the scenario
  itself.
- **Case for _Near and dear first_:** Special duties to the people we love are not a
  bias to be forgiven; they are part of morality. A parent who weighed their own child
  as one among many would be missing something essential. Love that plays no
  favourites is not love.
- **Case for _Everyone counts equally_:** Each of those children matters exactly as
  much as mine, and two lives are more than one. I would probably save my own, and I
  could be forgiven for it, but that is a fact about my heart, not about whose life
  is worth more.
- **Turn the dial:**
  - **Toward _Near and dear first_:** It is your child or one stranger's child.
  - **Toward _Everyone counts equally_:** It is your child or ten strangers' children.
- **Rules:** —

### The winter gift

- **Tests:** Moral circle: _Near and dear first_ ↔ _Everyone counts equally_
- **Also touches:** Compatriot priority vs. global impartiality
- **Facet:** Local community against distant strangers
- **Scenario:** You have a sum of money to give away. It could get a struggling family
  in your own town through a hard winter, or, through a reliable charity, save several
  children far away from going blind. Which do you owe more?
- **Localise:** The currency and the local hardship (heating, rent, food). Never
  change that the distant gift does several times more good.
- **Case for _Near and dear first_:** My town is the community whose life I share and
  that shares mine; its people have claims on me that strangers far away do not. If
  everyone looks after their own, everyone is looked after, and a duty owed to all of
  humanity at once is owed to no one in particular.
- **Case for _Everyone counts equally_:** Distance does not change how much a child's
  sight matters. The same money does several times more good abroad, and never
  meeting those children does not make them count less. Where someone happens to live
  is not a moral fact about them.
- **Turn the dial:**
  - **Toward _Near and dear first_:** The family in your town is your cousin's.
  - **Toward _Everyone counts equally_:** The distant gift would save ten children's
    lives, not their sight.
- **Rules:** —

### The two floods

- **Tests:** Moral circle: _Near and dear first_ ↔ _Everyone counts equally_
- **Also touches:** Basis of nationhood, Compatriot priority vs. global impartiality
- **Facet:** Fellow nationals against foreigners
- **Scenario:** On the same day, a flood kills twenty people in your own country and
  two hundred in a distant one. Should the deaths at home weigh more with you, morally,
  or is it just natural that they do?
- **Localise:** The disaster may be any natural one. Never change the ten-to-one ratio.
- **Case for _Near and dear first_:** A nation is a shared life: a common history,
  language, and mutual dependence. Fellow members owe each other more, as families do,
  and grief that follows those ties is fitting, not a mistake to be corrected.
- **Case for _Everyone counts equally_:** A death is a death. Nationality is an
  accident of birth, and morally the two hundred matter ten times as much. Feeling
  closer to the twenty is a fact of psychology, not a reason.
- **Turn the dial:**
  - **Toward _Near and dear first_:** Some of the twenty were from your own town.
  - **Toward _Everyone counts equally_:** Your country's own actions helped cause the
    distant flood.
- **Rules:** —

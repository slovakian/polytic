# Tempo of change

A Political Dimension in the Core, in the taxonomy group _Political change_. Copied from
`docs/dimension-taxonomy.md`, which wins on any difference.

## The Dimension

- **Poles:** Fundamental transformation ↔ Incremental reform
- **Definition:** When society needs to change, should it change step by step, keeping
  what works, or fundamentally and quickly? Direction-neutral: one can be an incremental
  progressive or a radical restorationist.
- **Facets:** Speed; scope; reversibility.

## Using these skeletons

This is a Political Dimension: every skeleton asks what the law, the state, or public
institutions should do. Pole order means nothing. Localise only as
`docs/dilemma-skeletons.md` allows: swap generic institutions, places, and amounts for
local ones; never change the collision, a fact either side relies on, or either Case.
For "Push me harder", use the **Turn the dial** variant that pushes against the person's
answer.

The taxonomy defines this as direction-neutral, so the three skeletons point in
different directions: a public-service redesign, a restoration of an older order, and
an economic transition that can run either way.

## Dilemma Skeletons

### The failing health system

- **Tests:** Tempo of change: _Fundamental transformation_ ↔ _Incremental reform_
- **Also touches:** Design vs. evolved order, Human nature
- **Facet:** Scope and speed of reform to a public service
- **Scenario:** A country's health system is widely agreed to be failing. One plan
  replaces it entirely with a new design within three years. The other makes a series
  of smaller changes over fifteen years, keeping what works. Which should the country
  choose?
- **Localise:** The country's current system, described neutrally. Never say what
  the new design is; the question is speed and scope, not model.
- **Case for _Fundamental transformation_:** When a system is failing, patching keeps
  its failures alive. Tinkering lets every vested interest defend its corner, and only
  a clean redesign can deliver what people need within their own lifetimes.
- **Case for _Incremental reform_:** Large systems hold knowledge no planner can see.
  Replacing everything at once risks breaking what works with no way back, and small,
  reversible steps let a country learn and correct as it goes.
- **Turn the dial:**
  - **Toward _Fundamental transformation_:** Three rounds of piecemeal reform over
    twenty years have left it worse.
  - **Toward _Incremental reform_:** The last country that replaced its system at once
    saw waiting times double during the changeover.
- **Rules:** —

### The restoration

- **Tests:** Tempo of change: _Fundamental transformation_ ↔ _Incremental reform_
- **Also touches:** Tradition or reason, Direction of history
- **Facet:** Speed of a return to an older order
- **Scenario:** A movement believes the country's culture and institutions have been
  hollowed out over fifty years. It wants to restore the older order quickly and
  wholesale, through a sweeping new constitution, before it is too late. Others who
  share the same aims want to reverse course step by step. Which approach should those
  who share the aim take?
- **Localise:** The older order may be any the person's country has a movement to
  restore (religious, monarchical, national, or social). Never name a real movement.
- **Case for _Fundamental transformation_:** The decline happened through many small
  steps. If every reversal has to be small, the forces that produced the decline will
  outlast the effort, and a moment of public will like this one may never come again.
- **Case for _Incremental reform_:** A society cannot be remade, even back into an
  older shape, without uprooting everything people have built their lives on since.
  Sudden reversal provokes backlash and damage no one intended, and lasting change is
  gradual.
- **Turn the dial:**
  - **Toward _Fundamental transformation_:** The older order is still within living
    memory, and a clear majority wants it back.
  - **Toward _Incremental reform_:** The last sweeping constitution was repealed
    within a decade.
- **Rules:** The person's view of the older order itself is not this Dimension; record
  it on the Dimensions it concerns.

### The overnight transition

- **Tests:** Tempo of change: _Fundamental transformation_ ↔ _Incremental reform_
- **Also touches:** Economic coordination, Design vs. evolved order
- **Facet:** Speed of an economic transition
- **Scenario:** A country has decided, by a large majority, to change its economic
  model fundamentally: from state-run to market, or from market to publicly owned.
  Some advisers want to do it all at once; others want to phase it in over a decade.
  Which should it do?
- **Localise:** The direction may be set to whichever transition is debated in the
  person's country, or left open. Never let the direction become the question.
- **Case for _Fundamental transformation_:** Half-reforms create the worst of both
  systems, and the window of public consent is short. Doing it at once lets people
  adjust to clear new rules quickly and stops the old interests from regrouping.
- **Case for _Incremental reform_:** Overnight change destroys jobs, savings, and
  institutions before new ones exist, and the people who bear the cost have no time to
  adapt. A gradual path lets the new economy, and trust in it, grow together.
- **Turn the dial:**
  - **Toward _Fundamental transformation_:** A similar country's gradual reforms
    stalled after a few years, captured by the old interests.
  - **Toward _Incremental reform_:** A similar country that changed overnight saw life
    expectancy fall during the transition.
- **Rules:** The person's view of which model is better is recorded on _Economic
  coordination_, not here.

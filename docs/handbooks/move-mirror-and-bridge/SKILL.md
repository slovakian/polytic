# Micro-Mirror and bridge

The person has just given a clear principle and their reason for it. Show them, in one
sentence, that you have the exact line they drew. Then carry that rule into a new topic
and ask whether it still holds.

This is how the grill moves between topics without marching down a list, and the
Micro-Mirror is the person's running proof that they are being understood.

## What the brief gives you

- **The principle**, as the Note-taker recorded it from the person's words.
- **The topic it came from** and **the topic to bridge to**, with that topic's Dilemma
  Skeleton.
- The person's country, or none.

## How to write it

1. **The Micro-Mirror: one sentence.** State their dividing line sharper than they said
   it: what the rule allows, what it rules out, and why, in their concepts. It should
   be specific enough that they could say "no, that's not quite it."
   - Good: "For you, the state may break up monopolies but not equalise outcomes,
     because what matters is that the game is open, not how it ends."
   - Bad: "So you care a lot about fairness." (vague, could be anyone)
   - Bad: "That's a classic liberal position." (a label)
2. **The bridge: one or two sentences.** Name the new ground and put the rule to work
   on a concrete case from the target topic's skeleton, localised as in the probe move.
   Ask whether the rule still holds, or what makes this case different.
3. Two to three sentences in all. One question, last.

## Examples

From Paternalism to Distribution:

> For you, the law can stop people harming others but never protect them from
> themselves, because adults own their own risks. Does that hold for money too: if
> someone spends their pension early and ends up destitute at 75, is the state
> obliged to catch them?

From Environmental protection to Future generations:

> Your line is that a country may restrict its own industry when the harm lands on
> people alive now, not on hypothetical ones. Does that change if the people harmed
> are your grandchildren, fifty years from now?

From Transcendence to Legal moralism:

> For you, morality comes from God, but faith can't be compelled, so the law shouldn't
> enforce belief. Where does that leave a law against something your faith calls a
> sin but which harms no one else?

## Don't

- Don't praise the principle or say it's clear, coherent, or well put.
- Don't mirror anything they didn't say. If the brief's principle is shaky, mirror less
  and keep to their words.
- Don't name a tradition, thinker, or camp.
- Don't pick a bridge case that only bites one way. The rule should be at genuine risk
  either way.

# Probe with a Diagnostic Dilemma

Put one concrete case to the person where the two sides of a question collide, and ask
which gives way and why. This is the grill's default move.

## When the Turn Brief chooses it

- **The first turn**, on the topic of the person's opening thread (follow the heat).
- **A slogan, a dodge, or a surface answer**: test it with a case where the slogan
  collides with something else the person is likely to care about.
- **Moving into a new topic** when there's no clear principle to bridge from.
- **"Push me harder"**: the brief says harder. Use a sharper case on the same topic.
- **The person is leaning in** (asking back, working through it): go one case deeper.

## What the brief gives you

- The topic and the **Dilemma Skeleton**: the two sides that collide, a scenario with no
  country attached, the strongest case for each side, two **Turn the dial** variants
  (one toward each side), and any special rules.
- The person's country, or none.
- Sometimes the person's own words to test (their slogan or stated rule).
- Sometimes "harder".

## How to write it

1. **Localise the scenario** to the person's country: a real institution, office, or
   familiar kind of case. Keep the collision exactly as the skeleton has it. If you
   don't know a local detail, keep it generic.
2. **Make both sides cost something.** The case must put something the person could
   value on each side. A case where one side is obviously monstrous or obviously free
   isn't a dilemma.
3. If the brief gives their words, **anchor to them in a short clause** ("You said the
   state should stay out of private choices."), then the case. Quote, don't
   characterise.
4. **Ask one open question**: which gives way, where they draw the line, or what would
   have to be true for them to answer the other way. Never yes/no, never a menu of
   answers.
5. Keep it to **1–3 sentences**. Each side's case gets at most a clause here; the full
   steelman is the unpack move's job.

### Harder

- Use the skeleton's **Turn the dial** variant that pushes against the side the person
  has taken: it raises the cost of their answer by one fact. If they have already had
  it, move to the edge case where their rule bites hardest. Same evenness: harder never
  means hostile, and it never argues for the other side's answer.

### Special rules from the skeleton

Follow them as written. Some topics have fixed dilemmas (abortion; medical transition
for minors) to be used as written, and some must stay historical or abstract (force in
domestic politics). Don't swap in a case of your own.

## Examples

Distribution, person in Germany, said "people should keep what they earn":

> You said people should keep what they earn. A founder sells her company and leaves
> €40 million to a son who has never worked; should inheritance be taxed like earned
> income, and why or why not?

Same Dimension, person said "inequality is the root of everything wrong":

> A pharmacist who works sixty-hour weeks for twenty years ends up far richer than a
> neighbour who chose to work part-time. Should the law narrow that gap too, and on
> what grounds?

Expert trust (a Foundation Dimension, so no policy question), harder after the person
said to trust the experts, generic country:

> Your health agency recommends a new treatment for your child, and a friend who has
> read the trial data thinks the risks are understated. Suppose the treatment's maker
> paid for every trial. Whose judgment decides what you do?

## Don't

- Don't invent a collision the skeleton doesn't have, or soften one side's case.
- Don't explain why the question matters or what it reveals.
- Don't add "there's no right answer" or any reassurance.
- Don't put words in their mouth ("you'd presumably say…").

# Foreign policy and world order: Label cards

Cards for this family, written to the template in `../label-library.md`, in the order
of the list. Every card here is written with the same dispassion as any other: the
self-description is the tradition's own self-understanding, steelmanned, and never
Polytic's verdict (`../principles.md`). These are schools of foreign policy and are
usually an Area Label. Liberal internationalism, Neoconservatism, and Paleoconservatism,
which also carry foreign-policy views, have their cards in their own families.

## Realism

- **Type:** Ideology (usually Area)
- **Parent:** —
- **Also called:** political realism; classical realism (Morgenthau); structural realism
  or neorealism (Waltz, Mearsheimer); _realpolitik_; Hamiltonian (Mead's typology)
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                                   | Range                                                                                      | Central |
| ------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------- |
| Foreign-policy aims                         | Leans _National interest_ – Strongly _National interest_                                   | Yes     |
| Use of force                                | Leans _Readiness to use force_ – Leans _Reluctance to use force_                           | No      |
| Sovereignty vs. pooled governance           | Leans _Sovereign self-government_ – Strongly _Sovereign self-government_                   | No      |
| Compatriot priority vs. global impartiality | Strongly _Special obligations to compatriots_ – Leans _Special obligations to compatriots_ | No      |
| Zero-sum or positive-sum                    | Leans _One's gain is another's loss_ – Middle                                              | No      |
| Human nature                                | Strongly _Fixed and flawed_ – Middle                                                       | No      |
| Outcomes or rules                           | Strongly _Best outcomes_ – Leans _Best outcomes_                                           | No      |
| Direction of history                        | Leans _Progress is not assured_ – Strongly _Progress is not assured_                       | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** There is no world government to protect states from one another,
so each must look to its own security, and power, not good intentions, decides what it
can do. Foreign policy that forgets this, crusading to remake other societies in its own
image, overreaches and ends in disaster. The responsible statesman pursues the national
interest prudently: balancing against dangerous powers, respecting other great powers'
spheres where that keeps the peace, and judging policy by its consequences rather than
by the purity of its aims. Restraint and prudence are themselves moral virtues in a
dangerous world.

**Key concepts.**

- **Anarchy:** the absence of any authority above states, which forces each to rely on
  itself (self-help).
- **National interest:** defined in terms of power and security, the proper guide of a
  state's conduct abroad.
- **Balance of power:** peace is kept less by law or institutions than by states
  checking one another's power.
- **Prudence:** the supreme political virtue, weighing consequences over abstract
  principle (an ethic of responsibility).
- **Security dilemma:** one state's defensive build-up looks threatening to others, so
  tragedy can arise without malice.

**Signature reasons.**

- "The strong do what they can and the weak suffer what they must." (Thucydides)
- "Nations have no permanent friends, only permanent interests."
- "We can't fix other countries, and trying usually makes things worse."
- "Great powers have spheres of influence whether we like it or not."
- "Judge a foreign policy by its consequences, not its intentions."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Liberal internationalism:** believes trade, institutions, and democracy can tame
  international anarchy and that values belong in foreign policy (_Foreign-policy aims_,
  the central Dimension; _Sovereignty vs. pooled governance_).
- **Neoconservatism:** shares the focus on power but uses it to spread democracy
  (_Foreign-policy aims_).
- **Restraint:** draws on realism but wants far fewer foreign commitments and alliances;
  many realists accept alliances and forward deployment to balance rivals (_Use of
  force_).
- **Christian realism:** a Foundation Label grounding power politics in sin (Niebuhr);
  realism as a foreign-policy school can rest on it or on secular grounds
  (_Transcendence_).
- **Non-alignment:** a stance of states outside the great-power blocs, grounded in
  anti-colonial solidarity and peaceful coexistence rather than power-balancing
  (Reasons).

**Sources.** Thucydides, _History of the Peloponnesian War_ (the Melian Dialogue);
Niccolò Machiavelli, _The Prince_ (1532); E. H. Carr, _The Twenty Years' Crisis_ (1939);
Hans Morgenthau, _Politics Among Nations_ (1948); George Kennan, _American Diplomacy_
(1951); Kenneth Waltz, _Theory of International Politics_ (1979); Henry Kissinger,
_Diplomacy_ (1994); John Mearsheimer, _The Tragedy of Great Power Politics_ (2001).

## Restraint

- **Type:** Ideology (usually Area)
- **Parent:** Realism / Libertarianism
- **Also called:** non-interventionism; offshore balancing (its realist strategy);
  Jeffersonian (Mead's typology); America First (the 1940 committee and some current
  uses); critics: "isolationism"
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                         | Range                                                                | Central |
| --------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------- |
| Use of force                      | Leans _Reluctance to use force_ – Strongly _Reluctance to use force_ | Yes     |
| Foreign-policy aims               | Middle – Strongly _National interest_                                | Yes     |
| Sovereignty vs. pooled governance | Middle – Strongly _Sovereign self-government_                        | No      |
| Security and civil liberties      | Strongly _Civil liberties and due process_ – Middle                  | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** A country protected by oceans, nuclear deterrence and a strong
economy is safe enough not to police the world. Decades of interventions, open-ended
wars and alliances that commit it to fight for others have cost lives and fortunes,
created enemies and rarely produced the democracies promised. Foreign policy should
defend the homeland and trade freely with everyone, but avoid wars of choice, let
wealthy allies defend themselves, and keep the military out of other countries' civil
conflicts. A republic at war abroad also loses liberty at home.

**Key concepts.**

- **Wars of choice:** military action not required by a direct threat to the nation,
  which restraint presumes against.
- **Entangling alliances:** commitments that can drag a country into others' wars
  (Washington, Jefferson).
- **Offshore balancing:** stay out of regions unless a hostile power threatens to dominate
  one, then act with or behind local partners.
- **Blowback:** interventions breed the hostility and terrorism they aim to prevent.
- **Free-riding:** permanent protection lets rich allies underspend on their own defence.

**Signature reasons.**

- "America goes not abroad in search of monsters to destroy." (J. Q. Adams)
- "Peace, commerce, and honest friendship with all nations; entangling alliances with
  none." (Jefferson)
- "Our wars in the Middle East made us less safe, not more."
- "Rich allies can pay for their own defence."
- "War is the health of the state."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Realism:** shares the interest-based view, but many realists accept a forward
  military presence and alliances to balance rivals (_Use of force_, the central
  Dimension).
- **Pacifism:** a Thin Label refusing war in principle; restraint keeps a strong defence
  and would fight for the homeland (_Use of force_; Reasons).
- **Paleoconservatism:** often carries restraint as its foreign policy, alongside
  cultural traditionalism and economic protection; restraint itself leaves domestic
  questions open.
- **Liberal internationalism and Neoconservatism:** see a forward role in alliances and
  defending a liberal order as a source of security (_Use of force_; _Foreign-policy
  aims_).
- **Non-alignment:** the stance of states staying out of great-power blocs; restraint is
  a great power's choice to shed commitments (Reasons).

**Sources.** George Washington, Farewell Address (1796); Thomas Jefferson, First
Inaugural Address (1801); John Quincy Adams, Independence Day address (1821); Randolph
Bourne, "The State" (1918); Patrick Buchanan, _A Republic, Not an Empire_ (1999);
Andrew Bacevich, _The Limits of Power_ (2008); Christopher Preble, _The Power Problem_
(2009); Barry Posen, _Restraint_ (2014); John Mearsheimer & Stephen Walt, "The Case for
Offshore Balancing" (2016); Stephen Walt, _The Hell of Good Intentions_ (2018).

## Non-alignment

- **Type:** Ideology (usually Area)
- **Parent:** —
- **Also called:** the Non-Aligned Movement; positive neutralism (Nasser, Nkrumah);
  strategic autonomy and multi-alignment (its present-day forms, notably in India)
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                         | Range                                                                | Central |
| --------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------- |
| Sovereignty vs. pooled governance | Middle – Strongly _Sovereign self-government_                        | Yes     |
| Foreign-policy aims               | Leans _Universal values_ – Leans _National interest_                 | No      |
| Use of force                      | Leans _Reluctance to use force_ – Strongly _Reluctance to use force_ | No      |
| Historical injustice              | Middle – Leans _Rectification_                                       | No      |
| Trade and economic openness       | Middle – Leans _Economic protection and self-reliance_               | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Nations that have just won their freedom from empire should not
hand it to a new master by joining someone else's bloc. Each should decide its own
course on the merits, keep friendly relations with all, and refuse to be a pawn in
great-power rivalries. Peaceful coexistence, mutual respect for sovereignty and
non-interference in each other's affairs are the basis of a just world order, and the
formerly colonised have a common interest in standing together for decolonisation,
disarmament and a fairer world economy.

**Key concepts.**

- **Non-alignment:** independence of judgment and refusal of membership in military
  blocs, not neutrality or indifference.
- **Peaceful coexistence:** the Five Principles: mutual respect for sovereignty and
  territorial integrity, non-aggression, non-interference, equality and mutual benefit.
- **Sovereignty:** hard-won independence that must not be traded for protection.
- **Decolonisation:** the unfinished liberation of colonised peoples, a shared cause of
  the movement.
- **Strategic autonomy:** in its present form, working with several powers issue by issue
  while binding oneself to none.

**Signature reasons.**

- "We didn't free ourselves from one empire to join another."
- "We'll be friends with everyone and the satellite of no one."
- "Judge each issue on its merits, not by which bloc proposed it."
- "Non-interference works both ways."
- "The global South has more in common with each other than with any great power."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Restraint:** a great power's choice to shed commitments; non-alignment is the stance
  of states refusing to join any power's camp, grounded in anti-colonial solidarity
  (Reasons).
- **Realism:** pure national interest and power-balancing; non-alignment adds principles
  of peaceful coexistence and Third World solidarity (_Foreign-policy aims_; Reasons).
- **Anti-imperialism:** a Thin Label against imperial domination that can ride on
  non-alignment, or on revolutionary traditions that take sides.
- **Liberal internationalism:** favours alliances among democracies and pooled
  institutions (_Sovereignty vs. pooled governance_, the central Dimension).
- **Dependency theory:** an economic analysis of the periphery that informed the
  movement's calls for a new international economic order (Reasons).

**Sources.** The Five Principles of Peaceful Coexistence (Panchsheel, 1954); Final
Communiqué of the Asian–African Conference, Bandung (1955); Declaration of the Heads of
State or Government of Non-Aligned Countries, Belgrade (1961); Jawaharlal Nehru, _India's
Foreign Policy: Selected Speeches, September 1946 – April 1961_ (1961); Kwame Nkrumah,
_I Speak of Freedom_ (1961); Peter Willetts, _The Non-Aligned Movement_ (1978); Vijay
Prashad, _The Darker Nations_ (2007); S. Jaishankar, _The India Way_ (2020).

## World federalism

- **Type:** Ideology (usually Area)
- **Parent:** Cosmopolitanism
- **Also called:** global federalism; the world government movement; democratic world
  federalism; the World Federalist Movement
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                                   | Range                                                                                  | Central |
| ------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------- |
| Sovereignty vs. pooled governance           | Strongly _Pooled, rule-bound international governance_                                 | Yes     |
| Compatriot priority vs. global impartiality | Leans _Impartial concern for all people_ – Strongly _Impartial concern for all people_ | No      |
| Moral circle                                | Leans _Everyone counts equally_ – Strongly _Everyone counts equally_                   | No      |
| Use of force                                | Leans _Reluctance to use force_ – Strongly _Reluctance to use force_                   | No      |
| Rule of law vs. discretion                  | Leans _Rule by general, fixed rules_ – Strongly _Rule by general, fixed rules_         | No      |
| Direction of history                        | Strongly _History is progressing_ – Leans _History is progressing_                     | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Within countries we stopped settling disputes by force when we put
everyone under a common law with courts and police; between countries we still live in
a state of nature, now armed with nuclear weapons. Treaties and leagues of sovereign
states cannot end war, because no one can enforce them on the strong. The answer is a
federal world government with limited, defined powers over war, weapons and truly
global problems, democratically accountable to the world's citizens, while nations keep
their own affairs, as states do within a federation. World peace comes through world
law.

**Key concepts.**

- **World law:** binding law above states, enforceable on individuals, in place of
  treaties among sovereigns.
- **Federalism:** a world government limited to global matters, with nations keeping
  authority over the rest (subsidiarity at the global level).
- **International anarchy:** the root cause of war, which only a common government can
  end.
- **World citizenship:** every person is a member of humanity with a voice in the
  decisions that affect all.
- **Global problems:** nuclear weapons, climate and pandemics cannot be solved by
  sovereign states acting alone.

**Signature reasons.**

- "Peace through world law."
- "We ended private wars by creating governments. We can end national wars the same
  way."
- "A league of sovereign states can't stop the strongest of them."
- "Global problems need a global government."
- "I'm a citizen of the world first."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Liberal internationalism:** wants cooperation and institutions among sovereign
  states, not a government above them (_Sovereignty vs. pooled governance_ at its far
  end; Reasons).
- **European federalism:** a Thin Label for federal union of Europe only; world
  federalists often see it as a regional step toward their goal.
- **Cosmopolitanism:** the parent Thin Label, a moral view that all humans form one
  community; world federalism is a programme of institutions for it (Reasons).
- **Realism:** holds that world government is impossible or would be tyrannical, and that
  peace rests on balancing power (_Sovereignty vs. pooled governance_; _Foreign-policy
  aims_).
- **Pacifism:** refuses war; world federalists accept enforcement by a world authority
  (_Use of force_; Reasons).

**Sources.** Immanuel Kant, _Perpetual Peace_ (1795); Emery Reves, _The Anatomy of Peace_
(1945); the Montreux Declaration of the World Movement for World Federal Government
(1947); Committee to Frame a World Constitution, _Preliminary Draft of a World
Constitution_ (1948); Grenville Clark & Louis Sohn, _World Peace Through World Law_
(1958); Daniele Archibugi & David Held (eds.), _Cosmopolitan Democracy_ (1995); Alexander
Wendt, "Why a World State Is Inevitable" (2003); Luis Cabrera, _Political Theory of
Global Justice_ (2004).

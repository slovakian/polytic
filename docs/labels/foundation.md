# Foundation Labels: Label cards

Cards for this family, written to the template in `../label-library.md`, in the order
of the list. A Foundation Label is a named position on Foundation Dimensions, so its
Positions list only Foundation Dimensions, and it is never a person's Main Label
(`../framework.md`). Every card here is written with the same dispassion as any other:
the self-description is the tradition's own self-understanding, steelmanned, and never
Polytic's verdict (`../principles.md`).

Foundation Labels have no parent, except where every holder of one also fits another's
central Positions on the same question. Then the narrower one is the child, so the
most-specific rule shows only it: Christian realism under Constrained vision, and
Divine command theory and Natural law under Moral realism.

## Libertarian free will

- **Type:** Foundation
- **Parent:** —
- **Also called:** incompatibilist free will; agent causation and event-causal
  libertarianism (its two main forms); metaphysical libertarianism (unrelated to the
  political Libertarianism)
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension | Range                              | Central |
| --------- | ---------------------------------- | ------- |
| Free will | Strongly _Self-authored agency_    | Yes     |
| Desert    | Strongly _Earned_ – Leans _Earned_ | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** When I deliberate and choose, the outcome is genuinely open, and I
am its source: my choice is not settled in advance by my genes, my upbringing, or the
state of the world before I was born. That is what makes praise, blame, and real
responsibility fitting, and it is how every person experiences their own agency. If
determinism were true of us, responsibility would be an illusion, so we have strong
reason to think it is not.

**Key concepts.**

- **Freedom:** the ability to do otherwise in exactly the same circumstances, and being
  the ultimate source of one's own choices.
- **Responsibility:** deserving praise and blame for what one does, which requires
  control that determinism would rule out (incompatibilism).
- **Causation of choice:** either the agent, not a prior event, causes the choice (agent
  causation: Reid, Chisholm, O'Connor), or the choice is an undetermined event in the
  agent's own deliberation (Kane).

**Signature reasons.**

- "I could have done otherwise; that's what makes it my choice."
- "If everything I do was fixed before I was born, no one could really deserve blame."
- "The experience of deciding is evidence, and we shouldn't explain it away."
- "Being the ultimate source of what you do is part of what it is to be a person."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Compatibilism:** holds that responsibility survives determinism; Libertarian free
  will says it does not, and that our choices are undetermined (_Free will_ further
  toward _Self-authored agency_; Reasons).
- **Free-will skepticism:** agrees that determinism rules out deserved blame (both are
  incompatibilist), but concludes that no one has such freedom (_Free will_, _Desert_).
- **Existentialism:** also stresses radical freedom, but as a claim about how to live
  and author one's commitments, not a theory of how choices are caused (Reasons).

**Sources.** Thomas Reid, _Essays on the Active Powers of Man_ (1788); Roderick Chisholm,
"Human Freedom and the Self" (1964); Peter van Inwagen, _An Essay on Free Will_ (1983);
Robert Kane, _The Significance of Free Will_ (1996); Timothy O'Connor, _Persons and
Causes_ (2000); Randolph Clarke & Justin Capes, "Incompatibilist (Nondeterministic)
Theories of Free Will", _Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy_.

## Compatibilism

- **Type:** Foundation
- **Parent:** —
- **Also called:** soft determinism (historical, William James's term); semi-compatibilism
  (Fischer, one form); critics: "a wretched subterfuge" (Kant)
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension | Range                                 | Central |
| --------- | ------------------------------------- | ------- |
| Free will | Leans _Self-authored agency_ – Middle | Yes     |
| Desert    | Leans _Earned_ – Middle               | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Freedom is not an exemption from cause and effect; it is acting from
your own reasons, values, and character, without coercion or compulsion. A person who
deliberates and acts on what they care about is free, and rightly held responsible,
whether or not the laws of physics are deterministic. Holding one another responsible is
a practice we cannot and should not give up, and it needs no metaphysical miracle.

**Key concepts.**

- **Freedom:** acting on desires and reasons you endorse, with the capacity to respond to
  reasons (Frankfurt's identification; Fischer and Ravizza's reasons-responsiveness),
  as opposed to being forced, manipulated, or compelled.
- **Responsibility:** grounded in the reactive attitudes (resentment, gratitude,
  indignation) that are part of human relationships (P. F. Strawson), not in uncaused
  choice.
- **Determinism:** no threat to freedom; the threat is constraint, not causation.
  Semi-compatibilists hold that responsibility survives determinism even if the ability
  to do otherwise does not.

**Signature reasons.**

- "Being caused isn't the same as being forced."
- "You're free when you act on your own reasons, not when your choice comes from
  nowhere."
- "We'd hold people responsible even if physics were deterministic, and we'd be right
  to."
- "The kind of free will worth wanting is the kind we actually have." (after Dennett)

**Telling it apart.**

- **Libertarian free will:** requires that choices be undetermined; compatibilism does
  not (_Free will_; Reasons).
- **Free-will skepticism:** can share the same premises about causation, but denies that
  anyone has the control deserved blame needs; compatibilism keeps desert and blame
  (_Desert_; Reasons).
- **Naturalism:** many naturalists are compatibilists, but compatibilism is a view
  about agency, not about whether a transcendent order exists; Augustine, Calvin, and
  Jonathan Edwards were theological compatibilists (_Transcendence_).

**Sources.** Thomas Hobbes, _Leviathan_ (1651) ch. 21; David Hume, _An Enquiry concerning
Human Understanding_ (1748) §8; P. F. Strawson, "Freedom and Resentment" (1962); Harry
Frankfurt, "Freedom of the Will and the Concept of a Person" (1971); Daniel Dennett,
_Elbow Room_ (1984); John Martin Fischer & Mark Ravizza, _Responsibility and Control_
(1998); Michael McKenna & D. Justin Coates, "Compatibilism", _Stanford Encyclopedia of
Philosophy_.

## Free-will skepticism

- **Type:** Foundation
- **Parent:** —
- **Also called:** hard incompatibilism (Pereboom); hard determinism (its older,
  determinist form); free-will denial
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension         | Range                                                | Central |
| ----------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- | ------- |
| Free will         | Leans _Caused behavior_ – Strongly _Caused behavior_ | Yes     |
| Desert            | Leans _Unearned_ – Strongly _Unearned_               | Yes     |
| Causes of fortune | Middle – Strongly _Luck and structure_               | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Whatever we choose flows in the end from factors we did not choose:
our genes, upbringing, brains, and circumstances, plus whatever chance adds. So no one
deserves, in the basic sense, to suffer for wrongdoing or to be rewarded for virtue.
Seeing this clearly does not make morality collapse; it makes us more humane, replacing
retribution with protection, rehabilitation, and understanding.

**Key concepts.**

- **Basic desert:** deserving blame or praise simply because of what one did. Free-will
  skeptics deny anyone has it, while keeping forward-looking reasons to praise, blame,
  and restrain.
- **Hard incompatibilism:** determinism rules out responsibility, and indeterminism
  would add only chance, which does not restore it, so either way basic desert is out
  (Pereboom).
- **Luck:** no one can be the ultimate cause of themselves, so luck goes all the way down
  (G. Strawson; Levy).
- **Quarantine:** dangerous people may be restrained to protect others, as with
  infectious disease, but with no more harm than safety requires (Pereboom, Caruso).

**Signature reasons.**

- "No one chose the brain or the childhood that made their choices."
- "Retribution only makes sense if people deserve to suffer, and no one does."
- "We can protect the public without pretending criminals made themselves."
- "Understanding why someone did something should soften our judgment, not harden it."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Compatibilism:** keeps responsibility and desert even if determinism is true;
  free-will skepticism denies basic desert (_Desert_; Reasons).
- **Libertarian free will:** agrees that determinism would rule out desert, but holds
  that our choices are undetermined (_Free will_).
- **Luck egalitarianism:** also treats much of fortune as luck, but keeps people
  responsible for their choices, which free-will skepticism denies at the root
  (_Desert_).

**Sources.** Baron d'Holbach, _The System of Nature_ (1770); Galen Strawson, "The
Impossibility of Moral Responsibility" (1994); Derk Pereboom, _Living Without Free Will_
(2001) and _Free Will, Agency, and Meaning in Life_ (2014); Neil Levy, _Hard Luck_
(2011); Gregg Caruso, _Rejecting Retributivism_ (2021); Daniel Dennett & Gregg Caruso,
_Just Deserts_ (2021).

## Meritocratic desert

- **Type:** Foundation
- **Parent:** —
- **Also called:** desert-based justice; meritocracy (as a moral view; Michael Young
  coined the word in satire, 1958); critics: "the tyranny of merit" (Sandel)
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension         | Range                                         | Central |
| ----------------- | --------------------------------------------- | ------- |
| Desert            | Strongly _Earned_ – Leans _Earned_            | Yes     |
| Causes of fortune | Strongly _Effort and choice_ – Middle         | No      |
| Free will         | Strongly _Self-authored agency_ – Middle      | No      |
| Work              | Strongly _Work is a source of worth_ – Middle | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** People who work hard, develop their abilities, and contribute more
deserve to keep more of what they earn, and people who do wrong deserve to bear the
consequences. Talent may be partly luck, but what you make of it is yours, and a society
that rewards contribution is both fairer and more productive than one that ignores it.
Denying desert insults people by treating their efforts and achievements as accidents.

**Key concepts.**

- **Desert:** a fitting relation between what a person has done and what they receive,
  which exists before any institution recognizes it (Feinberg); effort, contribution,
  and achievement are its bases.
- **Talent:** a gift, but one whose development and use are the person's own; you can
  deserve what you do with what you were given.
- **Fairness:** proportion, with rewards matched to contribution, as in Aristotle's
  distributive justice.

**Signature reasons.**

- "If you worked for it, you earned it."
- "Rewarding effort is fair, and it gives everyone a reason to try."
- "Calling talent luck erases everything people did with it."
- "People deserve credit for their successes and responsibility for their mistakes."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Luck egalitarianism:** holds that people deserve the results of their choices only,
  not of unchosen talent (_Desert_ toward _Unearned_; Reasons).
- **Natural hierarchy:** ranks people by nature; meritocratic desert rewards what people
  do against a background of equal worth (_Hierarchy or equality_).
- **Libertarian free will:** often used to support desert, but desert can be held
  without it, and many meritocrats are compatibilists (_Free will_).

**Sources.** Aristotle, _Nicomachean Ethics_ V; Joel Feinberg, "Justice and Personal
Desert" (1970); George Sher, _Desert_ (1987); David Miller, _Principles of Social
Justice_ (1999); Owen McLeod, "Desert", _Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy_.

## Luck egalitarianism

- **Type:** Foundation
- **Parent:** —
- **Also called:** responsibility-sensitive egalitarianism; equality of fortune
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension             | Range                                                    | Central |
| --------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------- | ------- |
| Desert                | Middle – Leans _Unearned_                                | Yes     |
| Causes of fortune     | Middle – Strongly _Luck and structure_                   | No      |
| Free will             | Leans _Self-authored agency_ – Middle                    | No      |
| Hierarchy or equality | Leans _Equality is basic_ – Strongly _Equality is basic_ | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** It is unfair for some people to be worse off than others through no
fault or choice of their own. Where you were born, the talents you were dealt, and the
accidents that befall you are brute luck, and justice should cancel out their effects;
what people do with fair shares is their own responsibility. That joins the egalitarian's
concern for the unlucky with respect for people's choices.

**Key concepts.**

- **Brute luck and option luck:** outcomes of risks you did not choose, and outcomes of
  gambles you deliberately took (Dworkin).
- **Equality of what:** resources (Dworkin), opportunity for welfare (Arneson), or access
  to advantage (Cohen).
- **Responsibility:** choices, ambitions, and effort can justify inequalities; endowments
  and circumstances cannot.

**Signature reasons.**

- "No one deserves to be worse off because of the family they were born into."
- "If you choose to work less, that's your call; if you were born ill, that's bad luck we
  should share."
- "Talent is a lottery prize, not an achievement."
- "Equality should be sensitive to ambition, not to endowment." (after Dworkin)

**Telling it apart.**

- **Meritocratic desert:** treats the rewards of talent as deserved; luck egalitarianism
  treats talent as luck (_Desert_).
- **Free-will skepticism:** goes further, treating choices as luck too (_Desert_, _Free
  will_).
- **Egalitarian liberalism:** Rawls rejects desert as a basis of distribution and
  focuses on the worst off; luck egalitarianism is the refinement many of his followers
  made to put choice back in (Reasons).

**Sources.** Ronald Dworkin, "What Is Equality? Part 2: Equality of Resources" (1981) and
_Sovereign Virtue_ (2000); Richard Arneson, "Equality and Equal Opportunity for Welfare"
(1989); G. A. Cohen, "On the Currency of Egalitarian Justice" (1989); Kasper
Lippert-Rasmussen, _Luck Egalitarianism_ (2015) and "Justice and Bad Luck", _Stanford
Encyclopedia of Philosophy_.

## Constrained vision

- **Type:** Foundation
- **Parent:** —
- **Also called:** the tragic vision (Sowell's later term); the Augustinian or Hobbesian
  view of human nature (as Reasons variants)
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension            | Range                                                  | Central |
| -------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ | ------- |
| Human nature         | Strongly _Fixed and flawed_ – Leans _Fixed and flawed_ | Yes     |
| Tradition or reason  | Strongly _Inherited practice carries wisdom_ – Middle  | No      |
| Direction of history | Middle – Strongly _Progress is not assured_            | No      |
| Given or made        | Strongly _Given by nature_ – Middle                    | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Human beings are limited in knowledge, virtue, and selflessness, and
these limits are permanent features of the species, not products of bad institutions.
Good institutions therefore work with human nature as it is: they channel self-interest,
check power, and rely on dispersed experience rather than on the wisdom of any planner.
Every arrangement involves trade-offs, not solutions, and the question is always what
is gained and what is lost.

**Key concepts.**

- **Human nature:** self-interested, partial, and limited in what any one mind can know,
  and much the same across history.
- **Trade-offs:** policy choices balance costs; there are no costless solutions
  (Sowell).
- **Institutions:** checks and balances, markets, traditions, and law that coordinate
  flawed people without requiring them to be good.
- **Grounds:** original sin (Augustinian) or fear and self-preservation (Hobbesian) are
  both covered, as Reasons.

**Signature reasons.**

- "If men were angels, no government would be necessary." (Madison)
- "There are no solutions, only trade-offs." (Sowell)
- "Power corrupts, so design for the worst person who might hold it."
- "Every scheme to perfect humanity has ended up crushing actual humans."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Unconstrained vision:** holds that reason and better institutions can substantially
  improve people (_Human nature_).
- **Christian realism:** its child: the same view of human limits, grounded in sin and
  joined to belief in a transcendent order (_Transcendence_; Reasons).
- **Burkean traditionalism:** often held with it, but a separate claim about inherited
  practice rather than human nature; a person can hold either alone (_Tradition or
  reason_).

**Sources.** Augustine, _The City of God_ (426); Thomas Hobbes, _Leviathan_ (1651); James
Madison, _Federalist_ No. 51 (1788); Edmund Burke, _Reflections on the Revolution in
France_ (1790); Thomas Sowell, _A Conflict of Visions_ (1987) and _The Vision of the
Anointed_ (1995); Steven Pinker, _The Blank Slate_ (2002) ch. 16.

## Unconstrained vision

- **Type:** Foundation
- **Parent:** —
- **Also called:** perfectibilism; human perfectibility; the utopian vision (Pinker's
  term); critics: "utopianism" (the "unconstrained vision" itself is Sowell's term)
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension            | Range                                                                  | Central |
| -------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------- |
| Human nature         | Leans _Malleable and improvable_ – Strongly _Malleable and improvable_ | Yes     |
| Direction of history | Strongly _History is progressing_ – Middle                             | No      |
| Given or made        | Middle – Strongly _Made by society_                                    | No      |
| Tradition or reason  | Middle – Strongly _Reason should audit inheritance_                    | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Much of human selfishness, ignorance, and cruelty is produced by bad
institutions, poverty, and miseducation, not fixed in our nature. Reason, education, and
better social arrangements can make people substantially wiser, kinder, and freer, as
history's moral progress already shows. Treating human limits as permanent is a counsel
of despair that has always served those who benefit from things as they are.

**Key concepts.**

- **Perfectibility:** humans can improve without any fixed limit (Godwin, Condorcet),
  which does not mean they will ever be perfect.
- **Human nature:** shaped by circumstances and institutions; vice is largely a social
  product.
- **Reason:** the faculty by which people and societies can understand and remake
  themselves.
- **Solutions:** social problems have causes that can be found and removed, not only
  trade-offs to be managed.

**Signature reasons.**

- "People aren't born selfish; bad systems teach them to be."
- "Every generation has proved the cynics wrong about what humans can become."
- "Change the conditions and you change the people."
- "Calling our flaws permanent is how the powerful excuse doing nothing."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Constrained vision:** holds that human limits are permanent and institutions must
  work around them (_Human nature_).
- **Progressive view of history:** holds that history is in fact progressing; the
  unconstrained vision is about what humans can become, and can coexist with thinking
  that progress has stalled (_Direction of history_).
- **Social constructionism:** explains human differences and categories by social
  arrangements; the unconstrained vision is about moral improvement, and can coexist
  with belief in inborn individual differences (_Given or made_).

**Sources.** Jean-Jacques Rousseau, _Discourse on Inequality_ (1755); William Godwin,
_Enquiry concerning Political Justice_ (1793); Condorcet, _Sketch for a Historical
Picture of the Progress of the Human Mind_ (1795); Robert Owen, _A New View of Society_
(1813); John Passmore, _The Perfectibility of Man_ (1970), a history of the idea. The
name is Thomas Sowell's, _A Conflict of Visions_ (1987).

## Christian realism

- **Type:** Foundation
- **Parent:** Constrained vision
- **Also called:** Niebuhrian realism
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension            | Range                                                                        | Central |
| -------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------- |
| Human nature         | Strongly _Fixed and flawed_ – Leans _Fixed and flawed_                       | Yes     |
| Transcendence        | Strongly _A transcendent order exists_ – Leans _A transcendent order exists_ | Yes     |
| Direction of history | Middle – Strongly _Progress is not assured_                                  | No      |
| Moral objectivity    | Strongly _Objective moral truth_ – Leans _Objective moral truth_             | No      |
| Outcomes or rules    | Leans _Best outcomes_ – Middle                                               | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Human beings are made in God's image but bent by sin, above all by
pride and the will to power, and sin is magnified in nations, classes, and groups. So
love cannot be the direct rule of politics: justice in a fallen world needs a balance of
power and checks on coercion, and the humility to act responsibly without claiming God's
righteousness for our side. Idealists who forget sin end up either naive or fanatical.

**Key concepts.**

- **Sin:** pride and self-interest rooted in the human condition, and worse in groups
  than in individuals ("moral man and immoral society").
- **Love and justice:** love is the ultimate norm; justice, an approximation of it
  secured through balanced power, is what politics can achieve.
- **Irony:** virtues turn into vices when pursued without humility, and nations that
  believe themselves innocent become dangerous.
- **Responsibility:** choosing the lesser evil and accepting the guilt of using power,
  rather than keeping one's hands clean.

**Signature reasons.**

- "Man's capacity for justice makes democracy possible; but man's inclination to
  injustice makes democracy necessary." (Niebuhr)
- "Groups are more selfish than the people in them."
- "In a fallen world, justice depends on balancing power, not on goodwill."
- "Beware anyone who thinks God is entirely on their side."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Constrained vision:** its parent; Christian realism adds a transcendent order and
  grounds human limits in sin (_Transcendence_; Reasons).
- **Realism:** the foreign-policy school shares the focus on power, but Christian
  realism is a view of human nature that judges power by a transcendent standard of
  love and justice (Reasons).
- **Divine command theory:** may share the theism, but it is a theory of moral
  obligation, not of the limits of human nature and politics (_Source of moral
  authority_; Reasons).

**Sources.** Reinhold Niebuhr, _Moral Man and Immoral Society_ (1932), _The Nature and
Destiny of Man_ (1941–43), _The Children of Light and the Children of Darkness_ (1944),
and _The Irony of American History_ (1952); Robin Lovin, _Reinhold Niebuhr and Christian
Realism_ (1995).

## Social constructionism

- **Type:** Foundation
- **Parent:** —
- **Also called:** social constructivism (in the social sciences); critics: "the blank
  slate" (Pinker)
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension          | Range                                                                                                                                    | Central |
| ------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------- |
| Given or made      | Leans _Made by society_ – Strongly _Made by society_                                                                                     | Yes     |
| Sex and gender     | Middle – Strongly _Gender is made by society_                                                                                            | No      |
| Race and ethnicity | Leans _Group differences are largely made by history and society_ – Strongly _Group differences are largely made by history and society_ | No      |
| Human nature       | Middle – Strongly _Malleable and improvable_                                                                                             | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Many of the categories and differences we treat as natural, such as
gender roles, sexual identities, what counts as intelligence or madness, and race, are
produced and kept in place by social arrangements, language, and institutions. Seeing
them as made rather than given does not make them unreal, but it shows they could be
otherwise. That frees people from treating historically contingent hierarchies as
nature's verdict.

**Key concepts.**

- **Construction:** a category is socially constructed when its existence or its
  boundaries depend on social practices, not on nature alone (Berger and Luckmann;
  Hacking).
- **Looping effects:** classifying people changes how they behave and understand
  themselves, which in turn changes the category (Hacking's "interactive kinds").
- **Naturalization:** presenting a contingent arrangement as natural and inevitable,
  which is what constructionist analysis exposes.

**Signature reasons.**

- "What looks natural is often just familiar."
- "Categories that were made can be unmade."
- "How we label people shapes who they become."
- "Biology sets limits, but society decides what counts as normal."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Psychological nativism:** holds that individual differences are substantially
  inborn (_Given or made_).
- **Racial constructivism:** the same view applied to race, on its own Dimension; a
  person can hold one without the other (_Race and ethnicity_).
- **Unconstrained vision:** about whether people can be morally improved, not about
  where differences come from (_Human nature_).

**Sources.** Peter Berger & Thomas Luckmann, _The Social Construction of Reality_ (1966);
Michel Foucault, _The History of Sexuality_, vol. 1 (1976); Ian Hacking, _The Social
Construction of What?_ (1999); Sally Haslanger, _Resisting Reality_ (2012); Ron Mallon,
"Naturalistic Approaches to Social Construction", _Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy_.

## Psychological nativism

- **Type:** Foundation
- **Parent:** —
- **Also called:** nativism (in psychology; unrelated to anti-immigrant nativism);
  behavioral-genetic realism; critics: "biological determinism", "genetic determinism"
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension      | Range                                                | Central |
| -------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- | ------- |
| Given or made  | Strongly _Given by nature_ – Leans _Given by nature_ | Yes     |
| Sex and gender | Strongly _Gender follows from sex_ – Middle          | No      |
| Human nature   | Strongly _Fixed and flawed_ – Middle                 | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Twin and adoption studies show that differences between individuals
in intelligence, personality, temperament, and even political attitudes are
substantially heritable, and the mind comes with a rich evolved structure rather than as
a blank slate. Taking this seriously is not fatalism: heritability says nothing about
whether outcomes can be changed. But policies that assume everyone would turn out the
same with the right upbringing will fail, and will blame the wrong people.

**Key concepts.**

- **Heritability:** the share of variation in a trait within a population explained by
  genetic differences; substantial for most measured psychological traits.
- **Human nature:** an evolved, universal architecture of the mind (Chomsky, Pinker),
  on which individual differences are variations.
- **Nature via nurture:** genes act through environments; nativism is not the claim that
  genes fix outcomes.

**Signature reasons.**

- "Anyone with more than one child knows they're born different."
- "All human behavioral traits are heritable." (Turkheimer's first law)
- "Heritable doesn't mean unchangeable."
- "Denying inborn differences means blaming parents and teachers for what they can't
  control."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Social constructionism:** holds that individual differences are mainly made by
  upbringing and society (_Given or made_).
- **Hereditarianism:** about average differences between racial groups, a separate
  Dimension; many nativists about individuals reject it (_Race and ethnicity_).
- **Constrained vision:** about the universal limits of human nature, not differences
  between individuals (_Human nature_).

**Sources.** Noam Chomsky, _Aspects of the Theory of Syntax_ (1965); Thomas Bouchard et
al., "Sources of Human Psychological Differences: The Minnesota Study of Twins Reared
Apart" (1990); Eric Turkheimer, "Three Laws of Behavior Genetics and What They Mean"
(2000); Steven Pinker, _The Blank Slate_ (2002); Robert Plomin, _Blueprint_ (2018);
Kathryn Paige Harden, _The Genetic Lottery_ (2021).

## Hereditarianism

- **Type:** Foundation
- **Parent:** —
- **Also called:** the hereditarian hypothesis; "race realism" (by some adherents);
  critics: "scientific racism"
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension          | Range                                                                                                | Central |
| ------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------- |
| Race and ethnicity | Strongly _Group differences are largely inherited_ – Leans _Group differences are largely inherited_ | Yes     |
| Given or made      | Strongly _Given by nature_ – Leans _Given by nature_                                                 | No      |
| Sex and gender     | Strongly _Gender follows from sex_ – Middle                                                          | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Human populations that evolved apart differ genetically, as is
plain for traits like skin color and disease risk, and there is no reason to assume the
brain is the one organ exempt. Average differences between racial groups in measured
traits such as IQ persist after accounting for environment, and the honest hypothesis
is that they are partly genetic. Saying so licenses no judgment of any individual by
their group, but blaming every gap on discrimination produces policies that cannot work
and accusations that are unjust.

**Key concepts.**

- **Race:** a real biological category, tracking clusters of genetic ancestry, not only
  a social construct.
- **Group differences:** differences in averages with wide overlap; claims are about
  populations, never about any individual.
- **The default hypothesis:** genes and environment contribute to differences between
  groups as they do to differences within them (Jensen).
- **Taboo:** adherents see opposition to the hypothesis as political rather than
  scientific, and its suppression as a harm to science.

**Signature reasons.**

- "Evolution didn't stop at the neck."
- "An average tells you nothing about the person in front of you."
- "If the gaps are partly genetic, blaming them all on racism is unfair to everyone."
- "Science shouldn't have forbidden hypotheses."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Racial constructivism:** holds that race is socially constructed and that group
  differences come from history and environment (_Race and ethnicity_).
- **Psychological nativism:** about differences between individuals, a separate
  Dimension; many nativists reject hereditarianism (_Given or made_).
- **Natural hierarchy:** ranks people's worth; hereditarians make a claim about traits,
  and many hold basic moral equality (_Hierarchy or equality_).
- **White nationalism:** a political Ideology that often cites hereditarianism;
  hereditarianism itself carries no political program, and its holders range from
  liberal individualists to separatists (Reasons).

**Sources.** Arthur Jensen, "How Much Can We Boost IQ and Scholastic Achievement?"
(1969) and _The g Factor_ (1998); Richard Herrnstein & Charles Murray, _The Bell Curve_
(1994); J. Philippe Rushton & Arthur Jensen, "Thirty Years of Research on Race
Differences in Cognitive Ability" (2005); Nicholas Wade, _A Troublesome Inheritance_
(2014); Charles Murray, _Human Diversity_ (2020); Michael James & Adam Burgos, "Race",
_Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy_.

## Racial constructivism

- **Type:** Foundation
- **Parent:** —
- **Also called:** social constructionism about race; racial eliminativism (one form,
  which holds race should not be used at all: Appiah, Zack)
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension          | Range                                                                                                                                    | Central |
| ------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------- |
| Race and ethnicity | Leans _Group differences are largely made by history and society_ – Strongly _Group differences are largely made by history and society_ | Yes     |
| Given or made      | Middle – Strongly _Made by society_                                                                                                      | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Racial categories were invented in particular histories, chiefly to
justify colonialism and slavery, and they do not divide humanity at biological joints:
most genetic variation lies within groups and shades gradually across geography. Race is
real as a social fact, with real effects on people's lives, but group differences in
outcomes are produced by history, environment, and discrimination. Treating them as
inborn mistakes the effects of racism for its justification.

**Key concepts.**

- **Race:** a social kind, real in its effects ("racial formation": Omi and Winant) but
  not a natural kind. Constructivists differ on whether to keep the concept for
  analysing racism (Haslanger, P. C. Taylor) or abandon it (Appiah, Zack).
- **Human genetic variation:** mostly within populations (Lewontin), and gradual across
  regions rather than sorted into discrete races.
- **Group differences:** produced by history, environment, and social arrangements.

**Signature reasons.**

- "There's more genetic variation within any race than between them."
- "Race is real the way money is real: because we made it so."
- "Who counted as White has changed many times; biology didn't."
- "Racial gaps track history and treatment, not genes."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Hereditarianism:** holds that race is biological and group differences partly
  genetic (_Race and ethnicity_).
- **Social constructionism:** the general view, applied to individual differences and
  other categories on their own Dimension (_Given or made_).
- **Critical race theory:** an Ideology that builds on racial constructivism and adds a
  theory of structural racism and a political program; the Foundation view carries no
  program (Reasons).

**Sources.** Franz Boas, _The Mind of Primitive Man_ (1911); UNESCO, _The Race Question_
(1950); Richard Lewontin, "The Apportionment of Human Diversity" (1972); Michael Omi &
Howard Winant, _Racial Formation in the United States_ (1986); K. Anthony Appiah & Amy
Gutmann, _Color Conscious_ (1996); Sally Haslanger, "Gender and Race: (What) Are They?
(What) Do We Want Them to Be?" (2000); Michael James & Adam Burgos, "Race", _Stanford
Encyclopedia of Philosophy_.

## Moral realism

- **Type:** Foundation
- **Parent:** —
- **Also called:** moral objectivism; naturalist and non-naturalist realism (its two
  main forms)
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension         | Range                                                            | Central |
| ----------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- | ------- |
| Moral objectivity | Strongly _Objective moral truth_ – Leans _Objective moral truth_ | Yes     |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Some things are really wrong (torturing a child for fun, for
instance), and they would be wrong even if everyone came to approve of them. Moral
claims can be true or false, and moral inquiry, like inquiry in mathematics or science,
can discover truths and correct errors. Without this, moral progress, such as the
abolition of slavery, would be mere change, and we would have nothing to say to those
who disagree except that we feel differently.

**Key concepts.**

- **Moral truth:** true regardless of what any person or culture believes or desires.
- **Moral facts:** either natural facts (naturalist or "Cornell" realism: Boyd, Brink)
  or irreducibly normative ones (non-naturalism: Moore, Parfit, Enoch).
- **Moral progress:** discovery of what was already true, not just a change of taste.

**Signature reasons.**

- "Slavery was wrong even when most people thought it was fine."
- "If morality were just opinion, there'd be no such thing as moral progress."
- "Some things are wrong, full stop."
- "We argue about morality as if there's an answer, because there is."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Moral constructivism:** holds that moral truths are constructed from a rational
  standpoint rather than existing independently (_Moral objectivity_ toward Middle;
  Reasons).
- **Moral anti-realism:** denies objective moral truth (_Moral objectivity_).
- **Natural law and Divine command theory:** its children, which also say what grounds
  moral truth (human nature and its goods; God's commands). Realists who work morality
  out by reason alone, such as Parfit or Singer, stay here (_Source of moral
  authority_).

**Sources.** Plato, _Republic_; G. E. Moore, _Principia Ethica_ (1903); David Brink, _Moral
Realism and the Foundations of Ethics_ (1989); Russ Shafer-Landau, _Moral Realism: A
Defence_ (2003); David Enoch, _Taking Morality Seriously_ (2011); Derek Parfit, _On What
Matters_ (2011); Geoff Sayre-McCord, "Moral Realism", _Stanford Encyclopedia of
Philosophy_.

## Moral constructivism

- **Type:** Foundation
- **Parent:** —
- **Also called:** Kantian constructivism (Rawls, Korsgaard) and Humean constructivism
  (Street), its two main forms
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                 | Range                                                       | Central |
| ------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- | ------- |
| Moral objectivity         | Leans _Objective moral truth_ – Leans _Human-made morality_ | Yes     |
| Source of moral authority | Leans _Worked out by us_ – Strongly _Worked out by us_      | Yes     |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Moral truths are neither strange facts out in the universe waiting
to be discovered nor mere feelings: they are the principles that rational agents would
arrive at by reasoning correctly from the proper standpoint. That makes morality
objective in the way that matters, binding on everyone who reasons, while explaining why
it has authority over us: it is the law our own reason gives.

**Key concepts.**

- **Construction:** a procedure (the original position, the categorical imperative,
  what no one could reasonably reject) whose outcome defines the moral truths, rather
  than tracking prior facts.
- **Autonomy:** moral authority is legislated by our own reason, not imposed from
  outside (Kant, Korsgaard).
- **Objectivity:** validity for every rational agent, rather than independence from all
  minds.
- **Kantian and Humean:** whether the standpoint is that of any rational agent
  (Korsgaard) or of a particular agent's own values (Street).

**Signature reasons.**

- "Morality is what we'd all agree to if we reasoned fairly."
- "Moral rules have authority because they're the rules reason gives itself."
- "You don't need mysterious moral facts to have right answers."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Moral realism:** holds that moral truths exist independently of any procedure
  (_Moral objectivity_).
- **Moral anti-realism:** denies that any procedure yields truths binding on all
  reasoners (_Moral objectivity_; Reasons).
- **Social contract theory:** a theory of which principles hold (those people would
  agree to); constructivism is a view of what moral truth is. Contractualists are often
  constructivists, but not always (Reasons).

**Sources.** Immanuel Kant, _Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals_ (1785); John Rawls,
"Kantian Constructivism in Moral Theory" (1980); Christine Korsgaard, _The Sources of
Normativity_ (1996); T. M. Scanlon, _What We Owe to Each Other_ (1998); Sharon Street,
"Constructivism about Reasons" (2008); Carla Bagnoli, "Constructivism in Metaethics",
_Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy_.

## Moral anti-realism

- **Type:** Foundation
- **Parent:** —
- **Also called:** moral subjectivism; expressivism (non-cognitivism), error theory, and
  moral relativism (its main forms); critics: "relativism", "nihilism"
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                 | Range                                                        | Central |
| ------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ | ------- |
| Moral objectivity         | Leans _Human-made morality_ – Strongly _Human-made morality_ | Yes     |
| Source of moral authority | Leans _Worked out by us_ – Strongly _Worked out by us_       | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** There is no moral truth built into the universe; morality is
something humans make, out of our feelings, needs, and agreements, or something our
cultures hand down. Seeing this clearly is not nihilism: we still care, argue, and
commit, but as the authors of our values rather than discoverers of cosmic facts. For
many, it also counsels humility about imposing one's own morality on others as if it
were written in the stars.

**Key concepts.**

- **Expressivism:** moral statements express attitudes of approval or disapproval
  rather than describe facts (Ayer, Stevenson, Blackburn, Gibbard).
- **Error theory:** moral statements aim at objective truth, but all of them fail,
  because there are no such facts (Mackie).
- **Relativism:** moral claims are true only relative to a culture's or a person's
  standards (Harman, Wong).
- **Queerness:** objective values would be strange things, unlike anything else we know,
  and known in no way we can explain (Mackie).

**Signature reasons.**

- "Morality is something we made, not something we found."
- "Different cultures have different morals, and there's no neutral referee."
- "When I say something is wrong, I'm telling you where I stand."
- "No list of facts adds up to an 'ought'."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Moral realism:** holds that some moral claims are true independent of anyone's
  attitudes (_Moral objectivity_).
- **Moral constructivism:** holds that correct reasoning yields moral truths binding on
  every rational agent (_Moral objectivity_; Reasons).
- **Existentialism:** also says we author our values, but as an ethic for how to live,
  not a theory of what moral claims mean (_Self-authorship or given role_; Reasons).

**Sources.** David Hume, _A Treatise of Human Nature_ (1739–40), book III; A. J. Ayer,
_Language, Truth and Logic_ (1936); Gilbert Harman, "Moral Relativism Defended" (1975);
J. L. Mackie, _Ethics: Inventing Right and Wrong_ (1977); Simon Blackburn, _Ruling
Passions_ (1998); David Wong, _Natural Moralities_ (2006); Richard Joyce, "Moral
Anti-Realism", _Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy_.

## Divine command theory

- **Type:** Foundation
- **Parent:** Moral realism
- **Also called:** theological voluntarism; modified divine command theory (Adams, its
  main contemporary form)
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                 | Range                                                            | Central |
| ------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- | ------- |
| Moral objectivity         | Strongly _Objective moral truth_ – Leans _Objective moral truth_ | Yes     |
| Source of moral authority | Strongly _Given from beyond us_                                  | Yes     |
| Transcendence             | Strongly _A transcendent order exists_                           | Yes     |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Moral obligation is real and binding because it is commanded by a
perfectly good and loving God, to whom we owe our existence. Without God, nothing could
make obligations absolute and universal rather than human preferences or conventions.
Because God's commands flow from God's good nature, they are not arbitrary; they
express the goodness on which all value depends.

**Key concepts.**

- **Obligation:** what God commands. In Adams's modified theory, goodness itself is
  grounded in God's nature, and obligation in God's commands.
- **The Euthyphro dilemma:** "is it good because God commands it, or commanded because
  it is good?" Answered by grounding goodness in God's nature, so commands are neither
  arbitrary nor answerable to a standard outside God.
- **Revelation:** scripture, tradition, and conscience as the ways God's commands are
  known.

**Signature reasons.**

- "If there's no God, nothing is really forbidden."
- "Only a perfectly good God could make a duty truly binding."
- "Right and wrong aren't up to us; they're up to the one who made us."
- "God's commands aren't arbitrary, because they come from his good nature."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Moral realism:** its parent; Divine command theory adds that moral obligation is
  grounded in God (_Source of moral authority_, _Transcendence_).
- **Natural law:** also usually theistic, but grounds morality in human nature knowable
  by reason; Divine command theory grounds obligation in God's will, known mainly
  through revelation (Reasons).
- **Theism:** many theists, including natural lawyers, reject Divine command theory
  (_Source of moral authority_; Reasons).

**Sources.** William of Ockham, _Quodlibetal Questions_; Søren Kierkegaard, _Fear and
Trembling_ (1843); Robert Adams, "A Modified Divine Command Theory of Ethical
Wrongness" (1973) and _Finite and Infinite Goods_ (1999); Philip Quinn, _Divine Commands
and Moral Requirements_ (1978); C. Stephen Evans, _God and Moral Obligation_ (2013); Mark
Murphy, "Theological Voluntarism", _Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy_.

## Natural law

- **Type:** Foundation
- **Parent:** Moral realism
- **Also called:** natural law theory; Thomistic natural law; new natural law theory
  (Grisez, Finnis, a contemporary school)
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                 | Range                                                                                | Central |
| ------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------- |
| Moral objectivity         | Strongly _Objective moral truth_                                                     | Yes     |
| Source of moral authority | Strongly _Given from beyond us_ – Leans _Given from beyond us_                       | Yes     |
| What makes a life go well | Leans _Some things are good for anyone_ – Strongly _Some things are good for anyone_ | Yes     |
| Outcomes or rules         | Leans _Inviolable rules_ – Strongly _Inviolable rules_                               | No      |
| Transcendence             | Strongly _A transcendent order exists_ – Middle                                      | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Human beings share a nature, and what fulfils it (life, knowledge,
friendship, family, play, beauty, religion, practical reasonableness) is good for anyone.
Reason can grasp these basic goods and the principles that follow from them, so the
moral law is written into our nature and knowable by anyone who reflects, whatever their
faith. Some acts, such as intentionally killing the innocent, are always wrong because
they directly attack a basic good.

**Key concepts.**

- **Basic goods:** self-evident aspects of human flourishing (Finnis's seven; Aquinas's
  natural inclinations).
- **Natural law:** "the rational creature's participation in the eternal law" (Aquinas),
  knowable by reason without revelation.
- **Moral absolutes:** never act intentionally against a basic good; the doctrine of
  double effect allows harm that is foreseen but not intended.
- **Unjust law:** a human law contrary to natural law is a corruption of law, not law.

**Signature reasons.**

- "Some things are good for every human being, because we share a nature."
- "You can know right from wrong by reason, without opening a holy book."
- "It's never right to intentionally kill an innocent person, whatever the payoff."
- "An unjust law is no law at all." (after Augustine and Aquinas)

**Telling it apart.**

- **Moral realism:** its parent; Natural law adds that moral truth is grounded in human
  nature and its goods (_Source of moral authority_, _What makes a life go well_).
- **Divine command theory:** grounds obligation in God's will; natural law holds that
  morality can be known by reason from human nature (Reasons).
- **Natural rights:** a modern offshoot centred on rights rather than goods and duties;
  one can hold natural rights without natural law's account of human purposes
  (_Rights: natural or man-made_; Reasons).
- **Objective-list theory:** shares the list of goods, but is a theory of well-being
  only, with no moral law or absolute prohibitions (_Outcomes or rules_).

**Sources.** Aristotle, _Nicomachean Ethics_; Thomas Aquinas, _Summa Theologiae_ I–II,
qq. 90–94; John Finnis, _Natural Law and Natural Rights_ (1980); Germain Grisez, Joseph
Boyle & John Finnis, "Practical Principles, Moral Truth, and Ultimate Ends" (1987);
Robert P. George, _In Defense of Natural Law_ (1999); Mark Murphy, "The Natural Law
Tradition in Ethics", _Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy_.

## Social contract theory

- **Type:** Foundation
- **Parent:** —
- **Also called:** contractarianism (Hobbes, Gauthier) and contractualism (Rawls,
  Scanlon), its two main forms
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                 | Range                                                          | Central |
| ------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- | ------- |
| Source of moral authority | Leans _Worked out by us_ – Strongly _Worked out by us_         | Yes     |
| Moral objectivity         | Leans _Objective moral truth_ – Strongly _Human-made morality_ | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** The principles that should govern us are those that free and equal
people would agree to, or could not reasonably reject. Morality and political authority
are not handed down; they are terms of cooperation that each person has reason to
accept, which is why they bind us, and why they must be justifiable to everyone they
bind. A rule no one could agree to has no claim on anyone.

**Key concepts.**

- **Agreement:** hypothetical, not historical: what rational people would agree to under
  fair conditions (the state of nature, the original position).
- **Contractarianism and contractualism:** agreement among self-interested bargainers
  (Hobbes, Gauthier), or principles that no one could reasonably reject (Rawls,
  Scanlon).
- **Justifiability:** a principle is valid only if it can be justified to each person it
  affects.

**Signature reasons.**

- "Rules are fair if everyone could agree to them."
- "Morality is the deal rational people make so they can live together."
- "No one is bound by terms they could reasonably reject."
- "Authority has to be justified to the people it rules."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Moral constructivism:** a view of what moral truth is; contract theory is a theory
  of which principles hold, and Hobbesian contractarians are often anti-realists
  instead (_Moral objectivity_; Reasons).
- **Natural rights:** Locke uses a contract, but the rights come before it; pure
  contract theorists hold that rights come from the agreement (_Rights: natural or
  man-made_).
- **Utilitarianism:** judges principles by total outcomes; contract theory by whether
  each person could accept them, so it rejects sacrificing one person for the greater
  sum (_Outcomes or rules_; Reasons).

**Sources.** Thomas Hobbes, _Leviathan_ (1651); John Locke, _Second Treatise of Government_
(1689); Jean-Jacques Rousseau, _The Social Contract_ (1762); John Rawls, _A Theory of
Justice_ (1971); David Gauthier, _Morals by Agreement_ (1986); T. M. Scanlon, _What We Owe
to Each Other_ (1998); Ann Cudd & Seena Eftekhari, "Contractarianism", and Elizabeth
Ashford & Tim Mulgan, "Contractualism", _Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy_.

## Utilitarianism

- **Type:** Foundation
- **Parent:** —
- **Also called:** consequentialism (its wider family); act and rule utilitarianism
  (its main forms)
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                | Range                                                                                                                    | Central |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------- |
| Outcomes or rules        | Strongly _Best outcomes_ – Leans _Best outcomes_                                                                         | Yes     |
| Moral circle             | Leans _Everyone counts equally_ – Strongly _Everyone counts equally_                                                     | Yes     |
| One master value or many | Strongly _Unified values_ – Leans _Unified values_                                                                       | No      |
| Sacred or tradeable      | Middle – Strongly _Everything can be weighed_                                                                            | No      |
| Grounds of moral status  | Leans _What matters is the life's quality and capacities_ – Strongly _What matters is the life's quality and capacities_ | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** What ultimately matters is the well-being of everyone affected, and
the right thing to do is whatever makes things go best for them, counting each person's
happiness and suffering equally. Rules, rights, and traditions matter because, and in so
far as, they promote well-being; when following one would cause needless misery, it
should give way. This is simply benevolence made consistent and impartial.

**Key concepts.**

- **Utility:** well-being, understood as pleasure and the absence of pain (Bentham),
  with higher and lower pleasures (Mill), or as the satisfaction of preferences (Hare,
  Singer).
- **Impartiality:** "everybody to count for one, nobody for more than one" (Bentham, as
  quoted by Mill).
- **Act and rule:** judging each act by its consequences, or following the rules whose
  general acceptance would be best (Hooker).
- **Sentience:** the capacity to suffer, not species, is what makes a being count.

**Signature reasons.**

- "The question is what actually makes people's lives better."
- "Everyone's suffering counts the same, strangers' and animals' included."
- "A rule that makes everyone worse off isn't worth keeping."
- "Doing less good than you could have is a choice too."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Deontology:** holds that some acts are forbidden whatever the outcome (_Outcomes or
  rules_).
- **Value pluralism:** holds that there are many values with no common measure; the
  utilitarian measures all by well-being (_One master value or many_).
- **Impartialism:** shares equal concern for everyone, but is a view of whose interests
  count, held by Kantians too, not a theory of right action (_Outcomes or rules_).
- **Sentientism:** shares moral status for all sentient beings, but says who counts,
  not what to do (Reasons).

**Sources.** Jeremy Bentham, _An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation_
(1789); John Stuart Mill, _Utilitarianism_ (1861); Henry Sidgwick, _The Methods of
Ethics_ (1874); Peter Singer, _Practical Ethics_ (1979); R. M. Hare, _Moral Thinking_
(1981); Brad Hooker, _Ideal Code, Real World_ (2000); Julia Driver, "The History of
Utilitarianism", _Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy_.

## Deontology

- **Type:** Foundation
- **Parent:** —
- **Also called:** deontological ethics; Kantian ethics (its best-known form);
  threshold deontology and side-constraint theory (forms)
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension           | Range                                                                          | Central |
| ------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------- |
| Outcomes or rules   | Leans _Inviolable rules_ – Strongly _Inviolable rules_                         | Yes     |
| Sacred or tradeable | Strongly _Some things are beyond price_ – Leans _Some things are beyond price_ | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Some things may not be done to a person even to produce better
results overall: you may not kill an innocent, torture, lie, or break faith simply
because the numbers favour it. Persons are ends in themselves, not resources for
maximising a total, and morality is made of duties, rights, and constraints that the
good of others cannot simply outweigh. A theory that permits anything for a big enough
payoff has misunderstood what morality is.

**Key concepts.**

- **Constraints:** limits on what you may do, not goals to promote ("side-constraints":
  Nozick).
- **Dignity:** persons have dignity, not a price; never treat humanity merely as a means
  (Kant).
- **Doing and allowing; intending and foreseeing:** killing is worse than letting die,
  and harm intended is worse than harm foreseen.
- **Thresholds and prima facie duties:** constraints that give way only to catastrophe
  (threshold deontology), or several basic duties weighed in context (Ross).

**Signature reasons.**

- "Some things you just don't do, whatever the payoff."
- "People aren't means to someone else's ends."
- "Killing someone isn't the same as failing to save them."
- "You can't harvest one patient's organs to save five."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Utilitarianism:** holds that the right act is the one with the best outcome
  (_Outcomes or rules_).
- **Natural law:** a deontological theory with a particular grounding in human nature
  and its goods; deontology names the structure, not the grounding (_Source of moral
  authority_; Reasons).
- **Virtue ethics:** also rejects maximising, but makes character, not rules, basic
  (Reasons).

**Sources.** Immanuel Kant, _Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals_ (1785); W. D. Ross,
_The Right and the Good_ (1930); Robert Nozick, _Anarchy, State, and Utopia_ (1974),
ch. 3; Frances Kamm, _Intricate Ethics_ (2007); Larry Alexander & Michael Moore,
"Deontological Ethics", _Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy_.

## Virtue ethics

- **Type:** Foundation
- **Parent:** —
- **Also called:** aretaic ethics; neo-Aristotelian ethics (its main contemporary form)
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                     | Range                                                                                | Central |
| ----------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------- |
| What makes a life go well     | Leans _Some things are good for anyone_ – Strongly _Some things are good for anyone_ | Yes     |
| Outcomes or rules             | Leans _Best outcomes_ – Leans _Inviolable rules_                                     | No      |
| Self-authorship or given role | Middle – Leans _Live up to a given role_                                             | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Ethics is first about what kind of person to be, not about which
rule to follow or which outcome to maximise. A good life is a flourishing life, and the
virtues (courage, justice, temperance, practical wisdom, honesty, compassion) are the
traits of character a human being needs to live well and act well. No rulebook or
calculation can replace the practical wisdom to see what a situation calls for.

**Key concepts.**

- **Eudaimonia:** flourishing, a life lived well by human standards, which the virtues
  partly make up.
- **Virtue:** a stable trait of character, reliably expressed in feeling and action, and
  learned by habit and example.
- **Practical wisdom (phronesis):** the ability to see what matters in a particular
  situation and act well in it.
- **Right action:** what a virtuous person would characteristically do in the
  circumstances (Hursthouse).

**Signature reasons.**

- "The question isn't what rule to follow but what kind of person to be."
- "Character is formed by practice, like any skill."
- "You can't write a rule for every situation; you need judgment."
- "Being good and living well go together."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Utilitarianism and Deontology:** take outcomes or rules as basic; virtue ethics
  takes character as basic (_Outcomes or rules_ toward Middle; Reasons).
- **Objective-list theory:** shares the objective goods, but is a theory of well-being,
  not of how to act (Reasons).
- **Natural law:** its Thomist cousin, which adds a moral law with absolute rules
  (_Outcomes or rules_, _Source of moral authority_).
- **Role ethics:** centres the good life on roles and relationships one did not choose,
  rather than on traits of individual character (_Self-authorship or given role_;
  Reasons).

**Sources.** Aristotle, _Nicomachean Ethics_; G. E. M. Anscombe, "Modern Moral
Philosophy" (1958); Alasdair MacIntyre, _After Virtue_ (1981); Rosalind Hursthouse, _On
Virtue Ethics_ (1999); Philippa Foot, _Natural Goodness_ (2001); Rosalind Hursthouse &
Glen Pettigrove, "Virtue Ethics", _Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy_.

## Value pluralism

- **Type:** Foundation
- **Parent:** —
- **Also called:** moral pluralism; agonistic liberalism (Gray's political form)
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                | Range                                                                      | Central |
| ------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------- |
| One master value or many | Leans _Plural, conflicting values_ – Strongly _Plural, conflicting values_ | Yes     |
| Moral objectivity        | Strongly _Objective moral truth_ – Middle                                  | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** There are many genuine values (liberty and equality, justice and
mercy, loyalty and honesty), and they cannot all be fully realised together or measured
on one scale. Real moral choices often mean losing something of real worth, and no master
formula can make the loss disappear. This is not relativism: the values are real, but
they conflict, and recognising that is the beginning of political wisdom and toleration.

**Key concepts.**

- **Incommensurability:** some values cannot be ranked on a common measure (Berlin,
  Raz).
- **Tragic conflict:** choosing one genuine good can mean the lasting loss of another.
- **Pluralism, not relativism:** the values are objective and recognisably human, and
  their number is limited.
- **Against monism:** the belief in a single final answer is a source of fanaticism
  (Berlin).

**Signature reasons.**

- "Freedom for the wolves has often meant death to the sheep." (Berlin)
- "You can't have everything good at once; something always gives."
- "There's no single scale on which to weigh justice against mercy."
- "Anyone with one master value will sacrifice everything else to it."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Utilitarianism:** a monism that measures every value by well-being (_One master
  value or many_).
- **Moral anti-realism:** pluralists hold the many values are objective, not attitudes
  or conventions (_Moral objectivity_).
- **Deontology:** Ross's plural prima facie duties are close to value pluralism, but
  deontology adds constraints that outweighing cannot cross (_Outcomes or rules_).

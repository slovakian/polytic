## Conservatism

- **Type:** Ideology
- **Parent:** —
- **Also called:** Burkean conservatism (for its founding form); Toryism (British)

- **Centered group:** —

| Direction of history         | Middle – Strongly _Progress is not assured_                                              | No      |

| Individuals or wholes        | Middle – Strongly _Groups and relations are real_                                        | No      |

| Religion and state           | Strongly _Public role for religion_ – Middle                                             | No      |
| Channels of change           | Strongly _Within institutions_ – Leans _Within institutions_                             | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Societies are not machines to be redesigned but living inheritances,
built up over generations, that hold more practical wisdom than any one generation or
theory can see. Human beings are limited in knowledge and virtue, so sweeping plans to
remake society tend to destroy what works and produce what no one intended. The task of
politics is to preserve and repair the institutions, such as family, religion, law, local
community, and nation, that make a decent and free life possible, changing them
carefully when they must change.

**Key concepts.**

- **Tradition:** accumulated, often unspoken knowledge about how to live together,
  tested by time; it deserves the benefit of the doubt.
- **Imperfection:** human nature and human reason are limited, so politics should
  restrain power and distrust grand designs.
- **Change:** necessary to preserve what matters ("a state without the means of some
  change is without the means of its conservation"), but gradual and responsive to
  real problems.
- **Order:** a precondition of freedom, sustained by authority, law, and shared norms.
- **Community:** people are formed by attachments they did not choose (family, place,
  faith, nation), and these deserve protection.

**Signature reasons.**

- "If it isn't broken, don't fix it, and be careful even when it is."
- "Society is a partnership between the dead, the living, and those yet to be born."
- "Grand plans to remake society usually end badly."
- "Freedom depends on order, family, and community."
- "It's easier to destroy an institution than to build one."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Liberalism:** judges institutions by whether they secure individual liberty, and
  gives inherited practice no authority of its own (_Tradition or reason_).
- **Libertarianism:** shares the distrust of state planning, but on grounds of individual
  rights rather than inherited wisdom, and rejects law upholding traditional morality
  (_Legal moralism_, _Tradition or reason_).
- **Fascism:** sometimes allied with conservatives, but is a revolutionary mass movement
  under a single leader and party, seeking national rebirth rather than preservation
  (_Tempo of change_, _Pluralism vs. unity_, _Leadership_).
- **Christian democracy:** shares the emphasis on family and community, but grounds it in
  Catholic social teaching and builds a generous welfare state (_Welfare and social
  insurance_; Reasons).
- **Communitarianism:** shares the attention to community, but is not tied to defending
  inherited institutions and can be reformist (_Tradition or reason_, _Tempo of
  change_).

**Sources.** Edmund Burke, _Reflections on the Revolution in France_ (1790); Russell Kirk,
_The Conservative Mind_ (1953); Michael Oakeshott, "On Being Conservative" (1956);
Samuel Huntington, "Conservatism as an Ideology" (1957); Anthony Quinton, _The Politics
of Imperfection_ (1978); Roger Scruton, _The Meaning of Conservatism_ (1980) and
_Conservatism: An Invitation to the Great Tradition_ (2017); Edmund Fawcett,
_Conservatism: The Fight for a Tradition_ (2020); Andy Hamilton, "Conservatism",
_Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy_.

## Traditionalist conservatism

- **Type:** Ideology
- **Parent:** Conservatism
- **Also called:** traditional conservatism; Burkean traditionalism
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                           | Range                                                                                                          | Central |
| ----------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------- |

| Tempo of change                     | Leans _Incremental reform_ – Strongly _Incremental reform_                                                     | Yes     |
| Design vs. evolved order            | Strongly _Evolved, spontaneous order_ – Leans _Evolved, spontaneous order_                                     | Yes     |
| Civil society vs. the state         | Strongly _Family, community, and voluntary association_ – Leans _Family, community, and voluntary association_ | No      |
| Local vs. central                   | Strongly _Local and regional self-rule_ – Leans _Local and regional self-rule_                                 | No      |
| Human nature                        | Strongly _Fixed and flawed_ – Leans _Fixed and flawed_                                                         | No      |
| Individuals or wholes               | Leans _Groups and relations are real_ – Strongly _Groups and relations are real_                               | No      |
| Moral circle                        | Strongly _Near and dear first_ – Leans _Near and dear first_                                                   | No      |
| Self-authorship or given role       | Middle – Strongly _Live up to a given role_                                                                    | No      |
| Transcendence                       | Strongly _A transcendent order exists_ – Middle                                                                | No      |
| Religion and state                  | Strongly _Public role for religion_ – Middle                                                                   | No      |
| Direction of history                | Leans _Progress is not assured_ – Strongly _Progress is not assured_                                           | No      |
| Social hierarchy                    | Middle – Leans _Ordered rank and deference_                                                                    | No      |
| Sex roles in family and public life | Strongly _Distinct roles for the sexes_ – Middle                                                               | No      |
| Future generations                  | Middle – Leans _Future people's claims_                                                                        | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** A society is an inherited partnership across generations, held
together less by law and contract than by custom, religion, family, and loyalty to
place. These "little platoons" form our characters and give our lives meaning, and they
are far easier to destroy than to rebuild. Politics should protect them from both the
centralizing state and the restless market, prefer what is familiar and tried to what
is new and theoretical, and hand on to our children a world at least as rich in
inherited goods as the one we received.

**Key concepts.**

- **Custom:** the slow, unplanned deposit of a people's experience, carrying knowledge
  that no one can fully state.
- **Little platoons:** family, parish, neighborhood, and local association, the
  communities where loyalty and virtue are learned.
- **Prejudice:** in Burke's sense, a settled, pre-reflective attachment to inherited
  ways, which is often wiser than abstract reasoning.
- **Skepticism:** for its Oakeshottian strand, conservatism is less a creed than a
  disposition: a preference for the present and familiar, and a distrust of rationalist
  politics.
- **Stewardship:** we hold inherited goods in trust for those who come after us.

**Signature reasons.**

- "We are guardians of what we inherited, not owners who can do as we like."
- "Communities, churches, and families do what no government program can."
- "To be conservative is to prefer the familiar to the unknown."
- "Old customs often hold reasons we've forgotten."
- "Neither the state nor the market should be allowed to dissolve the ties that bind us."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Liberal conservatism:** conserves liberal institutions and embraces markets;
  traditionalists are warier of capitalism's effect on community (_Economic
  coordination_; Reasons).
- **Paleoconservatism:** a US-centered, more populist form that centers immigration,
  trade, and non-intervention (_Immigration and borders_, _Trade and economic
  openness_).
- **Counter-revolutionary traditionalism:** seeks to restore a pre-revolutionary order of
  throne and altar, not to conserve the present one (_Tempo of change_, _Pluralism vs.
  unity_, _Social hierarchy_).
- **Social conservatism:** an Area focused on law that upholds traditional morality;
  traditionalists often trust custom over legislation (_Legal moralism_; Reasons).
- **Post-liberalism:** holds that liberalism itself is the problem and seeks a new
  common-good order rather than conserving the present one (_Neutrality vs.
  perfectionism_, _Tempo of change_).

**Sources.** Edmund Burke, _Reflections on the Revolution in France_ (1790); Samuel
Taylor Coleridge, _On the Constitution of the Church and State_ (1830); T. S. Eliot, _The
Idea of a Christian Society_ (1939); Russell Kirk, _The Conservative Mind_ (1953); Robert
Nisbet, _The Quest for Community_ (1953); Michael Oakeshott, _Rationalism in Politics_
(1962); Roger Scruton, _The Meaning of Conservatism_ (1980).

## One-nation conservatism

- **Type:** Ideology
- **Parent:** Conservatism
- **Also called:** one-nation Toryism; Tory democracy (historical); paternalist
  conservatism; Red Toryism (its Canadian and later British communitarian form: Grant,
  Blond)
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                                   | Range                                                                                      | Central |
| ------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------- |
| Welfare and social insurance                | Middle – Leans _Public social insurance_                                                   | Yes     |
| Economic coordination                       | Leans _Public direction_ – Leans _Market coordination_                                     | Yes     |
| Tradition or reason                         | Strongly _Inherited practice carries wisdom_ – Leans _Inherited practice carries wisdom_   | No      |
| Tempo of change                             | Leans _Incremental reform_ – Strongly _Incremental reform_                                 | No      |
| Distribution                                | Leans _Market entitlement_ – Leans _Egalitarian redistribution_                            | No      |
| Compatriot priority vs. global impartiality | Strongly _Special obligations to compatriots_ – Leans _Special obligations to compatriots_ | No      |
| Social hierarchy                            | Middle – Leans _Ordered rank and deference_                                                | No      |
| Civil society vs. the state                 | Leans _Family, community, and voluntary association_ – Middle                              | No      |
| Basis of nationhood                         | Middle – Leans _Shared heritage and culture_                                               | No      |
| Religion and state                          | Leans _Public role for religion_ – Middle                                                  | No      |
| Pluralism vs. unity                         | Leans _Legitimate pluralism and opposition_ – Middle                                       | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** A nation is one community, not two nations of rich and poor
living side by side as strangers. Those with wealth, power, and position owe duties to
those without, and a conservative government should use social reform, decent public
services, and a mixed economy to bind the classes together and keep the nation whole.
Reform carried out by those who love the country's institutions is the best protection
against revolution carried out by those who hate them.

**Key concepts.**

- **The nation:** a shared home whose unity matters more than any class's interest.
- **Duty:** privilege carries obligations to the less fortunate (noblesse oblige).
- **Social reform:** a conservative tool for preserving the social fabric, not a step
  toward socialism.
- **The middle way:** a mixed economy between laissez-faire and state socialism.

**Signature reasons.**

- "We are one nation, not two."
- "With privilege comes responsibility."
- "Reform to preserve; don't wait for revolution."
- "Markets serve the nation; the nation doesn't serve markets."
- "Decent public services are part of what holds a country together."

**Telling it apart.**

- **New Right:** rolls back the welfare state and the mixed economy that one-nation
  conservatives defend (_Welfare and social insurance_, _Economic coordination_).
- **Liberal conservatism:** puts markets first and justifies them on liberal grounds
  (_Economic coordination_, _Welfare and social insurance_).
- **Christian democracy:** builds social protection through Catholic social teaching,
  subsidiarity, and European integration (_Civil society vs. the state_, _Sovereignty vs.
  pooled governance_; Reasons).
- **Blue Labour:** a similar politics of place, work, and reciprocity from the labour
  movement, with more weight on worker voice (_Workplace governance_; Reasons).
- **Social democracy:** seeks equality through class solidarity, not national unity
  under a social hierarchy (_Distribution_, _Social hierarchy_).

**Sources.** Benjamin Disraeli, _Sybil, or The Two Nations_ (1845) and Crystal Palace
speech (1872); Harold Macmillan, _The Middle Way_ (1938); Quintin Hogg, _The Case for
Conservatism_ (1947); George Grant, _Lament for a Nation_ (1965); Ian Gilmour, _Inside
Right_ (1977); Phillip Blond, _Red Tory_ (2010).

## Liberal conservatism

- **Type:** Ideology
- **Parent:** Conservatism
- **Also called:** conservative liberalism (a near-synonym in some countries);
  centre-right
- **Variants:** Fusionism
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                                         | Range                                                                                            | Central |
| ------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------- |
| Economic coordination                             | Leans _Market coordination_ – Strongly _Market coordination_                                     | Yes     |
| Tradition or reason                               | Strongly _Inherited practice carries wisdom_ – Leans _Inherited practice carries wisdom_         | Yes     |
| Pluralism vs. unity                               | Strongly _Legitimate pluralism and opposition_ – Leans _Legitimate pluralism and opposition_     | Yes     |
| Tempo of change                                   | Leans _Incremental reform_ – Strongly _Incremental reform_                                       | No      |
| Property in capital and goods                     | Strongly _Robust private ownership_ – Leans _Robust private ownership_                           | No      |
| Distribution                                      | Strongly _Market entitlement_ – Middle                                                           | No      |
| Welfare and social insurance                      | Leans _Self-reliance and voluntary provision_ – Middle                                           | No      |
| Trade and economic openness                       | Strongly _Open exchange_ – Leans _Open exchange_                                                 | No      |
| Legal moralism                                    | Leans _Liberty of self-regarding conduct_ – Middle                                               | No      |
| Religion and state                                | Leans _Public role for religion_ – Middle                                                        | No      |
| Popular sovereignty vs. constitutional constraint | Strongly _Entrenched rights and judicial review_ – Leans _Entrenched rights and judicial review_ | No      |
| Rule of law vs. discretion                        | Leans _Rule by general, fixed rules_ – Strongly _Rule by general, fixed rules_                   | No      |
| Leadership                                        | Leans _Dispersed, checked authority_ – Strongly _Dispersed, checked authority_                   | No      |
| Security and civil liberties                      | Middle – Leans _Collective security and order_                                                   | No      |
| Use of force                                      | Leans _Readiness to use force_ – Middle                                                          | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Free markets, private property, constitutional government, and
individual rights are among the great achievements of our civilization, and they are
worth conserving precisely because they are inherited institutions that have proven
themselves. They rest on moral and cultural foundations, such as family, religion,
personal responsibility, and patriotism, that liberalism alone cannot supply. A wise
politics defends economic and political freedom and the traditions that make free
people capable of using it well.

**Key concepts.**

- **Ordered liberty:** freedom within a framework of law, custom, and moral restraint.
- **Markets:** an inherited, proven institution that rewards responsibility and
  generates prosperity.
- **Virtue:** indispensable to a free society, but best cultivated by families, churches,
  and communities, not imposed by the state; for its Fusionist variant, virtue coerced
  is no virtue.
- **Prudence:** reform where needed, without ideological zeal in either direction.

**Signature reasons.**

- "Free markets and strong families go together."
- "Our freedoms were inherited; we have a duty to pass them on."
- "Liberty needs virtue, and virtue needs liberty."
- "Lower taxes and limited government, but respect for faith and tradition."
- "Change carefully, and keep what works."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Classical liberalism:** defends markets and rights as universal principles rather
  than as an inheritance, with less concern for their cultural foundations (_Tradition
  or reason_; Reasons).
- **Traditionalist conservatism:** warier of markets and their effect on community
  (_Economic coordination_; Reasons).
- **New Right:** more radical in reform and more emphatic about state authority on order
  and nation (_Tempo of change_, _Security and civil liberties_).
- **Christian democracy:** builds a larger welfare state through Catholic social teaching
  and subsidiarity (_Welfare and social insurance_; Reasons).
- **Paleolibertarianism:** pairs markets with tradition, but rejects the state far more
  thoroughly and opposes foreign intervention (_Political authority_, _Use of force_).

**Sources.** Edmund Burke, _Thoughts and Details on Scarcity_ (1795); Alexis de
Tocqueville, _Democracy in America_ (1835–40); Frank Meyer, _In Defense of Freedom_
(1962); George H. Nash, _The Conservative Intellectual Movement in America Since 1945_
(1976); Roger Scruton, _How to Be a Conservative_ (2014); Edmund Fawcett,
_Conservatism: The Fight for a Tradition_ (2020).

## New Right

- **Type:** Ideology
- **Parent:** Conservatism
- **Also called:** Thatcherism and Reaganism (national forms); critics: "neoliberal
  conservatism"
- **Variants:** Supply-side economics
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                           | Range                                                                                            | Central |
| ----------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------- |
| Economic coordination               | Leans _Market coordination_ – Strongly _Market coordination_                                     | Yes     |
| Welfare and social insurance        | Strongly _Self-reliance and voluntary provision_ – Leans _Self-reliance and voluntary provision_ | Yes     |
| Security and civil liberties        | Leans _Collective security and order_ – Strongly _Collective security and order_                 | Yes     |
| Distribution                        | Strongly _Market entitlement_ – Leans _Market entitlement_                                       | No      |
| Property in capital and goods       | Strongly _Robust private ownership_                                                              | No      |
| Workplace governance                | Leans _Owner and managerial authority_ – Strongly _Owner and managerial authority_               | No      |
| Reciprocity in support              | Leans _Conditional (reciprocal) support_ – Strongly _Conditional (reciprocal) support_           | No      |
| Money and public finance            | Leans _Rules and fiscal discipline_ – Strongly _Rules and fiscal discipline_                     | No      |
| Punishment                          | Strongly _Desert and deterrence_ – Leans _Desert and deterrence_                                 | No      |
| Basis of nationhood                 | Middle – Leans _Shared heritage and culture_                                                     | No      |
| Sex roles in family and public life | Leans _Distinct roles for the sexes_ – Middle                                                    | No      |
| Use of force                        | Strongly _Readiness to use force_ – Leans _Readiness to use force_                               | No      |
| Sovereignty vs. pooled governance   | Middle – Strongly _Sovereign self-government_                                                    | No      |
| Tempo of change                     | Leans _Fundamental transformation_ – Leans _Incremental reform_                                  | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** By the 1970s, big government, high taxes, nationalized industry,
and union power had produced stagnation, inflation, and a culture of dependency, while
crime and moral permissiveness eroded the social order. The answer is a free economy
and a strong state: cut taxes, deregulate, privatize, and curb union power so
enterprise can flourish, while the state concentrates firmly on its proper tasks of law
and order, national defense, and upholding the family and the nation.

**Key concepts.**

- **Enterprise:** individual initiative, rewarded by low taxes and free markets, is the
  engine of prosperity and character.
- **Strong state:** limited in scope but firm in authority over law, order, and defense.
- **Dependency:** an overgrown welfare state saps responsibility and family.
- **Nation and family:** the moral foundations a free economy depends on.

**Signature reasons.**

- "Government isn't the solution; government is the problem."
- "Free markets, strong defense, and family values."
- "Lower taxes let people keep what they earn and grow the economy."
- "Law and order is the first duty of government."
- "There is no such thing as public money, only taxpayers' money."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Liberal conservatism:** more moderate in pace and less emphatic about state authority
  (_Tempo of change_, _Security and civil liberties_).
- **One-nation conservatism:** defends the welfare state and mixed economy the New Right
  rolled back (_Welfare and social insurance_, _Economic coordination_).
- **Market liberalism:** shares the economics but not the nation, family, and order, and
  is friendlier to supranational rules (_Basis of nationhood_, _Sovereignty vs. pooled
  governance_).
- **Neoconservatism:** more accepting of a welfare state and centered on democracy
  promotion abroad (_Welfare and social insurance_, _Foreign-policy aims_).
- **National conservatism:** more open to tariffs and industrial policy (_Trade and
  economic openness_, _Economic coordination_).

**Sources.** F. A. Hayek, _The Road to Serfdom_ (1944); Keith Joseph, _Stranded on the
Middle Ground?_ (1976); Milton & Rose Friedman, _Free to Choose_ (1980); Ronald Reagan,
First Inaugural Address (1981); Desmond King, _The New Right: Politics, Markets and
Citizenship_ (1987); Andrew Gamble, _The Free Economy and the Strong State_ (1988);
Margaret Thatcher, _The Downing Street Years_ (1993).

- **Type:** Ideology
- **Parent:** Conservatism

| Basis of nationhood               | Strongly _Shared civic principles_ – Leans _Shared civic principles_                         | No      |
| Group difference                  | Leans _Difference-blind common rules_ – Strongly _Difference-blind common rules_             | No      |
| Moral objectivity                 | Strongly _Objective moral truth_ – Leans _Objective moral truth_                             | No      |
| Religion and state                | Leans _Public role for religion_ – Middle                                                    | No      |
| Legal moralism                    | Middle – Leans _Enforcement of shared morality_                                              | No      |
| Design vs. evolved order          | Leans _Evolved, spontaneous order_ – Middle                                                  | No      |
| Economic coordination             | Leans _Market coordination_                                                                  | No      |
| Welfare and social insurance      | Leans _Self-reliance and voluntary provision_ – Leans _Public social insurance_              | No      |
| Trade and economic openness       | Strongly _Open exchange_ – Leans _Open exchange_                                             | No      |
| Pluralism vs. unity               | Strongly _Legitimate pluralism and opposition_ – Leans _Legitimate pluralism and opposition_ | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Liberal democracy is worth defending, and it faces two threats:
at home, a culture of relativism and ambitious social engineering that weakens the
moral and civic habits democracy depends on; abroad, tyrannies that will exploit any
weakness. Social policy should respect the law of unintended consequences, and culture
needs the support of religion, family, and civic pride. Abroad, the free world should
use its power, military included, to confront tyranny and support democracy, because
moral clarity and strength keep the peace better than accommodation.

**Key concepts.**

- **Moral clarity:** some regimes are evil, and saying so matters; relativism is a
  cultural and strategic weakness.
- **Peace through strength:** credible military power deters aggressors and makes
  diplomacy work.
- **Democracy promotion:** spreading liberal democracy serves both justice and security.
- **Unintended consequences:** ambitious social programs often backfire, so domestic
  reform should be modest and evidence-tested.
- **Civic culture:** democracy depends on virtues fostered by religion, family, and
  patriotism.

**Signature reasons.**

- "A neoconservative is a liberal who has been mugged by reality." (Irving Kristol)
- "Weakness invites aggression."
- "America should stand for freedom in the world, not just its own interests."
- "Good intentions in social policy often make things worse."
- "Some regimes are evil, and we should say so."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Liberal internationalism:** also promotes democracy, but through international
  institutions and multilateral consent rather than national power (_Sovereignty vs.
  pooled governance_, _Use of force_).
- **Realism:** pursues national interest and balance of power, not values abroad
  (_Foreign-policy aims_, the central difference).
- **Paleoconservatism:** opposes foreign intervention and defines the nation by heritage
  rather than a civic creed (_Use of force_, _Basis of nationhood_).
- **New Right:** more radical about rolling back the welfare state (_Welfare and social
  insurance_).
- **Restraint:** avoids foreign wars and entangling alliances (_Use of force_).

**Sources.** Nathan Glazer, _Affirmative Discrimination_ (1975); Daniel Bell, _The
Cultural Contradictions of Capitalism_ (1976); Jeane Kirkpatrick, "Dictatorships and
Double Standards" (1979); Irving Kristol, _Neoconservatism: The Autobiography of an
Idea_ (1995); William Kristol & Robert Kagan, "Toward a Neo-Reaganite Foreign Policy"
(1996); Project for the New American Century, "Statement of Principles" (1997); Justin
Vaïsse, _Neoconservatism: The Biography of a Movement_ (2010).

## Paleoconservatism

- **Type:** Ideology
- **Parent:** Conservatism
- **Also called:** "paleo"; America First conservatism (its foreign-policy slogan)

- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Basis of nationhood                         | Leans _Shared heritage and culture_ – Strongly _Shared heritage and culture_                     | Yes     |
| Tradition or reason                         | Strongly _Inherited practice carries wisdom_                                                     | No      |
| Local vs. central                           | Strongly _Local and regional self-rule_ – Leans _Local and regional self-rule_                   | No      |
| Sovereignty vs. pooled governance           | Leans _Sovereign self-government_ – Strongly _Sovereign self-government_                         | No      |
| Foreign-policy aims                         | Leans _National interest_ – Strongly _National interest_                                         | No      |
| Compatriot priority vs. global impartiality | Strongly _Special obligations to compatriots_                                                    | No      |
| Moral circle                                | Strongly _Near and dear first_ – Leans _Near and dear first_                                     | No      |
| Religion and state                          | Strongly _Public role for religion_ – Leans _Public role for religion_                           | No      |
| Sex roles in family and public life         | Strongly _Distinct roles for the sexes_ – Leans _Distinct roles for the sexes_                   | No      |
| Expert trust                                | Leans _Judge for yourself_ – Strongly _Judge for yourself_                                       | No      |
| Economic scale and concentration            | Middle – Leans _Dispersed ownership and small scale_                                             | No      |
| Direction of history                        | Leans _Progress is not assured_ – Strongly _Progress is not assured_                             | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** A nation is a particular people with a particular inheritance:
its faith, customs, language, and the communities its ancestors built, not an idea or a
marketplace open to all comers. Mass immigration, free-trade deals that hollow out
industry, and wars to spread democracy abroad all sacrifice that inheritance to the
interests of a managerial elite. Conservatives should defend the real, local, historic
nation: secure its borders, protect its workers, keep out of foreign wars, and return
power from distant capitals to states and communities.

**Key concepts.**

- **The nation:** a concrete people with a shared history and culture, not a creed
  anyone can join by assent.
- **The managerial elite:** a class of bureaucrats, corporate leaders, and media that
  rules in its own interest and against the inherited culture.
- **Non-intervention:** a republic, not an empire; no wars for abstract ideals.
- **Localism:** real loyalty and self-government live in families, towns, and regions.
- **Economic nationalism:** trade should serve the nation's workers and productive base.

**Signature reasons.**

- "A nation is a people with a history, not just an idea."
- "We should be a republic, not an empire."
- "Free trade shipped our jobs overseas."
- "Mass immigration is changing the country without our consent."
- "Washington and Wall Street don't speak for the heartland."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Neoconservatism:** favors intervention abroad and defines the nation by a civic creed
  (_Use of force_, _Basis of nationhood_).
- **National conservatism:** close in outlook, but an international movement that
  accepts more central state power and industrial policy, and is less localist and less
  consistently anti-interventionist (_Local vs. central_, _Use of force_; Reasons).
- **Paleolibertarianism:** shares the cultural outlook, but rejects tariffs and most of
  the state (_Trade and economic openness_, _Welfare and social insurance_).
- **National populism:** shares the anti-elite and immigration stance, but without the
  traditionalist canon (_Tradition or reason_; Reasons).
- **White nationalism:** centers a race spanning nations rather than an inherited national
  culture (centered group; _Race and ethnicity_).

**Sources.** Paul Gottfried & Thomas Fleming, _The Conservative Movement_ (1988); M. E.
Bradford, _The Reactionary Imperative_ (1990); Samuel Francis, _Beautiful Losers_ (1993);
Patrick J. Buchanan, _A Republic, Not an Empire_ (1999); Joseph Scotchie (ed.), _The
Paleoconservatives_ (1999); George Hawley, _Right-Wing Critics of American Conservatism_
(2016).

## Social conservatism

- **Type:** Ideology (usually Area)
- **Parent:** Conservatism
- **Also called:** moral conservatism; traditional-values conservatism; the religious
  right (its US movement form)
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                           | Range                                                                                                          | Central |
| ----------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------- |
| Legal moralism                      | Leans _Enforcement of shared morality_ – Strongly _Enforcement of shared morality_                             | Yes     |
| Life and body                       | Leans _Protection of human life and dignity_ – Strongly _Protection of human life and dignity_                 | Yes     |
| Sex roles in family and public life | Strongly _Distinct roles for the sexes_ – Leans _Distinct roles for the sexes_                                 | Yes     |
| Religion and state                  | Strongly _Public role for religion_ – Leans _Public role for religion_                                         | No      |
| Neutrality vs. perfectionism        | Leans _State perfectionism_ – Strongly _State perfectionism_                                                   | No      |
| Sex and gender identity             | Strongly _Law follows sex_ – Leans _Law follows sex_                                                           | No      |
| Paternalism                         | Leans _Protection_ – Middle                                                                                    | No      |
| Civil society vs. the state         | Strongly _Family, community, and voluntary association_ – Leans _Family, community, and voluntary association_ | No      |
| Grounds of moral status             | Strongly _Life itself is inviolable_ – Leans _Life itself is inviolable_                                       | No      |
| Source of moral authority           | Strongly _Given from beyond us_ – Leans _Given from beyond us_                                                 | No      |
| Transcendence                       | Strongly _A transcendent order exists_ – Middle                                                                | No      |
| Sex and gender                      | Strongly _Gender follows from sex_ – Leans _Gender follows from sex_                                           | No      |
| Self-authorship or given role       | Leans _Live up to a given role_ – Strongly _Live up to a given role_                                           | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Some moral truths about human life, marriage, family, and the
sexes are not matters of preference, and a healthy society reflects them in its laws
and culture. Law always teaches something; a law that treats every way of life as
equally good teaches that nothing is sacred. So the state should protect unborn and
vulnerable life, uphold marriage and the family as the foundation of society, respect
the public place of faith, and refuse to promote what corrupts, while families and
churches do the deeper work of forming character.

**Key concepts.**

- **Sanctity of life:** human life has inviolable worth from conception to natural death.
- **The family:** built on the marriage of a man and a woman, the first school of virtue
  and the foundation of society.
- **Law as teacher:** laws shape what people think is right, so they cannot be morally
  neutral.
- **Religious liberty:** faith has a rightful place in public life, and believers must be
  free to live by it.
- **Natural law:** moral truths about human flourishing that reason can know and law
  should respect.

**Signature reasons.**

- "Every human life is sacred, born and unborn."
- "Marriage is between a man and a woman, and children deserve a mother and a father."
- "The law teaches, whether we want it to or not."
- "Faith belongs in the public square."
- "Strong families are the foundation of a strong society."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Traditionalist conservatism:** a whole social philosophy, often trusting custom over
  legislation; social conservatism focuses on law upholding traditional morality (_Legal
  moralism_; Reasons).
- **Liberal conservatism:** shares many values but leaves virtue to families and churches,
  not law (_Legal moralism_).
- **Catholic integralism:** wants the state formally ordered to the Church's spiritual
  end (_Religion and state_ at the far end; Reasons).
- **Christian nationalism:** defines the nation itself as Christian (_Basis of
  nationhood_; Reasons).
- **Perfectionist liberalism:** also lets the state promote the good, but the good of
  autonomy, never by enforcing morality (_Legal moralism_, _Self-authorship or given
  role_).

**Sources.** Patrick Devlin, _The Enforcement of Morals_ (1965); John Finnis, _Natural
Law and Natural Rights_ (1980); James Davison Hunter, _Culture Wars_ (1991); Robert P.
George, _Making Men Moral_ (1993); Manhattan Declaration (2009); Sherif Girgis, Ryan T.
Anderson & Robert P. George, _What Is Marriage?_ (2012).

## Fiscal conservatism

- **Type:** Ideology (usually Area)
- **Parent:** Conservatism
- **Also called:** fiscal responsibility; "budget hawks" (colloquial); critics: "austerity"
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                    | Range                                                                        | Central |
| ---------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------- |
| Money and public finance     | Leans _Rules and fiscal discipline_ – Strongly _Rules and fiscal discipline_ | Yes     |
| Future generations           | Middle – Leans _Future people's claims_                                      | No      |
| Welfare and social insurance | Strongly _Self-reliance and voluntary provision_ – Middle                    | No      |
| Distribution                 | Strongly _Market entitlement_ – Middle                                       | No      |
| Economic coordination        | Middle – Strongly _Market coordination_                                      | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** A government, like a household, has to live within its means.
Persistent deficits and rising debt push today's costs onto our children, crowd out
private investment, and leave the country exposed when a real crisis comes. Spending
should be restrained, budgets balanced over the cycle, and debt kept low, because
sound public finances are the foundation of prosperity and of honest government.

**Key concepts.**

- **Balanced budgets:** spending should be matched by revenue over time; borrowing is for
  emergencies and real investment.
- **Debt:** a burden on future taxpayers who had no say in incurring it.
- **Spending restraint:** the size of government should be limited by what citizens are
  willing to pay for now.
- **Fiscal rules:** binding limits protect public finances from politicians' incentive to
  spend now and pay later.

**Signature reasons.**

- "You can't spend money you don't have forever."
- "Debt is a tax on our children."
- "Every dollar the government spends was earned by someone."
- "Balance the budget before the next crisis, not during it."
- "Politicians will always spend more unless rules stop them."

**Telling it apart.**

- **New Right:** a whole program; its supply-side wing will accept deficits to cut taxes,
  which fiscal conservatives resist (_Money and public finance_; Reasons).
- **Libertarianism:** shrinks the state on principle, not mainly to balance the books
  (_Property in capital and goods_, _Welfare and social insurance_; Reasons).
- **Keynesianism:** wants governments to run deficits in slumps to sustain employment
  (_Money and public finance_, the central difference).
- **Modern Monetary Theory:** holds that a currency-issuing government faces no budget
  constraint except inflation (_Money and public finance_ at the opposite end).
- **Ordoliberalism:** shares the fiscal rules but adds a framework of competition law
  (_Economic scale and concentration_).

**Sources.** Henry Simons, "Rules versus Authorities in Monetary Policy" (1936); James
M. Buchanan & Richard E. Wagner, _Democracy in Deficit_ (1977); Stability and Growth
Pact (1997); Germany's debt brake (_Schuldenbremse_, 2009); Carmen Reinhart & Kenneth
Rogoff, _This Time Is Different_ (2009); Alberto Alesina, Carlo Favero & Francesco
Giavazzi, _Austerity: When It Works and When It Doesn't_ (2019).

## National conservatism

- **Type:** Ideology
- **Parent:** Conservatism
- **Also called:** "NatCon"; nationalist conservatism
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                                   | Range                                                                                      | Central |
| ------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------- |
| Sovereignty vs. pooled governance           | Leans _Sovereign self-government_ – Strongly _Sovereign self-government_                   | Yes     |
| Immigration and borders                     | Strongly _Community control over membership_ – Leans _Community control over membership_   | Yes     |
| Basis of nationhood                         | Middle – Strongly _Shared heritage and culture_                                            | Yes     |
| Compatriot priority vs. global impartiality | Strongly _Special obligations to compatriots_ – Leans _Special obligations to compatriots_ | No      |
| Religion and state                          | Strongly _Public role for religion_ – Leans _Public role for religion_                     | No      |
| Foreign-policy aims                         | Leans _National interest_ – Strongly _National interest_                                   | No      |
| Trade and economic openness                 | Middle – Strongly _Economic protection and self-reliance_                                  | No      |
| Economic coordination                       | Leans _Public direction_ – Leans _Market coordination_                                     | No      |
| Tradition or reason                         | Strongly _Inherited practice carries wisdom_ – Leans _Inherited practice carries wisdom_   | No      |
| Sex roles in family and public life         | Strongly _Distinct roles for the sexes_ – Middle                                           | No      |
| Neutrality vs. perfectionism                | Middle – Leans _State perfectionism_                                                       | No      |
| Pluralism vs. unity                         | Leans _Legitimate pluralism and opposition_ – Middle                                       | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** The world is best ordered as a family of independent nations, each
governing itself according to its own traditions, rather than as a single global order
run by transnational institutions and elites. A nation is held together by shared
heritage, language, religion, and loyalty, and those bonds, not abstract principles
alone, are what make citizens willing to sacrifice for one another. National
governments should therefore control their borders, restore their industries, honor
their religious traditions, and strengthen the family, so that their peoples can remain
free and themselves.

**Key concepts.**

- **The nation-state:** the proper unit of political order, between empire and tribe,
  and the guarantor of freedom and self-government.
- **Sovereignty:** each nation decides its own laws, borders, and economic policy, free of
  supranational authority.
- **Loyalty:** mutual loyalty among members of a nation, rooted in shared heritage, is
  the basis of solidarity and democracy.
- **Public religion:** a nation's religious tradition, usually biblical, is a source of
  its moral order and deserves public honor.
- **Economic nationalism:** the state should protect industry and workers as part of the
  nation's strength.

**Signature reasons.**

- "The world should be a family of independent nations, not one global empire."
- "A country that can't control its borders isn't really a country."
- "Shared heritage, not just shared principles, holds a nation together."
- "We need to make things here again."
- "Faith and family are the backbone of the nation."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Paleoconservatism:** a US-rooted, more localist and more consistently
  anti-interventionist form, warier of central state power (_Local vs. central_, _Use of
  force_; Reasons).
- **National populism:** shares the stance on immigration and sovereignty but is defined
  by anti-elite popular appeal rather than a conservative canon (_Tradition or reason_;
  Reasons).
- **Post-liberalism:** goes further, rejecting liberalism as such in favor of an
  explicitly common-good order (_Neutrality vs. perfectionism_, _Pluralism vs. unity_).
- **Liberal conservatism:** favors free trade and accepts pooled sovereignty (_Trade and
  economic openness_, _Sovereignty vs. pooled governance_).
- **Ethnic nationalism:** makes descent the basis of membership (_Basis of nationhood_ at
  the far end; _Race and ethnicity_).
- **Christian nationalism:** defines the nation itself as Christian, with law reflecting
  that heritage (_Religion and state_; Reasons).

**Sources.** Roger Scruton, _The Need for Nations_ (2004); John Fonte, _Sovereignty or
Submission_ (2011); Yoram Hazony, _The Virtue of Nationalism_ (2018) and _Conservatism: A
Rediscovery_ (2022); Rich Lowry, _The Case for Nationalism_ (2019); Edmund Burke
Foundation, "National Conservatism: A Statement of Principles" (2022).

## Post-liberalism

- **Type:** Ideology
- **Parent:** Conservatism
- **Also called:** postliberalism; common-good conservatism (Vermeule 2022)
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                           | Range                                                                                          | Central |
| ----------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------- |

| Legal moralism                      | Middle – Strongly _Enforcement of shared morality_                                             | Yes     |
| Individuals or wholes               | Leans _Groups and relations are real_ – Strongly _Groups and relations are real_               | No      |

| Economic coordination               | Leans _Public direction_ – Middle                                                              | No      |
| Economic scale and concentration    | Leans _Dispersed ownership and small scale_ – Strongly _Dispersed ownership and small scale_   | No      |
| Trade and economic openness         | Middle – Leans _Economic protection and self-reliance_                                         | No      |
| Welfare and social insurance        | Middle – Leans _Public social insurance_                                                       | No      |
| Workplace governance                | Leans _Worker voice and self-management_ – Middle                                              | No      |
| Religion and state                  | Strongly _Public role for religion_ – Leans _Public role for religion_                         | No      |
| Sex roles in family and public life | Strongly _Distinct roles for the sexes_ – Leans _Distinct roles for the sexes_                 | No      |
| Life and body                       | Leans _Protection of human life and dignity_ – Strongly _Protection of human life and dignity_ | No      |
| Pluralism vs. unity                 | Middle – Leans _Unified common purpose_                                                        | No      |
| Direction of history                | Leans _Progress is not assured_ – Strongly _Progress is not assured_                           | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Liberalism has not failed because it was betrayed but because it
succeeded. By treating people as autonomous choosers and the state as neutral among
ways of life, it has dissolved the family, community, religion, and stable work that
it quietly depended on, leaving isolated individuals facing a market and a state that
both grow without limit. Politics must again aim openly at the common good: the
flourishing of families, communities, and workers, protected from both cultural
liberalism and market liberalism.

**Key concepts.**

- **The common good:** a good shared by the community as a whole, which the state may and
  should pursue, not merely the sum of individual preferences.
- **Liberalism's self-undermining:** liberal individualism erodes the inherited
  institutions that once sustained liberal societies.
- **Two liberalisms, one project:** cultural liberalism on the left and market
  liberalism on the right are allies in dissolving community.
- **Order:** freedom properly understood is the capacity to live well within limits, not
  the absence of constraint.

**Signature reasons.**

- "Liberalism failed because it succeeded."
- "The free market and the sexual revolution are two sides of the same coin."
- "Government should aim at the common good, not stay neutral about how we live."
- "Workers and families need protection from both big business and big government."
- "Freedom without limits isn't freedom; it's loneliness."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Traditionalist conservatism:** conserves inherited institutions, including liberal
  ones; post-liberalism treats liberalism itself as the problem (_Neutrality vs.
  perfectionism_, _Tempo of change_; Reasons).
- **Catholic integralism:** the narrower doctrine that temporal power should be ordered
  to the Church's spiritual end; many post-liberals are not integralists (_Religion and
  state_ at the far end; Reasons).
- **Communitarianism:** shares the critique of individualism but stays within liberal
  democracy and its neutrality norms (_Legal moralism_, _Neutrality vs. perfectionism_).
- **Blue Labour:** the labour-movement cousin, with more weight on worker voice and
  economic security (_Workplace governance_, _Welfare and social insurance_).
- **National conservatism:** centers the sovereign nation rather than a critique of
  liberalism as such (_Sovereignty vs. pooled governance_; Reasons).

**Sources.** Alasdair MacIntyre, _After Virtue_ (1981); John Milbank & Adrian Pabst, _The
Politics of Virtue_ (2016); Patrick Deneen, _Why Liberalism Failed_ (2018) and _Regime
Change_ (2023); Adrian Vermeule, _Common Good Constitutionalism_ (2022); Sohrab Ahmari,
_Tyranny, Inc._ (2023).

## Counter-revolutionary traditionalism

- **Type:** Ideology
- **Parent:** Conservatism
- **Also called:** reaction (embraced by some adherents, e.g. Gómez Dávila; pejorative
  in critics' use); legitimism and Carlism (historical national forms)
- **Variants:** Integral nationalism (Maurrassism)
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                           | Range                                                                              | Central |
| ----------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------- |
| Religion and state                  | Strongly _Public role for religion_                                                | Yes     |
| Social hierarchy                    | Leans _Ordered rank and deference_ – Strongly _Ordered rank and deference_         | Yes     |
| Pluralism vs. unity                 | Leans _Unified common purpose_ – Strongly _Unified common purpose_                 | Yes     |
| Hierarchy or equality               | Strongly _Hierarchy is natural_ – Leans _Hierarchy is natural_                     | No      |
| Leadership                          | Strongly _Strong, decisive leadership_ – Leans _Strong, decisive leadership_       | No      |
| Source of moral authority           | Strongly _Given from beyond us_                                                    | No      |
| Transcendence                       | Strongly _A transcendent order exists_                                             | No      |
| Tradition or reason                 | Strongly _Inherited practice carries wisdom_                                       | No      |
| Individuals or wholes               | Strongly _Groups and relations are real_                                           | No      |
| Legal moralism                      | Leans _Enforcement of shared morality_ – Strongly _Enforcement of shared morality_ | No      |
| Sex roles in family and public life | Strongly _Distinct roles for the sexes_                                            | No      |
| Direction of history                | Strongly _Progress is not assured_                                                 | No      |
| Local vs. central                   | Strongly _Local and regional self-rule_ – Middle                                   | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** The French Revolution and its heirs rest on false principles: that
society is a contract among isolated individuals, that authority comes from below, and
that human reason can rebuild the social order from nothing. Society is in truth a
God-given order of families, estates, corporations, and provinces, crowned by legitimate
authority and guided by the Church. What the Revolution destroyed, throne and altar,
hierarchy and faith, is not a past to mourn but a true order to restore.

**Key concepts.**

- **The Revolution:** not a single event but an ongoing principle, the rebellion of
  human will against divine and natural order.
- **Legitimacy:** authority descends from God through historic, hereditary institutions,
  not from popular consent.
- **Organic society:** a hierarchy of intermediate bodies (family, guild, province,
  Church) rather than a mass of equal individuals facing the state.
- **Throne and altar:** political and spiritual authority united in service of the
  common and eternal good.

**Signature reasons.**

- "Society is not a contract; it is an order given by God."
- "The rights of man ended in the Terror."
- "Authority comes from above, not from the ballot box."
- "Equality is a revolutionary lie; real societies are ordered hierarchies."
- "We don't want to slow the Revolution down; we want to undo it."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Traditionalist conservatism:** conserves the existing, largely post-revolutionary
  order and changes it gradually (_Tempo of change_, _Pluralism vs. unity_, _Social
  hierarchy_).
- **Catholic integralism:** a doctrine of Church and state that need not demand monarchy
  or a society of estates (_Leadership_, _Social hierarchy_; Reasons).
- **Monarchism:** a Thin Label for any form of monarchy, including constitutional
  monarchy within a liberal democracy (Reasons).
- **Traditionalism:** grounds its rejection of modernity in a perennialist metaphysics of
  primordial tradition (Guénon, Evola), not Catholic doctrine (Reasons).
- **Neoreaction:** also rejects democracy and equality, but in a secular, technological
  form that favors corporate sovereignty over throne and altar (_Transcendence_,
  _Religion and state_).
- **Fascism:** a modern, revolutionary mass movement led by a party, which
  counter-revolutionaries have often opposed as another child of the Revolution
  (Reasons; _Tempo of change_).

**Sources.** Louis de Bonald, _Theory of Political and Religious Power_ (1796); Joseph de
Maistre, _Considerations on France_ (1797); Juan Donoso Cortés, _Essay on Catholicism,
Liberalism, and Socialism_ (1851); Charles Maurras, _Enquête sur la monarchie_ (1900);
Plinio Corrêa de Oliveira, _Revolution and Counter-Revolution_ (1959); Nicolás Gómez
Dávila, _Escolios a un texto implícito_ (1977); Darrin McMahon, _Enemies of the
Enlightenment_ (2001).

## Gaullism

- **Type:** Ideology
- **Parent:** Conservatism
- **Also called:** neo-Gaullism (its later party forms)
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                                         | Range                                                                        | Central |
| ------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------- |
| Sovereignty vs. pooled governance                 | Leans _Sovereign self-government_ – Strongly _Sovereign self-government_     | Yes     |
| Leadership                                        | Strongly _Strong, decisive leadership_ – Leans _Strong, decisive leadership_ | Yes     |
| Economic coordination                             | Leans _Public direction_ – Middle                                            | Yes     |
| Foreign-policy aims                               | Leans _National interest_ – Strongly _National interest_                     | No      |
| Use of force                                      | Strongly _Readiness to use force_ – Leans _Readiness to use force_           | No      |
| Design vs. evolved order                          | Middle – Leans _Deliberate design_                                           | No      |
| Technology and risk                               | Strongly _Proaction_ – Leans _Proaction_                                     | No      |
| Pluralism vs. unity                               | Middle – Leans _Unified common purpose_                                      | No      |
| Representation vs. participation                  | Leans _Direct and participatory self-rule_ – Middle                          | No      |
| Popular sovereignty vs. constitutional constraint | Middle – Leans _Popular and legislative supremacy_                           | No      |
| Workplace governance                              | Leans _Worker voice and self-management_ – Middle                            | No      |
| Welfare and social insurance                      | Middle – Leans _Public social insurance_                                     | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** France, and any great nation, must remain master of its own
destiny. That requires a state strong enough to act: an executive above the quarrels of
parties, drawing its legitimacy directly from the people through elections and
referendums; a nation independent in defense, with its own nuclear deterrent; and a
state that plans and drives modernization, from industry to technology. Europe should
be a cooperation of sovereign nations, and France an ally of the West, never its
subordinate.

**Key concepts.**

- **National independence:** freedom of action in defense, diplomacy, and economy; no
  subordination to any bloc or supranational body.
- **The strong executive:** a head of state above parties, directly accountable to the
  nation (the Fifth Republic's presidency).
- **Rassemblement:** gathering the nation above the divisions of left and right and of
  party politics.
- **Dirigisme:** the state plans, invests, and leads modernization in partnership with
  private enterprise.
- **Participation:** association of workers in the life and profits of the firm, and of
  citizens through referendums.

**Signature reasons.**

- "France cannot be France without greatness."
- "A Europe of nations, not a European superstate."
- "The state must lead the country's modernization."
- "We need a leader above parties, answerable directly to the people."
- "An ally, yes; a vassal, never."

**Telling it apart.**

- **National conservatism:** also sovereigntist, but more religious and socially
  traditional and less dirigiste (_Economic coordination_, _Religion and state_).
- **Liberal conservatism:** more Atlanticist, market-led, and parliamentary
  (_Leadership_, _Sovereignty vs. pooled governance_, _Economic coordination_).
- **Christian democracy:** favors European integration and subsidiarity
  (_Sovereignty vs. pooled governance_, _Civil society vs. the state_).
- **Kemalism:** also pairs a strong state with nationalist modernization, but with
  assertive secularism and a one-party past (_Religion and state_, _Pluralism vs.
  unity_).
- **Peronism:** a populist, state-led nationalism built on the labour movement
  (_Workplace governance_; Reasons).

**Sources.** Charles de Gaulle, _The Edge of the Sword_ (1932), Bayeux speech (1946),
_War Memoirs_ (1954–59), and _Memoirs of Hope_ (1970); Constitution of the Fifth Republic
(1958); Stanley Hoffmann, _Decline or Renewal? France since the 1930s_ (1974); Serge
Berstein, _The Republic of de Gaulle_ (1993); Julian Jackson, _A Certain Idea of France_
(2018).

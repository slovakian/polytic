# Other Thin Labels: Label cards

Cards for this family, written to the template in `../label-library.md`, in the order
of the list. Ecologism and Feminism head their own families (`ecologism.md`,
`feminism.md`). Every Label here is Thin: it rides on a host Ideology, so each card
lists only the Dimensions its thin core commits it to and leaves the rest to the host
(a left and a right populist both fit Populism). Every card is written with the same
dispassion as any other: the self-description is the tradition's own
self-understanding, steelmanned, and never Polytic's verdict (`../principles.md`).

## Populism

- **Type:** Thin
- **Parent:** —
- **Also called:** popular politics; often a critics' term, which some adherents
  embrace (Laclau, Mouffe)
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                                         | Range                                                                                      | Central |
| ------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------- |
| Pluralism vs. unity                               | Middle – Strongly _Unified common purpose_                                                 | Yes     |
| Popular sovereignty vs. constitutional constraint | Leans _Popular and legislative supremacy_ – Strongly _Popular and legislative supremacy_   | Yes     |
| Popular judgment vs. expertise                    | Leans _Equal popular judgment_ – Strongly _Equal popular judgment_                         | No      |
| Expert trust                                      | Leans _Judge for yourself_ – Strongly _Judge for yourself_                                 | No      |
| Representation vs. participation                  | Strongly _Direct and participatory self-rule_ – Leans _Direct and participatory self-rule_ | No      |
| Leadership                                        | Strongly _Strong, decisive leadership_ – Middle                                            | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Democracy means rule by the people, but ordinary people have lost
control of it to a self-serving elite of politicians, officials, judges, financiers, and
media figures who look after one another. Politics should give voice to what the people
actually want, and the people's will should not be blocked by unelected bodies or
by an establishment that disdains them. Common sense and lived experience count for at
least as much as credentials.

**Key concepts.**

- **The people:** the ordinary, decent majority, whose interests and will are basically
  one.
- **The elite:** a narrow, connected establishment that has captured power and serves
  itself.
- **The general will:** what the people want, which politics should express directly.
- **Common sense:** practical wisdom of ordinary people, against the abstractions of
  experts.

**Signature reasons.**

- "Power belongs to the people, not the establishment."
- "Politicians have stopped listening to ordinary people."
- "Unelected judges and bureaucrats shouldn't overrule the voters."
- "The experts have been wrong before."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Left populism:** builds the people against an economic oligarchy (_Distribution_).
- **National populism:** an Ideology combining populism with nativism and strict order
  (_Immigration and borders_, _Basis of nationhood_).
- **Direct democracy:** favours referendums and initiatives on procedural grounds,
  without the people-versus-elite frame (_Pluralism vs. unity_; Reasons).

**Sources.** People's Party, Omaha Platform (1892); Michael Kazin, _The Populist
Persuasion_ (1995); Margaret Canovan, "Trust the People!" (_Political Studies_, 1999);
Cas Mudde, "The Populist Zeitgeist" (_Government and Opposition_, 2004); Ernesto Laclau,
_On Populist Reason_ (2005); Jan-Werner Müller, _What Is Populism?_ (2016); Cas Mudde &
Cristóbal Rovira Kaltwasser, _Populism: A Very Short Introduction_ (2017).

## Left populism

- **Type:** Thin
- **Parent:** Populism
- **Also called:** radical democratic populism
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                                         | Range                                                                        | Central |
| ------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------- |
| Distribution                                      | Leans _Egalitarian redistribution_ – Strongly _Egalitarian redistribution_   | Yes     |
| Popular sovereignty vs. constitutional constraint | Middle – Strongly _Popular and legislative supremacy_                        | Yes     |
| Economic scale and concentration                  | Middle – Strongly _Dispersed ownership and small scale_                      | No      |
| Popular judgment vs. expertise                    | Leans _Equal popular judgment_ – Strongly _Equal popular judgment_           | No      |
| Pluralism vs. unity                               | Leans _Legitimate pluralism and opposition_ – Leans _Unified common purpose_ | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** A small oligarchy of finance and corporate power has captured
democracy, and decades of consensus politics left ordinary people without a real
choice. The answer is to build a popular majority, "the people", from many different
demands (workers, renters, the indebted, the excluded) against that oligarchy, and to
radicalise democracy rather than abandon it. Politics is about drawing a line between
us and them, and the left must draw it against the few at the top, not against
immigrants or minorities.

**Key concepts.**

- **Chain of equivalence:** unconnected demands united by a common opponent into one
  popular subject (Laclau).
- **The oligarchy:** concentrated economic power that has hollowed out democracy.
- **Radical democracy:** deepening equality and liberty within pluralist institutions,
  not replacing them (Mouffe).
- **Hegemony:** winning the common sense of the age, not only elections.

**Signature reasons.**

- "We are the 99 per cent."
- "For the many, not the few."
- "It's the people against the oligarchy."
- "Democracy has been hijacked by money."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Populism:** the parent; left populism specifies the elite as economic and the people
  as plural and inclusive (_Distribution_; Reasons).
- **National populism:** draws the line against outsiders and immigrants (_Immigration
  and borders_).
- **Democratic socialism:** may share the goals, but works through a class and party
  frame rather than people-versus-elite (Reasons).
- **Agonistic democracy:** Mouffe's theory of democratic conflict that underpins left
  populism as a strategy (Reasons).

**Sources.** Ernesto Laclau & Chantal Mouffe, _Hegemony and Socialist Strategy_ (1985);
Ernesto Laclau, _On Populist Reason_ (2005); Íñigo Errejón & Chantal Mouffe, _Podemos:
In the Name of the People_ (2016); Chantal Mouffe, _For a Left Populism_ (2018); Thomas
Frank, _The People, No_ (2020).

## Agrarian populism

- **Type:** Thin
- **Parent:** Populism
- **Also called:** farmers' populism; peasant populism; narodnichestvo (the Russian
  Narodniks, historical)
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                        | Range                                                                                        | Central |
| -------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------- |
| Economic scale and concentration | Leans _Dispersed ownership and small scale_ – Strongly _Dispersed ownership and small scale_ | Yes     |
| Land and natural resources       | Strongly _Common inheritance_ – Middle                                                       | No      |
| Money and public finance         | Strongly _Discretion and active management_ – Leans _Discretion and active management_       | No      |
| Trade and economic openness      | Middle – Strongly _Economic protection and self-reliance_                                    | No      |
| Work                             | Strongly _Work is a source of worth_ – Leans _Work is a source of worth_                     | No      |
| Local vs. central                | Strongly _Local and regional self-rule_ – Leans _Local and regional self-rule_               | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** The people who grow the food and work the land are the backbone of
the nation, yet they are squeezed by banks, railroads, grain traders, agribusiness, and
distant governments that profit from their labour. Land belongs to those who work it.
Credit should be cheap and money plentiful for producers, not tight for the benefit of
creditors, and rural communities should control their own food and future.

**Key concepts.**

- **Producerism:** those who make things (farmers, artisans, workers) create wealth;
  financiers and middlemen take it.
- **Land to the tiller:** land should belong to those who work it, not to absentee owners
  or corporations.
- **Monopoly:** concentrated railroads, banks, and agribusiness that hold farmers hostage.
- **Food sovereignty:** communities' right to control their own food systems (La Vía
  Campesina).

**Signature reasons.**

- "The land belongs to those who work it."
- "Farmers feed the nation and get nothing but debt."
- "Wall Street and the big traders take the profit; we do the work."
- "Food sovereignty, not free trade."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Agrarianism:** honours farming life morally, without the people-versus-elite
  politics (Reasons).
- **Distributism:** also wants widely held property, but grounds it in Catholic social
  teaching and covers all property (Reasons).
- **Left populism:** builds a wider people against oligarchy, centred on urban working
  classes (Reasons).

**Sources.** People's Party, Omaha Platform (1892); Alexander Chayanov, _The Theory of
Peasant Economy_ (1925); Franco Venturi, _Roots of Revolution_ (1952; trans. 1960);
Lawrence Goodwyn, _Democratic Promise: The Populist Moment in America_ (1976); La Vía
Campesina, Declaration of Nyéléni (2007).

## Nationalism

- **Type:** Thin
- **Parent:** —
- **Also called:** patriotism (as many adherents prefer); national self-determination
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                                   | Range                                                                                      | Central |
| ------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------- |
| Compatriot priority vs. global impartiality | Strongly _Special obligations to compatriots_ – Leans _Special obligations to compatriots_ | Yes     |
| Moral circle                                | Strongly _Near and dear first_ – Middle                                                    | No      |
| Sovereignty vs. pooled governance           | Middle – Strongly _Sovereign self-government_                                              | No      |
| Individuals or wholes                       | Middle – Strongly _Groups and relations are real_                                          | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Human beings live in nations, communities bound by a shared
history, culture, and sense of belonging, and the nation is the natural home of
political life. A people has the right to govern itself, and its members owe one
another loyalty and special care. A world of free, self-governing nations is better than
an empire or a single global order, because it lets each people live by its own lights.
The nation is named in the narrative; this Label applies to any nation.

**Key concepts.**

- **The nation:** a community with a shared identity and history, which its members
  experience as theirs.
- **Self-determination:** the right of a nation to govern itself, in its own state or with
  autonomy.
- **Loyalty:** special obligations among members of the nation, above duties to
  humanity at large.
- **Sovereignty:** final authority over a people's own affairs.

**Signature reasons.**

- "A people has the right to govern itself."
- "We owe more to our fellow citizens than to strangers."
- "A world of nations is freer than an empire."
- "The nation is a family writ large."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Civic nationalism**, **Ethnic nationalism**, **Liberal nationalism**: branches that
  say what makes the nation (_Basis of nationhood_).
- **Cosmopolitanism:** holds that borders have little moral weight (_Compatriot priority
  vs. global impartiality_, the central Dimension).
- **Sovereigntism:** defends national self-government against supranational bodies
  specifically, not loyalty to the nation as such (_Sovereignty vs. pooled governance_).

**Sources.** Johann Gottfried Herder, _Ideas for the Philosophy of History of Humanity_
(1784–91); Giuseppe Mazzini, _The Duties of Man_ (1860); Ernest Renan, "What Is a
Nation?" (1882); Ernest Gellner, _Nations and Nationalism_ (1983); Benedict Anderson,
_Imagined Communities_ (1983); Anthony D. Smith, _National Identity_ (1991); Yoram Hazony,
_The Virtue of Nationalism_ (2018); Nenad Miscevic, "Nationalism", _Stanford
Encyclopedia of Philosophy_.

## Civic nationalism

- **Type:** Thin
- **Parent:** Nationalism
- **Also called:** civic patriotism; constitutional patriotism (its most
  principle-centred form)
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                                   | Range                                                                   | Central |
| ------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------- |
| Basis of nationhood                         | Strongly _Shared civic principles_ – Leans _Shared civic principles_    | Yes     |
| Compatriot priority vs. global impartiality | Strongly _Special obligations to compatriots_ – Middle                  | No      |
| Group difference                            | Middle – Strongly _Difference-blind common rules_                       | No      |
| Immigration and borders                     | Leans _Community control over membership_ – Leans _Freedom of movement_ | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** A nation is a community of citizens who share a commitment to the
same political principles and institutions, not a tribe of common blood. Anyone who
accepts those principles and takes up the duties of citizenship can belong, whatever
their ancestry, religion, or mother tongue. That shared allegiance is a real bond, worth
loyalty and sacrifice, and it lets a diverse people govern itself together. The nation
is named in the narrative.

**Key concepts.**

- **Citizenship:** membership defined by law and allegiance, open to all who take it up.
- **Shared principles:** the constitution, rights, and civic values that hold a nation
  together.
- **Daily plebiscite:** a nation lives by its members' continuing will to share a common
  life (Renan).
- **Integration:** newcomers join the nation by adopting its civic commitments.

**Signature reasons.**

- "Being American (or French, or Canadian) is about what you believe, not where your
  grandparents came from."
- "A nation is a daily plebiscite."
- "Anyone can become one of us by taking up our values."
- "Patriotism is loyalty to the constitution, not to a race."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Ethnic nationalism:** defines the nation by descent, culture, and language (_Basis of
  nationhood_, the central Dimension).
- **Liberal nationalism:** gives shared culture a larger role in nationhood and
  solidarity (_Basis of nationhood_).
- **French republicanism:** a full Ideology with a civic nation at its core, plus strict
  secularism and an indivisible republic (_Religion and state_).
- **Cosmopolitanism:** shares the universal principles, but denies they ground special
  loyalty to one nation (_Compatriot priority vs. global impartiality_).

**Sources.** Ernest Renan, "What Is a Nation?" (1882); Hans Kohn, _The Idea of
Nationalism_ (1944); Jürgen Habermas, "Citizenship and National Identity" (1992);
Michael Ignatieff, _Blood and Belonging_ (1993); Maurizio Viroli, _For Love of Country_
(1995).

## Ethnic nationalism

- **Type:** Thin
- **Parent:** Nationalism
- **Also called:** ethnonationalism; ethnocultural nationalism; völkisch nationalism (its
  German historical form)
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                                   | Range                                                                                      | Central |
| ------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------- |
| Basis of nationhood                         | Leans _Shared heritage and culture_ – Strongly _Shared heritage and culture_               | Yes     |
| Compatriot priority vs. global impartiality | Strongly _Special obligations to compatriots_ – Leans _Special obligations to compatriots_ | No      |
| Immigration and borders                     | Strongly _Community control over membership_ – Leans _Community control over membership_   | No      |
| Sovereignty vs. pooled governance           | Leans _Sovereign self-government_ – Strongly _Sovereign self-government_                   | No      |
| Individuals or wholes                       | Leans _Groups and relations are real_ – Strongly _Groups and relations are real_           | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** A nation is a people bound by shared ancestry, language, culture,
and memory, built over many generations. It is not a contract that anyone can sign.
Each such people has a right to its own state, where it can preserve its identity and
live by its own traditions, and to decide who joins it. Without a homeland of its own,
a people's culture and continuity are at the mercy of others. The nation is named in the
narrative; this Label covers any nation without a distinct canon of its own.

**Key concepts.**

- **The nation:** a community of common descent and culture, prior to the state.
- **Homeland:** a territory that belongs to a people by history and attachment.
- **Continuity:** the survival of a people's language, culture, and character across
  generations.
- **Self-determination:** each people's right to its own state.

**Signature reasons.**

- "A nation is a people, not just a passport."
- "Every people deserves a state of its own."
- "Our language and culture are what make us who we are."
- "We decide who becomes one of us."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Civic nationalism:** defines the nation by shared political principles (_Basis of
  nationhood_, the central Dimension).
- **White nationalism:** centres a race spanning many nations rather than one nation
  (centered group).
- **Liberal nationalism:** values national culture but within liberal limits and with
  openness to newcomers (_Immigration and borders_; Reasons).
- **National populism:** an Ideology joining nativism to populism and strict order; it
  often defines the nation culturally rather than by descent (_Pluralism vs. unity_,
  _Leadership_).

**Sources.** Johann Gottlieb Fichte, _Addresses to the German Nation_ (1808); Anthony D.
Smith, _The Ethnic Origins of Nations_ (1986); Walker Connor, _Ethnonationalism_ (1994);
Steven Grosby, _Nationalism: A Very Short Introduction_ (2005); Azar Gat, _Nations_
(2013).

## Liberal nationalism

- **Type:** Thin
- **Parent:** Nationalism / Liberalism
- **Also called:** —
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                                   | Range                                                                                        | Central |
| ------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------- |
| Basis of nationhood                         | Leans _Shared civic principles_ – Leans _Shared heritage and culture_                        | Yes     |
| Compatriot priority vs. global impartiality | Strongly _Special obligations to compatriots_ – Leans _Special obligations to compatriots_   | Yes     |
| Welfare and social insurance                | Leans _Public social insurance_ – Strongly _Public social insurance_                         | No      |
| Pluralism vs. unity                         | Strongly _Legitimate pluralism and opposition_ – Leans _Legitimate pluralism and opposition_ | No      |
| Group difference                            | Strongly _Group-differentiated recognition_ – Middle                                         | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** People are not free-floating individuals. A shared national culture
gives them a context of meaningful choices, a language to live in, and a sense of
belonging, and it grounds the trust and solidarity a welfare state and a democracy
need. Liberals should not leave nationalism to its illiberal forms. A nation can value
its culture and give its members priority while respecting individual rights, welcoming
newcomers who join its common life, and granting minority nations their own claims.

**Key concepts.**

- **Societal culture:** a national culture that provides the options among which free
  people choose (Kymlicka, Tamir).
- **Solidarity:** shared nationhood motivates the sacrifices that redistribution and
  democracy require (Miller).
- **National responsibility:** nations can be accountable for their past and choices.
- **Liberal limits:** national priority stops at individual rights and human rights.

**Signature reasons.**

- "Liberal democracy needs a 'we' to work."
- "People are willing to share with those they see as their own."
- "National culture is part of what makes our choices meaningful."
- "You can love your nation and still respect everyone's rights."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Civic nationalism:** grounds the nation in political principles alone (_Basis of
  nationhood_).
- **Ethnic nationalism:** defines the nation by descent and restricts membership more
  tightly (_Basis of nationhood_, _Immigration and borders_).
- **Multicultural liberalism:** an Ideology that also grounds rights in cultural
  membership, with more emphasis on minorities than on the national majority (Reasons).

**Sources.** Giuseppe Mazzini, _The Duties of Man_ (1860); Yael Tamir, _Liberal
Nationalism_ (1993) and _Why Nationalism_ (2019); David Miller, _On Nationality_ (1995);
Margaret Canovan, _Nationhood and Political Theory_ (1996); Will Kymlicka, _Politics in
the Vernacular_ (2001).

## Stateless-nation nationalism

- **Type:** Thin
- **Parent:** Nationalism
- **Also called:** sub-state nationalism; minority nationalism; regional nationalism;
  separatism or secessionism (often critics' terms)
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                                   | Range                                                                                      | Central |
| ------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------- |
| Local vs. central                           | Strongly _Local and regional self-rule_ – Leans _Local and regional self-rule_             | Yes     |
| Group difference                            | Strongly _Group-differentiated recognition_ – Leans _Group-differentiated recognition_     | Yes     |
| Compatriot priority vs. global impartiality | Strongly _Special obligations to compatriots_ – Leans _Special obligations to compatriots_ | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Our nation (Scotland, Catalonia, Quebec, Flanders, the Basque
Country, and others) has its own history, language or institutions, and identity, but no
state of its own. Decisions about our future are made by a larger state in which we
are a permanent minority. We have the same right as any nation to govern ourselves,
whether through deep autonomy or independence, decided democratically by our own
people. The nation is named in the narrative.

**Key concepts.**

- **Nation without a state:** a people with a national identity inside a state run by
  a larger nation.
- **Self-government:** autonomy or independence as the means of a nation's
  self-determination.
- **Recognition:** acknowledgement of the nation's distinct status, language, and
  institutions.
- **Democratic mandate:** the people of the nation should decide its constitutional
  future by vote.

**Signature reasons.**

- "Decisions about our nation should be made by our nation."
- "We are a nation, not a region."
- "Our language deserves the same respect as theirs."
- "Let the people decide."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Civic nationalism**, **Ethnic nationalism:** these say what makes a nation; a
  stateless-nation nationalist can be either, and is defined by seeking self-rule
  within or from a larger state (_Local vs. central_; Reasons).
- **Indigenous resurgence:** grounds claims in prior, never-ceded sovereignty and land
  relations (centered group; _Historical injustice_).
- **Multiculturalism:** accommodates cultural minorities within a shared state, without
  a claim to separate nationhood (Reasons).

**Sources.** Tom Nairn, _The Break-Up of Britain_ (1977); Allen Buchanan, _Secession_
(1991); Will Kymlicka, _Multicultural Citizenship_ (1995); Michael Keating, _Nations
Against the State_ (1996; 2nd ed. 2001); Montserrat Guibernau, _Nations Without States_
(1999).

## Pan-Africanism

- **Type:** Thin
- **Parent:** Nationalism
- **Also called:** —
- **Variants:** Négritude
- **Centered group:** Africa and the peoples of the African diaspora

### Positions

| Dimension                                   | Range                                                                                      | Central |
| ------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------- |
| Historical injustice                        | Leans _Rectification_ – Strongly _Rectification_                                           | Yes     |
| Compatriot priority vs. global impartiality | Strongly _Special obligations to compatriots_ – Leans _Special obligations to compatriots_ | No      |
| Sovereignty vs. pooled governance           | Strongly _Pooled, rule-bound international governance_ – Middle                            | No      |
| Trade and economic openness                 | Middle – Strongly _Economic protection and self-reliance_                                  | No      |
| Basis of nationhood                         | Middle – Strongly _Shared heritage and culture_                                            | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Africans and people of African descent across the world share a
history of enslavement, colonisation, and racism, and a common future. Divided into
small states with borders drawn by empires, and scattered across the Americas and
Europe, they remain weak before the powers that once ruled them. Unity, in political
union across the continent and solidarity across the diaspora, is the path to real
independence, economic self-reliance, dignity, and repair for the wrongs of the past.

**Key concepts.**

- **African unity:** the political union of African states, as a United States of Africa
  or a strong continental union (Nkrumah).
- **Diaspora:** Africans abroad are part of one people with Africa.
- **Neo-colonialism:** formal independence without economic independence (Nkrumah).
- **Reparations:** repair owed for slavery and colonialism.
- **Négritude:** the affirmation of a shared Black culture and identity (Senghor,
  Césaire).

**Signature reasons.**

- "Africa must unite."
- "Africa for the Africans, at home and abroad."
- "Independence without economic independence is not independence."
- "The borders were drawn by empires, not by us."

**Centered group.** Africa and the peoples of the African diaspora, as one people.

**Telling it apart.**

- **Black nationalism:** seeks self-determination for Black people within or apart from
  the countries where they live, often in the Americas; Pan-Africanism centres the
  continent and its unity (Reasons).
- **African socialism:** an Ideology many Pan-Africanists shared, about the economy of
  each new state (Reasons).
- **Anti-imperialism:** opposes imperial domination generally, not the unity of one
  people (centered group).

**Sources.** W. E. B. Du Bois, _The Souls of Black Folk_ (1903), and the Pan-African
Congresses (1919–45); Marcus Garvey, _Philosophy and Opinions of Marcus Garvey_
(1923–25); George Padmore, _Pan-Africanism or Communism?_ (1956); Kwame Nkrumah, _Africa
Must Unite_ (1963) and _Neo-Colonialism_ (1965); Organisation of African Unity Charter
(1963); Walter Rodney, _How Europe Underdeveloped Africa_ (1972); Hakim Adi,
_Pan-Africanism: A History_ (2018).

## Environmentalism

- **Type:** Thin
- **Parent:** —
- **Also called:** reform environmentalism; mainstream environmentalism; conservationism
  (its land-protection strand); critics: "shallow ecology" (Naess)
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                | Range                                                                          | Central |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------- |
| Environmental protection | Leans _Ecological protection first_ – Strongly _Ecological protection first_   | Yes     |
| Growth                   | Leans _Sufficiency and limits_ – Strongly _Growth and abundance_               | No      |
| Standing of nature       | Strongly _Human-centered_ – Leans _Nature-centered_                            | No      |
| Channels of change       | Strongly _Within institutions_ – Leans _Within institutions_                   | No      |
| Expert trust             | Strongly _Defer to expert institutions_ – Leans _Defer to expert institutions_ | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Pollution, climate change, and the loss of wild places are serious
problems, and they can be solved. Science tells us what is happening, and good laws,
regulation, clean technology, and international agreements can fix it without tearing
up the economy or asking everyone to change how they live. Protecting the environment
is part of good government and of a decent, prosperous society, not a revolution
against it.

**Key concepts.**

- **Sustainable development:** meeting present needs without compromising the future,
  within a growing economy (Brundtland).
- **Regulation:** limits on pollution and standards for industry, set by government on
  scientific advice.
- **Conservation:** protecting land, wildlife, and resources for people's use and
  enjoyment now and later.
- **Science:** environmental policy should follow the evidence.

**Signature reasons.**

- "Follow the science."
- "A clean environment and a strong economy go together."
- "We can fix this with the right policies and technologies."
- "Protect nature for our children to enjoy."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Ecologism:** holds that ecological limits require changing the economy and our
  values, not only policy; environmentalism is reform within existing arrangements
  (_Growth_, _Standing of nature_; Reasons).
- **Green liberalism:** an Ideology that gives a principled liberal case for protection
  (Reasons).
- **Ecomodernism:** pins its hopes on rapid technological transformation, especially
  nuclear and intensive agriculture (_Technology and risk_).

**Sources.** Gifford Pinchot, _The Fight for Conservation_ (1910); Rachel Carson, _Silent
Spring_ (1962); World Commission on Environment and Development, _Our Common Future_
(1987); William Nordhaus, _The Climate Casino_ (2013). On the contrast with ecologism:
Arne Naess, "The Shallow and the Deep, Long-Range Ecology Movement" (1973); Andrew
Dobson, _Green Political Thought_ (1990; 4th ed. 2007).

## Multiculturalism

- **Type:** Thin
- **Parent:** —
- **Also called:** cultural pluralism; the politics of recognition; critics: "identity
  politics"
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension             | Range                                                                                  | Central |
| --------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------- |
| Group difference      | Strongly _Group-differentiated recognition_ – Leans _Group-differentiated recognition_ | Yes     |
| Basis of nationhood   | Strongly _Shared civic principles_ – Leans _Shared civic principles_                   | No      |
| Individuals or wholes | Middle – Strongly _Groups and relations are real_                                      | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Modern societies contain many cultures, faiths, and peoples, and
asking minorities to shed their identities to belong is neither fair nor necessary.
People's cultures are part of who they are, and denying them public recognition
harms them. A shared civic life is strengthened, not weakened, when the state
accommodates difference: in schools, dress, holidays, language, and law, so that
everyone can belong without ceasing to be themselves.

**Key concepts.**

- **Recognition:** being seen and respected for who one is, including one's culture; its
  absence is a real harm (Taylor).
- **Accommodation:** adjusting rules and institutions to make room for minority
  practices.
- **Integration without assimilation:** belonging to a shared society while keeping one's
  identity.
- **Diversity:** a source of strength and richness in a society.

**Signature reasons.**

- "You shouldn't have to give up your culture to belong."
- "Diversity is our strength."
- "Equal treatment sometimes means accommodating difference."
- "Recognition is a vital human need."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Multicultural liberalism:** an Ideology that grounds group rights in liberal
  autonomy; this Thin Label marks the commitment on any host (Reasons).
- **French republicanism:** keeps group identity out of public institutions (_Group
  difference_, the central Dimension).
- **Civic nationalism:** shares civic nationhood but expects common rules for all
  (_Group difference_).
- **Identity-conscious progressivism:** extends recognition into an analysis of
  structural oppression and equity of outcomes (_Causes of fortune_).

**Sources.** Canadian Multiculturalism Act (1988); Charles Taylor, "The Politics of
Recognition" (1992); Will Kymlicka, _Multicultural Citizenship_ (1995); Bhikhu Parekh,
_Rethinking Multiculturalism_ (2000) and the Parekh Report, _The Future of Multi-Ethnic
Britain_ (2000); Tariq Modood, _Multiculturalism_ (2007; 2nd ed. 2013).

## Cosmopolitanism

- **Type:** Thin
- **Parent:** —
- **Also called:** world citizenship; global justice (its distributive strand)
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                                   | Range                                                                                  | Central |
| ------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------- |
| Compatriot priority vs. global impartiality | Leans _Impartial concern for all people_ – Strongly _Impartial concern for all people_ | Yes     |
| Moral circle                                | Leans _Everyone counts equally_ – Strongly _Everyone counts equally_                   | Yes     |
| Immigration and borders                     | Middle – Strongly _Freedom of movement_                                                | No      |
| Foreign-policy aims                         | Strongly _Universal values_ – Leans _Universal values_                                 | No      |
| Sovereignty vs. pooled governance           | Strongly _Pooled, rule-bound international governance_ – Middle                        | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Every human being has equal moral worth, and that worth does not
stop at a border. Where someone happens to be born is an accident, and it cannot
justify caring less about their suffering or their rights. Our first loyalty is to
humanity. Nations and states can be useful, but their claims are limited by what we
owe to everyone, including the global poor and people trying to move to a better
life.

**Key concepts.**

- **World citizenship:** belonging first to the community of all human beings (the
  Stoics, Kant).
- **Equal moral worth:** each person counts equally, wherever they are.
- **Global justice:** principles of justice apply across borders, not only within them.
- **Rooted cosmopolitanism:** local attachments are compatible with universal concern,
  as long as they do not override it (Appiah).

**Signature reasons.**

- "I am a citizen of the world."
- "Where you're born is an accident. It shouldn't decide your worth."
- "A child drowning far away is as much a child as one nearby."
- "Borders are lines on a map, not lines of moral concern."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Nationalism:** gives priority to compatriots (_Compatriot priority vs. global
  impartiality_, the central Dimension).
- **World federalism:** an Ideology that turns cosmopolitan principle into a programme of
  world government (_Sovereignty vs. pooled governance_).
- **Liberal internationalism:** promotes cooperation and institutions among states, which
  still put their citizens first (_Compatriot priority vs. global impartiality_).
- **Effective altruism:** shares impartial concern, and adds a method of maximising
  impact (Reasons).

**Sources.** Diogenes Laërtius, _Lives of the Eminent Philosophers_ VI (on Diogenes the
Cynic); Immanuel Kant, "Toward Perpetual Peace" (1795); Peter Singer, "Famine,
Affluence, and Morality" (1972); Charles Beitz, _Political Theory and International
Relations_ (1979); Martha Nussbaum, "Patriotism and Cosmopolitanism" (1994); Thomas
Pogge, _World Poverty and Human Rights_ (2002); Kwame Anthony Appiah, _Cosmopolitanism_
(2006); Pauline Kleingeld & Eric Brown, "Cosmopolitanism", _Stanford Encyclopedia of
Philosophy_.

## Sovereigntism

- **Type:** Thin
- **Parent:** —
- **Also called:** souverainisme; Euroscepticism (its European form)
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                                         | Range                                                                    | Central |
| ------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------- |
| Sovereignty vs. pooled governance                 | Leans _Sovereign self-government_ – Strongly _Sovereign self-government_ | Yes     |
| Popular sovereignty vs. constitutional constraint | Middle – Strongly _Popular and legislative supremacy_                    | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Democracy lives in the nation, where people share a language, a
public debate, and a sense that the government is theirs. When power moves to
supranational bodies, courts, and commissions far from voters, citizens lose the
ability to change the laws they live under. Nations should cooperate, trade, and make
treaties, but final authority over their laws, money, and borders must stay with their
own people and parliaments.

**Key concepts.**

- **Sovereignty:** a people's final authority over its own laws.
- **Democratic accountability:** those who make the law must answer to the voters who
  live under it.
- **Europe of nations:** cooperation among sovereign states rather than a federal union
  (de Gaulle).
- **Take back control:** returning powers from supranational bodies to national
  parliaments.

**Signature reasons.**

- "Laws should be made by people we can vote out."
- "Cooperation, yes; surrender of sovereignty, no."
- "We didn't vote for Brussels to run our country."
- "Take back control."

**Telling it apart.**

- **European federalism:** wants to pool sovereignty in a federal Europe (_Sovereignty vs.
  pooled governance_, the central Dimension).
- **Nationalism:** loyalty to the nation as such; sovereigntism is specifically about
  supranational authority and can be held on democratic grounds alone (Reasons).
- **Gaullism:** an Ideology with sovereigntist foreign policy plus a strong executive and
  state-led modernisation (_Leadership_).

**Sources.** Charles de Gaulle, press conferences on Europe (1960–65); Margaret
Thatcher, speech at the College of Europe, Bruges (1988); Philippe Séguin, speech to the
National Assembly against the Maastricht Treaty (5 May 1992); Jeremy Rabkin, _Law
Without Nations?_ (2005); Paul Taggart & Aleks Szczerbiak (eds.), _Opposing Europe?_
(2008); Richard Tuck, _The Left Case for Brexit_ (2020).

## European federalism

- **Type:** Thin
- **Parent:** —
- **Also called:** Euro-federalism; the European federalist movement
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                         | Range                                                                | Central |
| --------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------- |
| Sovereignty vs. pooled governance | Strongly _Pooled, rule-bound international governance_               | Yes     |
| Basis of nationhood               | Strongly _Shared civic principles_ – Leans _Shared civic principles_ | No      |
| Foreign-policy aims               | Strongly _Universal values_ – Leans _Universal values_               | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** The nations of Europe tore each other apart twice in a generation,
and each is now too small to shape a world of continental powers alone. Only a federal
union, with a common government accountable to a European parliament and citizens,
can secure peace, democracy, and a voice in the world. Sovereignty pooled is
sovereignty regained: problems such as trade, climate, migration, and security cross
borders, so democratic control must too.

**Key concepts.**

- **Federation:** a European government with real powers over common affairs, alongside
  national and regional ones.
- **Pooled sovereignty:** acting together gives Europeans control they cannot have alone.
- **Peace:** integration ends the wars between European nations.
- **European citizenship:** Europeans as one democratic public.

**Signature reasons.**

- "Europe will be made through concrete achievements." (Schuman)
- "Nationalism means war."
- "Alone, each European country is too small to matter."
- "Europe needs a government, not just a market."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Sovereigntism:** defends national self-government against supranational integration
  (_Sovereignty vs. pooled governance_, the central Dimension).
- **World federalism:** an Ideology seeking federal government for the whole world, not
  one continent (Reasons).
- **Liberal internationalism:** seeks cooperation through institutions among sovereign
  states rather than a federal union (Reasons).

**Sources.** Altiero Spinelli & Ernesto Rossi, _Ventotene Manifesto_ (1941); Schuman
Declaration (9 May 1950); Jean Monnet, _Memoirs_ (1976); Guy Verhofstadt, _The United
States of Europe_ (2006); Jürgen Habermas, _The Crisis of the European Union_ (2011;
trans. 2012).

## Pacifism

- **Type:** Thin
- **Parent:** —
- **Also called:** nonviolence; anti-war politics (its contingent form)
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                      | Range                                                              | Central |
| ------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------- |
| Use of force                   | Strongly _Reluctance to use force_                                 | Yes     |
| Force in domestic politics     | Leans _Principled nonviolence_ – Strongly _Principled nonviolence_ | No      |
| Citizenship: rights vs. duties | Middle – Strongly _Citizenship as protected liberty_               | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** War is the organised killing of human beings, and it is wrong.
It kills the innocent along with the guilty, breeds the hatreds that cause the next war,
and corrupts the societies that wage it. Nonviolent resistance, diplomacy, and the
refusal to fight are stronger than they look. Absolute pacifists hold that no
violence is ever right; contingent pacifists hold that no modern war can meet the
standards that would justify it. Either way, the duty is to refuse.

**Key concepts.**

- **Nonviolence:** resisting injustice without inflicting harm, as a principle and a
  method.
- **Conscientious objection:** the right to refuse military service on grounds of
  conscience.
- **Cycle of violence:** violence begets violence, so it cannot bring lasting peace.
- **Absolute vs. contingent:** rejecting all violence, or rejecting war in practice
  because it can never be justified as fought.

**Signature reasons.**

- "War is a crime against humanity."
- "An eye for an eye makes the whole world blind."
- "There is no way to peace; peace is the way."
- "I will not kill for any government."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Restraint:** an Ideology that avoids foreign wars for reasons of national interest,
  not because war is wrong (Reasons).
- **Christian anarchism:** grounds nonviolence in the Gospel and adds the rejection of the
  state (_Political authority_, _Transcendence_).
- **Gandhism:** an Ideology joining nonviolence to self-rule, village self-reliance, and
  trusteeship (Reasons).
- **Anabaptist political witness:** a religious community's refusal of the sword
  (_Transcendence_; Reasons).

**Sources.** Leo Tolstoy, _The Kingdom of God Is Within You_ (1894); Jane Addams, _Newer
Ideals of Peace_ (1907); Mohandas K. Gandhi, _Hind Swaraj_ (1909); Peace Pledge Union
(founded 1934); John Howard Yoder, _The Politics of Jesus_ (1972); Jenny Teichman,
_Pacifism and the Just War_ (1986); Robert L. Holmes, _On War and Morality_ (1989);
Andrew Fiala, "Pacifism", _Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy_.

## Anti-imperialism

- **Type:** Thin
- **Parent:** —
- **Also called:** anti-colonialism; critics: "campism" (for the strand that backs any
  rival of the West)
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                         | Range                                         | Central |
| --------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------- | ------- |
| Sovereignty vs. pooled governance | Middle – Strongly _Sovereign self-government_ | Yes     |
| Use of force                      | Middle – Strongly _Reluctance to use force_   | No      |
| Historical injustice              | Middle – Strongly _Rectification_             | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Powerful states have long dominated weaker peoples: conquering
them, extracting their wealth, overthrowing their governments, and dictating their
economies through debt, sanctions, and military bases. That domination continues long
after formal empires ended. Every people has the right to determine its own future, and
the wars, coups, and economic pressure of imperial powers, whatever ideals they claim,
should be opposed.

**Key concepts.**

- **Imperialism:** domination of one people by another, by conquest, by economic power,
  or by control of their politics.
- **Neo-colonialism:** formal independence under continued foreign economic and political
  control (Nkrumah).
- **Self-determination:** each people's right to choose its own government and path.
- **Blowback:** imperial intervention breeds the resistance and instability it claims to
  fight.

**Signature reasons.**

- "No war for empire."
- "Every people has the right to decide its own future."
- "They talk about democracy and arrive with bombs and bankers."
- "Colonialism didn't end. It changed shape."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Postcolonialism:** an Ideology that adds the critique of culture and knowledge
  (Reasons).
- **Restraint:** opposes foreign wars for the sake of the nation's own interests, rather
  than the dominated peoples' rights (Reasons).
- **Non-alignment:** a foreign-policy stance of newly independent states staying out of
  blocs (Reasons).
- **Pan-Africanism:** anti-imperialist, but defined by the unity of one people (centered
  group).

**Sources.** J. A. Hobson, _Imperialism: A Study_ (1902); Anti-Imperialist League,
Platform (1899); V. I. Lenin, _Imperialism, the Highest Stage of Capitalism_ (1917);
Final Communiqué of the Asian-African Conference, Bandung (1955); Kwame Nkrumah,
_Neo-Colonialism: The Last Stage of Imperialism_ (1965); Edward W. Said, _Culture and
Imperialism_ (1993); Noam Chomsky, _Hegemony or Survival_ (2003).

## Monarchism

- **Type:** Thin
- **Parent:** —
- **Also called:** royalism; constitutional monarchism (its majority form today);
  legitimism (support for a particular dynasty's claim)
- **Variants:** Divine-right absolutism
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension           | Range                                                              | Central |
| ------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------- |
| Tradition or reason | Strongly _Inherited practice carries wisdom_ – Middle              | No      |
| Social hierarchy    | Leans _Equality of status_ – Strongly _Ordered rank and deference_ | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** A hereditary monarch stands above party and faction as a living
symbol of the nation's continuity, binding the present to its history. Because the
crown is inherited rather than won, its holder owes nothing to any interest and can
belong equally to everyone. In a constitutional monarchy this keeps the highest office
out of the hands of politicians. For those who favour a ruling monarch, a king raised
for the role and responsible for a realm his heirs will inherit governs with a longer
view than any elected leader.

**Key concepts.**

- **Continuity:** the crown carries the nation's history and identity across
  generations.
- **Above politics:** a head of state who owes nothing to party or election can unite
  rather than divide.
- **Dignified and efficient parts:** the monarchy commands loyalty and legitimacy while
  elected government does the work (Bagehot).
- **Legitimacy:** for some, the rightful claim of a dynasty; for divine-right absolutists,
  authority given by God.

**Signature reasons.**

- "The crown belongs to everyone because no one voted for it."
- "A monarch keeps the top job out of politicians' hands."
- "It ties us to a thousand years of history."
- "A ruler who will pass the kingdom to his children governs for the long term."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Counter-revolutionary traditionalism:** an Ideology that restores throne and altar
  together against the Revolution's principles (_Religion and state_; Reasons).
- **Neoreaction:** favours concentrated sovereign rule, but on a CEO model, not
  hereditary continuity (Reasons).
- **Republicanism:** rejects hereditary office in favour of a self-governing citizenry
  (_Social hierarchy_; Reasons).

**Sources.** Robert Filmer, _Patriarcha_ (1680); Jacques-Bénigne Bossuet, _Politics
Drawn from the Very Words of Holy Scripture_ (1709); Joseph de Maistre, _Considerations
on France_ (1797); Walter Bagehot, _The English Constitution_ (1867); Vernon Bogdanor,
_The Monarchy and the Constitution_ (1995); Hans-Hermann Hoppe, _Democracy: The God That
Failed_ (2001).

## Agrarianism

- **Type:** Thin
- **Parent:** —
- **Also called:** the agrarian tradition; ruralism; the Southern Agrarians (US,
  historical); new agrarianism
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                        | Range                                                                                        | Central |
| -------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------- |
| Economic scale and concentration | Leans _Dispersed ownership and small scale_ – Strongly _Dispersed ownership and small scale_ | Yes     |
| Work                             | Strongly _Work is a source of worth_ – Leans _Work is a source of worth_                     | No      |
| Local vs. central                | Strongly _Local and regional self-rule_ – Leans _Local and regional self-rule_               | No      |
| Tradition or reason              | Strongly _Inherited practice carries wisdom_ – Leans _Inherited practice carries wisdom_     | No      |
| Trade and economic openness      | Middle – Strongly _Economic protection and self-reliance_                                    | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Working the land teaches the virtues a free society depends on:
patience, thrift, responsibility, independence, and care for a place over generations.
Farm families and rural communities are the moral backbone of a nation, not a leftover
from the past. Industrial agriculture, urban drift, and global markets are emptying the
countryside and turning farmers into the hired hands of agribusiness. Policy should
protect the family farm and the rural way of life.

**Key concepts.**

- **The yeoman:** the independent farmer who owns and works his land, as the model
  citizen (Jefferson).
- **Husbandry:** care for land, animals, and place across generations (Berry).
- **Rootedness:** belonging to a place and its community, against mobility and
  abstraction.
- **Independence:** owning the means of one's livelihood frees a person from dependence
  on employers and the state.

**Signature reasons.**

- "Those who labour in the earth are the chosen people of God." (Jefferson)
- "The family farm is the backbone of the nation."
- "When the farms die, the towns die with them."
- "A country that can't feed itself isn't free."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Agrarian populism:** turns rural grievance into politics against bankers and
  monopolies (Reasons).
- **Distributism:** wants widely held property in all sectors, grounded in Catholic
  social teaching (Reasons).
- **Eco-conservatism:** centres the stewardship of land and landscape generally, not
  farming life (_Environmental protection_; Reasons).

**Sources.** Hesiod, _Works and Days_; Thomas Jefferson, _Notes on the State of Virginia_
(1785), Query XIX; Twelve Southerners, _I'll Take My Stand_ (1930); Wendell Berry, _The
Unsettling of America_ (1977); Victor Davis Hanson, _Fields Without Dreams_ (1996);
Allan Carlson, _The New Agrarian Mind_ (2000); Paul B. Thompson, _The Agrarian Vision_
(2010).

## Animal rights

- **Type:** Thin
- **Parent:** —
- **Also called:** animal liberation; animal advocacy; animal protection (including its
  reformist welfare strand)
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension               | Range                                                                                                                    | Central |
| ----------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------- |
| Animals                 | Strongly _Legal protection of animal interests_ – Leans _Legal protection of animal interests_                           | Yes     |
| Grounds of moral status | Leans _What matters is the life's quality and capacities_ – Strongly _What matters is the life's quality and capacities_ | No      |
| Moral circle            | Leans _Everyone counts equally_ – Strongly _Everyone counts equally_                                                     | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Animals are not things. Pigs, cows, chickens, and fish can suffer,
and many have lives that matter to them. The capacity to suffer, not species, is what
gives a being a claim on us. Billions are confined, mutilated, and killed every year for
food, clothing, and research that we could largely do without. Law should protect them,
and for many advocates, humans should stop using animals as resources altogether.

**Key concepts.**

- **Sentience:** the capacity to feel pain and pleasure, which grounds moral standing
  (Bentham, Singer).
- **Speciesism:** giving less weight to a being's interests simply because it is not
  human (Singer).
- **Subject-of-a-life:** animals with beliefs, desires, and a welfare of their own have
  inherent value and rights (Regan).
- **Abolition vs. welfare:** ending animal use altogether, or reducing suffering within
  it (Francione).

**Signature reasons.**

- "The question is not, Can they reason? nor, Can they talk? but, Can they suffer?"
  (Bentham)
- "Animals are not ours to eat, wear, or experiment on."
- "Factory farming is one of the great moral crimes of our time."
- "Speciesism is prejudice, like racism or sexism."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Deep ecology:** values species and ecosystems as wholes, and can accept killing
  individual animals for them; animal rights protects individuals (Reasons).
- **Effective altruism:** shares impartial concern for animals, within a wider method of
  doing the most good (Reasons).
- **Ecofeminism:** links the domination of animals to the domination of women and
  nature (Reasons).

**Sources.** Jeremy Bentham, _An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and
Legislation_ (1789), ch. 17; Peter Singer, _Animal Liberation_ (1975); Tom Regan, _The
Case for Animal Rights_ (1983); Gary L. Francione, _Introduction to Animal Rights_
(2000); Sue Donaldson & Will Kymlicka, _Zoopolis_ (2011); Christine M. Korsgaard,
_Fellow Creatures_ (2018); Martha C. Nussbaum, _Justice for Animals_ (2023).

## Alter-globalization

- **Type:** Thin
- **Parent:** —
- **Also called:** the global justice movement; altermondialisme; critics: the
  "anti-globalization movement" (a name participants reject)
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                                   | Range                                                                                      | Central |
| ------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------- |
| Moral limits of markets                     | Strongly _Protected non-market spheres_ – Leans _Protected non-market spheres_             | Yes     |
| Compatriot priority vs. global impartiality | Leans _Impartial concern for all people_ – Strongly _Impartial concern for all people_     | Yes     |
| Distribution                                | Leans _Egalitarian redistribution_ – Strongly _Egalitarian redistribution_                 | No      |
| Trade and economic openness                 | Middle – Strongly _Economic protection and self-reliance_                                  | No      |
| Representation vs. participation            | Strongly _Direct and participatory self-rule_ – Leans _Direct and participatory self-rule_ | No      |
| Channels of change                          | Middle – Strongly _Beyond institutions_                                                    | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Globalisation is not the problem. The problem is the form it
has taken, written by and for multinational corporations and finance, through trade
deals, structural adjustment, and institutions that no one elected. Water, seeds,
health, and public services are being turned into commodities, while workers and the
environment race to the bottom. Another world is possible: a globalisation of
solidarity, rights, and democracy from below, connecting movements across borders.

**Key concepts.**

- **Corporate globalisation:** integration governed by trade and investment rules that
  favour capital over people.
- **Commons:** goods such as water, seeds, and knowledge that should not be privatised.
- **Global solidarity:** movements of workers, peasants, and citizens linked across
  borders.
- **Democracy from below:** open, horizontal forums rather than closed summits.

**Signature reasons.**

- "Another world is possible."
- "The world is not for sale."
- "People and the planet before profit."
- "Fair trade, not free trade."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Sovereigntism:** opposes supranational power in favour of the nation; alter-globalists
  seek global solidarity and justice (_Compatriot priority vs. global impartiality_,
  the central Dimension).
- **Anti-imperialism:** opposes domination by imperial states; alter-globalization
  targets corporate power and its rules (Reasons).
- **Cosmopolitanism:** shares impartial concern, but need not oppose markets or corporate
  globalisation (_Moral limits of markets_).
- **Degrowth:** an Ideology that targets material throughput; many alter-globalists share
  it, but their focus is justice and democracy (Reasons).

**Sources.** ATTAC, founding platform (1998); Naomi Klein, _No Logo_ (1999); José Bové
& François Dufour, _The World Is Not for Sale_ (2001); World Social Forum, Charter of
Principles (2001); Susan George, _Another World Is Possible If…_ (2004); Donatella della
Porta (ed.), _The Global Justice Movement_ (2007).

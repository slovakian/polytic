# Democratic theory and governance: Label cards

Cards for this family, written to the template in `../label-library.md`, in the order
of the list. Every card here is written with the same dispassion as any other: the
self-description is the tradition's own self-understanding, steelmanned, and never
Polytic's verdict (`../principles.md`). Most of these are models of democracy and are
usually an Area Label: they describe how a person thinks collective decisions should be
made, alongside a Main Label.

## Deliberative democracy

- **Type:** Ideology (usually Area)
- **Parent:** Liberalism / Republicanism
- **Also called:** discursive democracy (Dryzek); the deliberative turn; deliberative
  polling (Fishkin's method)
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                        | Range                                                                                | Central |
| -------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------- |
| Majoritarian vs. consensus       | Middle – Strongly _Power-sharing and broad consensus_                                | Yes     |
| Representation vs. participation | Leans _Direct and participatory self-rule_ – Middle                                  | No      |
| Pluralism vs. unity              | Leans _Legitimate pluralism and opposition_ – Middle                                 | No      |
| Popular judgment vs. expertise   | Middle – Leans _Equal popular judgment_                                              | No      |
| Tradition or reason              | Leans _Reason should audit inheritance_ – Strongly _Reason should audit inheritance_ | No      |
| Hierarchy or equality            | Leans _Equality is basic_ – Strongly _Equality is basic_                             | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Counting votes tells you what people want, not whether a decision
is justified. A law is legitimate when it could be defended with reasons that free and
equal citizens can accept, after they have heard each other out, weighed the evidence
and changed their minds where they should. Democracy should therefore be built around
good public reasoning: informed, inclusive and respectful discussion in parliaments,
media and citizens' assemblies, where the force of the better argument, not money or
numbers alone, carries the day.

**Key concepts.**

- **Legitimacy:** comes from public justification among equals, not only from the
  aggregation of preferences.
- **Deliberation:** reasoned exchange in which participants offer reasons others can
  accept and are open to persuasion.
- **Public reason:** arguments framed in terms all citizens could share, rather than in a
  single faith's or faction's doctrine.
- **Mini-publics:** randomly selected, informed citizens' panels that model what the
  public would think if it deliberated.
- **Preference change:** preferences are formed in discussion, not fixed inputs to be
  counted.

**Signature reasons.**

- "Democracy is more than counting heads. It's about giving reasons."
- "Ordinary citizens, given the facts and time to talk, make good decisions."
- "The point of debate is to find out who's right, not just who's more numerous."
- "A decision you can justify to those who lost is a legitimate one."
- "We need more citizens' assemblies and less shouting."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Agonistic democracy:** sees consensus as a dangerous illusion and wants conflict
  channelled between adversaries (_Majoritarian vs. consensus_, the central Dimension;
  _Pluralism vs. unity_).
- **Participatory democracy:** values participation itself, including at work;
  deliberative democrats care most about the quality of the reasoning, wherever it
  happens (_Representation vs. participation_; Reasons).
- **Direct democracy:** decides by popular vote, which deliberative democrats count as
  aggregation unless it follows real deliberation (Reasons).
- **Lottocracy:** makes selection by lot the source of binding power; deliberative
  democrats use random selection as one tool, often advisory (Reasons).
- **Minimalist democracy:** holds that citizens choose leaders, not policies, and cannot
  be expected to deliberate (_Representation vs. participation_).

**Sources.** Joshua Cohen, "Deliberation and Democratic Legitimacy" (1989); James
Fishkin, _Democracy and Deliberation_ (1991) and _When the People Speak_ (2009); Jürgen
Habermas, _Between Facts and Norms_ (1992; trans. 1996); Amy Gutmann & Dennis Thompson,
_Democracy and Disagreement_ (1996); John Rawls, "The Idea of Public Reason Revisited"
(1997); John Dryzek, _Deliberative Democracy and Beyond_ (2000); Hélène Landemore,
_Democratic Reason_ (2013); André Bächtiger et al. (eds.), _The Oxford Handbook of
Deliberative Democracy_ (2018).

## Participatory democracy

- **Type:** Ideology (usually Area)
- **Parent:** Democratic socialism / Republicanism
- **Also called:** strong democracy (Barber); grassroots democracy; workplace or
  industrial democracy (its economic arm)
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                        | Range                                                                                      | Central |
| -------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------- |
| Representation vs. participation | Strongly _Direct and participatory self-rule_ – Leans _Direct and participatory self-rule_ | Yes     |
| Workplace governance             | Strongly _Worker voice and self-management_ – Leans _Worker voice and self-management_     | No      |
| Local vs. central                | Strongly _Local and regional self-rule_ – Leans _Local and regional self-rule_             | No      |
| Popular judgment vs. expertise   | Leans _Equal popular judgment_ – Strongly _Equal popular judgment_                         | No      |
| Citizenship: rights vs. duties   | Leans _Citizenship as shared obligation_ – Middle                                          | No      |
| Human nature                     | Leans _Malleable and improvable_ – Strongly _Malleable and improvable_                     | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Voting every few years for someone else to decide is a thin kind
of self-government. People should take part directly in the decisions that shape their
lives, in neighbourhood assemblies, participatory budgets and the workplace, where most
adults spend their days under someone else's authority. Participation is also an
education: people who govern together become more capable, more public-spirited and
more confident citizens. A democratic society is one where democracy is practised
everywhere, not only at the ballot box.

**Key concepts.**

- **Participation:** the core of democracy, valuable both for better decisions and for
  what it makes of citizens (its educative function).
- **Workplace democracy:** authority at work should answer to the people who work there,
  since the firm is a site of government.
- **Decentralisation:** decisions made at the smallest level where people can actually
  take part.
- **Participatory budgeting:** residents directly deciding how part of a public budget is
  spent (Porto Alegre).
- **Strong democracy:** citizens governing themselves on public matters, rather than
  representatives governing in their name.

**Signature reasons.**

- "Democracy shouldn't happen once every four years."
- "The people affected by a decision should make it."
- "You learn democracy by doing it."
- "Why should we be citizens at the ballot box and subjects at work?"
- "Let the neighbourhood decide how its money is spent."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Direct democracy:** citizens vote on questions by initiative and referendum, often
  across a whole nation; participatory democracy wants ongoing involvement in
  assemblies, workplaces and local bodies (Reasons; _Local vs. central_).
- **Deliberative democracy:** cares most about the quality of reasoning, and can be
  satisfied by a small, randomly chosen panel (Reasons).
- **Anarchism and Libertarian socialism:** extend self-management to the abolition of
  the state; participatory democracy can live within a state (_Political authority_).
- **Republicanism:** prizes civic virtue and active citizenship within a mixed
  constitution, with representative institutions central (_Representation vs.
  participation_; Reasons).
- **Minimalist democracy:** holds that citizens should choose leaders, not policies
  (_Representation vs. participation_, the central Dimension).

**Sources.** Jean-Jacques Rousseau, _The Social Contract_ (1762); John Stuart Mill,
_Considerations on Representative Government_ (1861); G. D. H. Cole, _Guild Socialism
Restated_ (1920); Students for a Democratic Society, _The Port Huron Statement_ (1962);
Carole Pateman, _Participation and Democratic Theory_ (1970); C. B. Macpherson, _The
Life and Times of Liberal Democracy_ (1977); Benjamin Barber, _Strong Democracy_ (1984);
Archon Fung & Erik Olin Wright (eds.), _Deepening Democracy_ (2003); Gianpaolo Baiocchi,
_Militants and Citizens_ (2005).

## Agonistic democracy

- **Type:** Ideology (usually Area)
- **Parent:** —
- **Also called:** agonism; agonistic pluralism (Mouffe); radical democracy (the
  Laclau–Mouffe project)
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                  | Range                                                                      | Central |
| -------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------- |
| Pluralism vs. unity        | Strongly _Legitimate pluralism and opposition_                             | Yes     |
| One master value or many   | Leans _Plural, conflicting values_ – Strongly _Plural, conflicting values_ | Yes     |
| Majoritarian vs. consensus | Leans _Clear majority rule and accountability_ – Middle                    | No      |
| Moral objectivity          | Leans _Human-made morality_ – Strongly _Human-made morality_               | No      |
| Speech and expression      | Strongly _Maximal expressive freedom_ – Middle                             | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Conflict is not a failure of politics; it is what politics is.
Societies are divided by real disagreements about values and identities that no
rational procedure can dissolve, and pretending otherwise, as consensus-seeking
liberals do, pushes passions out of mainstream politics and into extremism. Democracy's
task is to turn enemies into adversaries: opponents who fight hard for real
alternatives but accept each other's right to exist and to win. Vigorous contest
between distinct choices keeps democracy alive.

**Key concepts.**

- **The political:** the dimension of antagonism, of "us" and "them", that is part of
  every society and can be tamed but never removed.
- **Adversary:** an opponent whose ideas you combat but whose right to defend them you
  do not question, as against an enemy to be destroyed.
- **Consensus:** always partial, a provisional hegemony that excludes something; a
  politics claiming full consensus hides that exclusion.
- **Hegemony:** every social order is the result of contingent political struggle, not
  rational necessity, and can be contested.
- **Passions:** collective identities and emotions are legitimate parts of democratic
  politics, not irrational noise.

**Signature reasons.**

- "There is no rational consensus waiting to be found. There are real conflicts."
- "When the parties all agree, the voters go looking for someone who doesn't."
- "Democracy needs real choices, not managers of a consensus."
- "Treat your opponents as adversaries, not enemies."
- "Every order is political, and every order can be challenged."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Deliberative democracy:** seeks reasoned agreement through public deliberation
  (_Majoritarian vs. consensus_; _One master value or many_).
- **Left populism:** Mouffe's later strategy builds a unified "people" against an
  oligarchy; agonism as a theory of democracy is about how all sides should contest,
  and does not require a populist frame (Reasons).
- **Minimalist democracy:** also sees democracy as competition, but between elites for
  votes, with citizens largely passive; agonists want passionate collective
  identities in the contest (Reasons).
- **Consociationalism:** manages deep divisions by elite power-sharing and mutual
  vetoes, which agonists would see as suppressing contest (_Majoritarian vs.
  consensus_).
- **Value pluralism:** the Foundation Label for plural, conflicting values that agonism
  builds on; it can be held without any view of democracy.

**Sources.** Ernesto Laclau & Chantal Mouffe, _Hegemony and Socialist Strategy_ (1985);
William Connolly, _Identity\Difference_ (1991) and _The Ethos of Pluralization_ (1995);
Bonnie Honig, _Political Theory and the Displacement of Politics_ (1993); James Tully,
_Strange Multiplicity_ (1995); Chantal Mouffe, _The Democratic Paradox_ (2000), _On the
Political_ (2005) and _Agonistics_ (2013); Mark Wenman, _Agonistic Democracy_ (2013).

## Direct democracy

- **Type:** Ideology (usually Area)
- **Parent:** —
- **Also called:** semi-direct democracy (the Swiss model); initiative and referendum;
  critics: "plebiscitary democracy" (for leader-called referendums)
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                                         | Range                                                                                      | Central |
| ------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------- |
| Representation vs. participation                  | Strongly _Direct and participatory self-rule_ – Leans _Direct and participatory self-rule_ | Yes     |
| Popular judgment vs. expertise                    | Leans _Equal popular judgment_ – Strongly _Equal popular judgment_                         | Yes     |
| Popular sovereignty vs. constitutional constraint | Middle – Strongly _Popular and legislative supremacy_                                      | No      |
| Local vs. central                                 | Leans _Local and regional self-rule_ – Middle                                              | No      |
| Expert trust                                      | Middle – Leans _Judge for yourself_                                                        | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** In a democracy the people are sovereign, so on the questions that
matter most they should decide themselves rather than hand the decision to politicians
who may have other interests. Citizens' initiatives let people put issues on the agenda
that the political class would rather avoid, and referendums let them overrule their
representatives. Switzerland shows that ordinary voters can handle this responsibly,
year after year, and that it makes government more accountable and citizens better
informed.

**Key concepts.**

- **Popular sovereignty:** the people's will is the final authority, and should be
  expressed directly where possible, not only through representatives.
- **Initiative:** citizens can propose laws or constitutional amendments by gathering
  signatures.
- **Referendum:** a popular vote that can approve or veto what the legislature passes.
- **Accountability:** the possibility of a popular veto keeps representatives closer to
  what citizens want.
- **Recall:** voters can remove an official before the end of a term.

**Signature reasons.**

- "Let the people decide."
- "Politicians shouldn't have the last word on everything."
- "The Swiss vote several times a year, and it works."
- "If enough citizens sign, the question should go on the ballot."
- "A people that can veto its government is a free people."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Participatory democracy:** ongoing involvement in assemblies and workplaces, more
  than votes on questions (Reasons; _Local vs. central_).
- **Deliberative democracy:** wary of referendums decided without real deliberation
  (Reasons).
- **Populism:** a Thin Label that sets a pure people against a corrupt elite; direct
  democracy is an institutional design, and many of its holders are not populists
  (Reasons).
- **Lottocracy:** gives power to a random sample of citizens, not to all voters
  (Reasons; _Representation vs. participation_).
- **Minimalist democracy:** the people choose leaders, not policies (_Representation vs.
  participation_, the central Dimension).

**Sources.** Jean-Jacques Rousseau, _The Social Contract_ (1762); Thomas Cronin, _Direct
Democracy: The Politics of Initiative, Referendum, and Recall_ (1989); Kris Kobach, _The
Referendum: Direct Democracy in Switzerland_ (1993); Wolf Linder, _Swiss Democracy_ (1994;
3rd ed. 2010); Ian Budge, _The New Challenge of Direct Democracy_ (1996); Matt Qvortrup,
_Direct Democracy: A Comparative Study of the Theory and Practice of Government by the
People_ (2013).

## Consociationalism

- **Type:** Ideology (usually Area)
- **Parent:** —
- **Also called:** consociational democracy; power-sharing; consensus democracy
  (Lijphart's broader model); _Proporzdemokratie_
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                  | Range                                                                                        | Central |
| -------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------- |
| Majoritarian vs. consensus | Strongly _Power-sharing and broad consensus_                                                 | Yes     |
| Group difference           | Strongly _Group-differentiated recognition_ – Leans _Group-differentiated recognition_       | Yes     |
| Pluralism vs. unity        | Strongly _Legitimate pluralism and opposition_ – Leans _Legitimate pluralism and opposition_ | No      |
| Local vs. central          | Leans _Local and regional self-rule_ – Middle                                                | No      |
| Individuals or wholes      | Leans _Groups and relations are real_ – Strongly _Groups and relations are real_             | No      |
| Tempo of change            | Leans _Incremental reform_ – Strongly _Incremental reform_                                   | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** In a society divided by religion, language or nationality,
winner-takes-all majority rule means one community permanently governs the others, and
that is a recipe for conflict. Stable democracy in such places, from the Netherlands and
Switzerland to Northern Ireland and Lebanon, comes from sharing power: grand coalitions
of the main communities' leaders, proportional shares of offices and funds, autonomy for
each community over its own affairs, and a veto on matters vital to it. People can
cooperate across deep divides if no group has to fear being outvoted on its survival.

**Key concepts.**

- **Grand coalition:** government by leaders of all the main segments together.
- **Proportionality:** offices, seats and public funds shared in proportion to each
  segment's size.
- **Segmental autonomy:** each community runs its own schools, culture and personal law.
- **Mutual veto:** each segment can block decisions that threaten its vital interests.
- **Elite accommodation:** stability rests on leaders who cooperate at the top while their
  communities stay distinct.

**Signature reasons.**

- "In a divided society, majority rule can mean permanent minority rule."
- "No community should have to fear being outvoted on its own survival."
- "Share the power, and you share the stake in peace."
- "Let each community run its own schools and culture."
- "Peace in Northern Ireland came from power-sharing, not from one side winning."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Neo-corporatism:** shares power among organised economic interests, not among
  religious, ethnic or linguistic segments (_Group difference_; Reasons).
- **Multicultural liberalism:** grounds group-differentiated rights in individual
  autonomy and cultural membership within a liberal state; consociationalism is a design
  for governing through segment leaders (Reasons; _Majoritarian vs. consensus_).
- **Minimalist democracy:** majoritarian competition with clear accountability
  (_Majoritarian vs. consensus_, the central Dimension).
- **Agonistic democracy:** wants open contest between adversaries rather than elite
  accommodation (_Majoritarian vs. consensus_).
- **Multiculturalism:** a Thin Label for recognising cultural diversity, which can ride
  on many hosts without consociational institutions.

**Sources.** Gerhard Lehmbruch, _Proporzdemokratie_ (1967); Arend Lijphart, _The Politics
of Accommodation_ (1968), "Consociational Democracy" (1969), _Democracy in Plural
Societies_ (1977) and _Patterns of Democracy_ (1999; 2nd ed. 2012); Kenneth McRae (ed.),
_Consociational Democracy_ (1974); John McGarry & Brendan O'Leary, _The Northern Ireland
Conflict: Consociational Engagements_ (2004). Its main rival school is Donald Horowitz,
_Ethnic Groups in Conflict_ (1985).

## Epistocracy

- **Type:** Ideology (usually Area)
- **Parent:** —
- **Also called:** rule of the knowledgeable; knowledge-weighted democracy; plural voting
  (Mill's proposal); critics: "elitism"
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                        | Range                                                                                      | Central |
| -------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------- |
| Popular judgment vs. expertise   | Strongly _Rule by the knowledgeable_ – Leans _Rule by the knowledgeable_                   | Yes     |
| Representation vs. participation | Leans _Representative, trustee government_ – Strongly _Representative, trustee government_ | No      |
| Outcomes or rules                | Strongly _Best outcomes_ – Leans _Best outcomes_                                           | No      |
| Citizenship: rights vs. duties   | Leans _Citizenship as protected liberty_ – Strongly _Citizenship as protected liberty_     | No      |
| Grounds for belief               | Strongly _Belief proportioned to evidence_ – Leans _Belief proportioned to evidence_       | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Political decisions are exercises of power over everyone, and we
have a right not to have that power exercised incompetently. Yet most voters, for
understandable reasons, know little about policy, and their mistakes fall on all of us.
Democracy is a tool, valued for its results, not a sacred right to equal political
power. If some form of government that gives more weight to informed judgment, through
voter qualification exams, plural votes or knowledge-weighted ballots, would decide
better, we should be willing to try it.

**Key concepts.**

- **Competence principle:** people have a right that high-stakes decisions affecting
  them be made competently and in good faith (Brennan).
- **Political ignorance:** most voters are rationally ignorant or biased about policy,
  so equal votes aggregate error.
- **Instrumentalism:** democracy is justified by its outcomes, not by the intrinsic
  value of equal political power.
- **Knowledge weighting:** votes or eligibility weighted by demonstrated political
  knowledge, or a knowledge-based veto.
- **Equal worth:** moral equality does not require equal political power, any more than
  equal worth requires everyone to be allowed to perform surgery.

**Signature reasons.**

- "We wouldn't let an ignorant jury decide a case. Why an ignorant electorate?"
- "Democracy is a tool, not a sacrament."
- "Equal moral worth doesn't mean equal say over everyone else."
- "Most voters don't know the basic facts, and we all pay for it."
- "If a different system made better decisions, we should be willing to use it."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Technocracy:** hands government itself to technical and scientific experts;
  epistocracy weights who chooses, through the franchise or a knowledge-based veto
  (_Expert trust_; _Design vs. evolved order_; Reasons).
- **Political Confucianism:** also favours rule by the capable, but by moral cultivation
  and virtue within a Confucian order, not by measured political knowledge (_Social
  hierarchy_; Reasons).
- **Minimalist democracy:** also expects little of voters, but keeps one person, one vote
  (_Popular judgment vs. expertise_, the central Dimension).
- **Lottocracy:** the opposite answer to voter ignorance: random ordinary citizens,
  well informed, rather than the most knowledgeable (_Popular judgment vs. expertise_).

**Sources.** Plato, _Republic_; John Stuart Mill, _Considerations on Representative
Government_ (1861), ch. 8; David Estlund, "Why Not Epistocracy?" (2003) and _Democratic
Authority_ (2008) (a critic who named the position); Bryan Caplan, _The Myth of the
Rational Voter_ (2007); Ilya Somin, _Democracy and Political Ignorance_ (2013); Jason
Brennan, _The Ethics of Voting_ (2011) and _Against Democracy_ (2016).

## Lottocracy

- **Type:** Ideology (usually Area)
- **Parent:** —
- **Also called:** sortition; demarchy (Burnheim); aleatory democracy; open democracy
  (Landemore, a close neighbour)
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                        | Range                                                              | Central |
| -------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------- |
| Representation vs. participation | Strongly _Direct and participatory self-rule_ – Middle             | Yes     |
| Popular judgment vs. expertise   | Leans _Equal popular judgment_ – Strongly _Equal popular judgment_ | Yes     |
| Social hierarchy                 | Strongly _Equality of status_ – Leans _Equality of status_         | No      |
| Citizenship: rights vs. duties   | Leans _Citizenship as shared obligation_ – Middle                  | No      |
| Majoritarian vs. consensus       | Middle – Leans _Power-sharing and broad consensus_                 | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Elections were long understood as aristocratic: they select the
rich, the well connected and the ambitious, and they bind representatives to donors and
parties. Athens chose most officials by lot, and juries still do. A legislature or
assembly of randomly selected citizens, given time, information and expert testimony,
would look like the people it serves, be free of campaign money and party machines, and
decide with the good sense ordinary people show when trusted with real power. Anyone
can govern, so everyone should have an equal chance to.

**Key concepts.**

- **Sortition:** selection by lot, giving every citizen an equal chance to hold office.
- **Descriptive representation:** a random sample mirrors the population in a way elected
  bodies never do.
- **Elections as aristocratic:** election selects distinguished people and invites
  capture by money and parties (Manin).
- **Cognitive diversity:** a diverse group of ordinary people can outperform a group of
  like-minded experts.
- **Rotation:** short terms and regular renewal so no one becomes a professional
  politician.

**Signature reasons.**

- "Elections pick politicians. Lotteries pick citizens."
- "If juries can decide guilt, citizens' assemblies can decide policy."
- "A random sample of people looks like the country. Parliament doesn't."
- "Nobody can buy a lottery."
- "Athens used lots; it's elections that are the modern experiment."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Deliberative democracy:** uses randomly chosen mini-publics, mostly as advisory
  bodies; lottocracy makes lot the source of binding authority (Reasons).
- **Direct democracy:** gives the decision to every voter, not to a random sample
  (Reasons).
- **Epistocracy:** gives more power to the most knowledgeable, the opposite answer to
  voter ignorance (_Popular judgment vs. expertise_, a central Dimension).
- **Minimalist democracy:** holds competitive election of leaders to be the essence of
  democracy (_Representation vs. participation_).

**Sources.** Aristotle, _Politics_ IV.9; John Burnheim, _Is Democracy Possible?_ (1985);
Ernest Callenbach & Michael Phillips, _A Citizen Legislature_ (1985); Bernard Manin, _The
Principles of Representative Government_ (1997); Oliver Dowlen, _The Political Potential
of Sortition_ (2008); Alexander Guerrero, "Against Elections: The Lottocratic
Alternative" (2014) and _Lottocracy_ (2024); David Van Reybrouck, _Against Elections_
(2013; trans. 2016); Hélène Landemore, _Open Democracy_ (2020).

## Technocracy

- **Type:** Ideology
- **Parent:** —
- **Also called:** government by experts; evidence-based governance (its milder current
  form); the technocracy movement (1930s North America)
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                        | Range                                                                                      | Central |
| -------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------- |
| Popular judgment vs. expertise   | Strongly _Rule by the knowledgeable_                                                       | Yes     |
| Expert trust                     | Strongly _Defer to expert institutions_ – Leans _Defer to expert institutions_             | Yes     |
| Design vs. evolved order         | Leans _Deliberate design_ – Strongly _Deliberate design_                                   | Yes     |
| Economic coordination            | Strongly _Public direction_ – Middle                                                       | No      |
| Grounds for belief               | Strongly _Belief proportioned to evidence_                                                 | No      |
| Tradition or reason              | Leans _Reason should audit inheritance_ – Strongly _Reason should audit inheritance_       | No      |
| Pluralism vs. unity              | Middle – Leans _Unified common purpose_                                                    | No      |
| Representation vs. participation | Leans _Representative, trustee government_ – Strongly _Representative, trustee government_ | No      |
| Direction of history             | Strongly _History is progressing_ – Leans _History is progressing_                         | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Most public problems, from energy and health to infrastructure and
the economy, are technical problems with better and worse answers. They should be
solved by people who understand them, using science, data and rational planning, not by
partisan bargaining, slogans and the electoral cycle. Government by competent experts,
judged by results, can deliver more prosperity and less waste than politics as usual.
The question is not left or right, but what works.

**Key concepts.**

- **Expertise:** specialised, tested knowledge is the right basis for public decisions in
  its domain.
- **Rational planning:** set goals, model options and choose by evidence, rather than by
  the pressure of interests.
- **Depoliticisation:** remove technical decisions from partisan politics, as with
  independent central banks and regulators.
- **Efficiency:** waste and dysfunction are failures of method, which better design can
  fix.
- **Problem-solving:** politics as the solving of shared problems, not as a contest of
  interests or ideologies.

**Signature reasons.**

- "Let the people who understand the problem solve it."
- "It's not left or right. It's what works."
- "We don't vote on bridge designs. Why vote on monetary policy?"
- "Follow the evidence, not the polls."
- "Most political fights are about problems engineers could solve."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Epistocracy:** weights the franchise or the choice of rulers by knowledge, rather than
  putting experts in charge of government (_Expert trust_; _Design vs. evolved order_;
  Reasons).
- **Developmental state:** an economic strategy run by an expert bureaucracy, which can
  sit within an electoral democracy; technocracy is a claim about government as a whole
  (Reasons).
- **Political Confucianism:** rule by the morally cultivated, not by technical experts
  (Reasons; _Social hierarchy_).
- **Libertarian paternalism:** experts design defaults and choice architecture but leave
  the final choice with the person (_Paternalism_).
- **Minimalist democracy:** leaves governing to elected elites who compete for votes;
  technocracy distrusts that competition (_Pluralism vs. unity_; Reasons).

**Sources.** Henri de Saint-Simon, _Du système industriel_ (1821); Thorstein Veblen, _The
Engineers and the Price System_ (1921); Howard Scott & M. King Hubbert et al.,
_Technocracy Study Course_ (1934); Jean Meynaud, _Technocracy_ (1964; trans. 1968);
Daniel Bell, _The Coming of Post-Industrial Society_ (1973); William Akin, _Technocracy
and the American Dream_ (1977); Frank Fischer, _Technocracy and the Politics of
Expertise_ (1990); Parag Khanna, _Technocracy in America_ (2017).

| Pluralism vs. unity              | Strongly _Legitimate pluralism and opposition_ – Leans _Legitimate pluralism and opposition_ | Yes     |
| Majoritarian vs. consensus       | Strongly _Clear majority rule and accountability_ – Middle                                   | No      |
| Popular judgment vs. expertise   | Leans _Rule by the knowledgeable_ – Middle                                                   | No      |
| Citizenship: rights vs. duties   | Leans _Citizenship as protected liberty_ – Strongly _Citizenship as protected liberty_       | No      |
| Channels of change               | Strongly _Within institutions_                                                               | No      |
| Human nature                     | Leans _Fixed and flawed_ – Middle                                                            | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Democracy is not the people ruling themselves; it is the people
choosing who rules them, and being able to throw them out. That is a modest ideal, but a
precious one: it lets a society change its government without bloodshed and keeps
rulers afraid of losing office. Expecting ordinary citizens to master policy or to
deliberate constantly is unrealistic and unnecessary. What matters is free, competitive
elections between rival teams of leaders, and the civil liberties that keep the
competition open.

**Key concepts.**

- **Democracy:** an institutional method in which leaders acquire the power to decide by
  competing for the people's vote (Schumpeter).
- **Removability:** the essential test is whether a government can be voted out
  peacefully (Popper, Przeworski).
- **Competition:** rival parties and leaders, not popular deliberation, are what keep
  power accountable.
- **Realistic citizens:** voters have limited time and information and judge governments
  mainly by results.
- **Division of labour:** citizens choose; elected leaders govern between elections.

**Signature reasons.**

- "Democracy means you can fire the government without a revolution."
- "Voters choose leaders, not policies."
- "Most people have better things to do than study policy, and that's fine."
- "Competition between parties keeps them honest."
- "Don't ask more of democracy than it can deliver."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Deliberative democracy and Participatory democracy:** want citizens to reason
  together or take part directly (_Representation vs. participation_, the central
  Dimension).
- **Epistocracy:** shares the low view of voter knowledge but would weight votes by
  knowledge; minimalists keep equal votes (_Popular judgment vs. expertise_).
- **Agonistic democracy:** also sees democracy as contest, but wants passionate
  collective identities in it, not elite competition before a passive public (Reasons).
- **Technocracy:** would take decisions out of electoral competition altogether
  (_Pluralism vs. unity_).
- **Liberalism:** its parent; minimalist democracy is a liberal account of what
  elections are for, not of rights as a whole.

**Sources.** Joseph Schumpeter, _Capitalism, Socialism and Democracy_ (1942), chs. 21–22;
Karl Popper, _The Open Society and Its Enemies_ (1945); Anthony Downs, _An Economic Theory
of Democracy_ (1957); William Riker, _Liberalism against Populism_ (1982); Adam
Przeworski, "Minimalist Conception of Democracy: A Defense" (1999); Christopher Achen &
Larry Bartels, _Democracy for Realists_ (2016).

## Libertarian paternalism

- **Type:** Ideology (usually Area)
- **Parent:** Liberalism
- **Also called:** nudge theory; behavioural public policy; soft paternalism; choice
  architecture; critics: "the nanny state" (some)
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                 | Range                                                                                                                        | Central |
| ------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------- |
| Paternalism               | Leans _Protection_ – Middle                                                                                                  | Yes     |
| What makes a life go well | Strongly _It depends on the person's own experience and wants_ – Leans _It depends on the person's own experience and wants_ | No      |
| Legal moralism            | Strongly _Liberty of self-regarding conduct_ – Leans _Liberty of self-regarding conduct_                                     | No      |
| Expert trust              | Leans _Defer to expert institutions_ – Middle                                                                                | No      |
| Grounds for belief        | Strongly _Belief proportioned to evidence_ – Leans _Belief proportioned to evidence_                                         | No      |
| Design vs. evolved order  | Leans _Deliberate design_ – Strongly _Deliberate design_                                                                     | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** People make predictable mistakes: they procrastinate, stick with
defaults, misjudge risks and act against their own long-term goals. Someone always
designs the context in which they choose, whether a cafeteria line, a pension form or an
organ-donor register, so there is no neutral option. Policy should design those choices
to help people do what they themselves would want on reflection, through defaults,
reminders and simpler options, while leaving them free to choose otherwise at little
cost. This respects liberty and improves lives at the same time.

**Key concepts.**

- **Choice architecture:** the unavoidable design of the context in which people choose.
- **Nudge:** a change to choice architecture that steers behaviour predictably without
  forbidding options or significantly changing incentives.
- **Bounded rationality:** people's systematic biases and limited attention, documented
  by behavioural science.
- **As judged by themselves:** the goal is to make people better off by their own
  standards, not the state's idea of the good.
- **Easy opt-out:** freedom of choice is preserved because people can decline the
  default at low cost.

**Signature reasons.**

- "There's no such thing as a neutral design."
- "Make the healthy choice the easy choice."
- "Automatic enrolment helps people save without forcing anyone."
- "Help people do what they'd want for themselves, and let them say no."
- "Test it, measure it, and keep what works."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Perfectionist liberalism:** promotes ways of life the state judges objectively
  valuable; libertarian paternalism aims at people's own ends (_What makes a life go
  well_; _Neutrality vs. perfectionism_).
- **Classical liberalism and Libertarianism:** most of their holders see nudges as
  manipulation and want the state out of personal choice (_Paternalism_, the central
  Dimension).
- **Technocracy:** expert rule over decisions themselves, not only over the design of
  choices (_Popular judgment vs. expertise_; Reasons).
- **Social conservatism:** enforces shared morality, not individual well-being (_Legal
  moralism_).

**Sources.** Colin Camerer et al., "Regulation for Conservatives: Behavioral Economics
and the Case for 'Asymmetric Paternalism'" (2003); Richard Thaler & Cass Sunstein,
"Libertarian Paternalism" (2003) and _Nudge_ (2008; final ed. 2021); Daniel Kahneman,
_Thinking, Fast and Slow_ (2011); Cass Sunstein, _Why Nudge?_ (2014) and _The Ethics of
Influence_ (2016); David Halpern, _Inside the Nudge Unit_ (2015).

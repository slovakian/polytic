# Republicanism and communitarianism: Label cards

Cards for this family, written to the template in `../label-library.md`, in the order
of the list. Every card here is written with the same dispassion as any other: the
self-description is the tradition's own self-understanding, steelmanned, and never
Polytic's verdict (`../principles.md`).

## Republicanism

- **Type:** Ideology
- **Parent:** —
- **Also called:** classical republicanism; civic humanism (its Renaissance and
  participatory form)
- **Variants:** Jeffersonian agrarian republicanism
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                        | Range                                                                                  | Central |
| -------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------- |
| Citizenship: rights vs. duties   | Strongly _Citizenship as shared obligation_ – Leans _Citizenship as shared obligation_ | Yes     |
| Leadership                       | Leans _Dispersed, checked authority_ – Strongly _Dispersed, checked authority_         | Yes     |
| Rule of law vs. discretion       | Leans _Rule by general, fixed rules_ – Strongly _Rule by general, fixed rules_         | Yes     |
| Majoritarian vs. consensus       | Middle – Leans _Power-sharing and broad consensus_                                     | No      |
| Economic scale and concentration | Middle – Leans _Dispersed ownership and small scale_                                   | No      |
| Neutrality vs. perfectionism     | Middle – Leans _State perfectionism_                                                   | No      |
| Social hierarchy                 | Leans _Equality of status_ – Middle                                                    | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** A people is free when it governs itself under laws of its own
making, not at the pleasure of a king, a faction, or a master. That freedom is fragile.
It survives only where power is divided and balanced in a mixed constitution, where laws
rule rather than men, and where citizens have the virtue to put the common good before
private interest and the independence to defend it. When citizens grow passive,
dependent, or corrupted by luxury, republics fall to tyranny.

**Key concepts.**

- **Liberty:** living as a free citizen under laws one shares in making, as opposed to
  dependence on another's will.
- **Civic virtue:** citizens' devotion to the common good and willingness to serve it,
  which the republic must cultivate.
- **Corruption:** the decay of virtue into private interest, faction, and dependence,
  the standing danger to every republic.
- **Mixed constitution:** power balanced among the one, the few, and the many, so that
  none can dominate.
- **The common good:** the _res publica_, the public thing, which belongs to all
  citizens and to no one in particular.

**Signature reasons.**

- "An empire of laws, and not of men."
- "Liberty is lost the moment citizens stop caring about public affairs."
- "Power must be made to check power."
- "Free citizens can't depend on a master's goodwill."
- "A republic, if you can keep it."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Liberalism:** treats liberty as non-interference and citizenship as protected
  liberty, with no duty to take part (_Citizenship: rights vs. duties_; Reasons).
- **Civic republicanism:** the contemporary neo-Roman school, which recasts republican
  liberty as non-domination and extends it to private power (_Social hierarchy_,
  _Workplace governance_; Reasons). Civic humanists who value participation for its own
  sake (Arendt, Sandel) are matched here, under Republicanism.
- **Communitarianism:** grounds politics in shared culture and the constitutive
  community, where republicanism grounds it in self-government and institutions
  (Reasons).
- **Monarchism:** a Thin Label for a hereditary head of state, the classic foil of
  republicanism (_Leadership_, _Social hierarchy_).

**Sources.** Cicero, _De re publica_ (54–51 BCE); Niccolò Machiavelli, _Discourses on
Livy_ (c. 1517); James Harrington, _The Commonwealth of Oceana_ (1656); Montesquieu,
_The Spirit of the Laws_ (1748); Alexander Hamilton, James Madison & John Jay, _The
Federalist_ (1787–88); Gordon S. Wood, _The Creation of the American Republic_ (1969);
J. G. A. Pocock, _The Machiavellian Moment_ (1975).

## Civic republicanism

- **Type:** Ideology
- **Parent:** Republicanism
- **Also called:** neo-Roman republicanism; neo-republicanism
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                                         | Range                                                                          | Central |
| ------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------- |
| Rule of law vs. discretion                        | Leans _Rule by general, fixed rules_ – Strongly _Rule by general, fixed rules_ | Yes     |
| Leadership                                        | Leans _Dispersed, checked authority_ – Strongly _Dispersed, checked authority_ | Yes     |
| Social hierarchy                                  | Strongly _Equality of status_ – Leans _Equality of status_                     | Yes     |
| Workplace governance                              | Strongly _Worker voice and self-management_ – Middle                           | No      |
| Economic scale and concentration                  | Middle – Leans _Dispersed ownership and small scale_                           | No      |
| Welfare and social insurance                      | Leans _Public social insurance_ – Strongly _Public social insurance_           | No      |
| Distribution                                      | Middle – Leans _Egalitarian redistribution_                                    | No      |
| Citizenship: rights vs. duties                    | Leans _Citizenship as shared obligation_ – Middle                              | No      |
| Popular sovereignty vs. constitutional constraint | Leans _Entrenched rights and judicial review_ – Middle                         | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Freedom is not just being left alone. It is not being at anyone's
mercy. A slave whose kind master never interferes is still unfree, because he lives at
the master's pleasure. A free society secures every citizen against arbitrary power,
whether of officials or of employers, husbands, landlords, and corporations, through
the rule of law, government that citizens can check and contest, and the resources to
stand on one's own feet, so that each person can look others in the eye without fear or
deference.

**Key concepts.**

- **Freedom as non-domination:** not being subject to anyone's arbitrary, uncontrolled
  power to interfere, whether or not they use it.
- **Arbitrary power:** power not forced to track the interests and ideas of those it
  affects.
- **Public and private power:** domination by the state (_imperium_) and by other
  people and organisations (_dominium_) both threaten freedom.
- **Contestation:** citizens must be able to challenge public decisions through courts,
  ombudsmen, and open debate, not only through elections.
- **The eyeball test:** each person should be able to look others in the eye without
  reason for fear or deference.

**Signature reasons.**

- "A slave with a kind master is still a slave."
- "Freedom means not living at someone else's mercy."
- "The state has to protect you from bosses, landlords and partners with arbitrary
  power over you, not only from itself."
- "Citizens must be able to contest every decision made in their name."
- "Everyone should be able to look anyone in the eye."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Republicanism:** the classical tradition, centred on civic virtue, participation, and
  a mixed constitution that kept ranks of citizens; civic republicanism centres
  non-domination and equal status (_Social hierarchy_; Reasons).
- **Liberalism** and **Social liberalism:** understand freedom as non-interference, so a
  kind master is no threat to it (_Workplace governance_; Reasons).
- **Neo-Brandeisianism:** an Area doctrine on concentrated corporate power that often
  rests on civic-republican grounds (_Economic scale and concentration_).
- **Communitarianism:** aims to sustain shared goods and identities, where civic
  republicanism seeks a freedom ideal that citizens of many views can share
  (_Neutrality vs. perfectionism_, _Individuals or wholes_).

**Sources.** Quentin Skinner, _Liberty before Liberalism_ (1998); Philip Pettit,
_Republicanism: A Theory of Freedom and Government_ (1997), _On the People's Terms_
(2012), _Just Freedom_ (2014); Maurizio Viroli, _Republicanism_ (1999; trans. 2002);
Cécile Laborde & John Maynor (eds.), _Republicanism and Political Theory_ (2008); Alex
Gourevitch, _From Slavery to the Cooperative Commonwealth_ (2015); Elizabeth Anderson,
_Private Government_ (2017).

## French republicanism

- **Type:** Ideology
- **Parent:** Republicanism
- **Also called:** the republican model (_le modèle républicain_); laïque
  republicanism; Jacobinism (historical, and used pejoratively by critics of its
  centralism)
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                                         | Range                                                                                  | Central |
| ------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------- |
| Religion and state                                | Strongly _Secular public order_                                                        | Yes     |
| Group difference                                  | Strongly _Difference-blind common rules_                                               | Yes     |
| Basis of nationhood                               | Strongly _Shared civic principles_ – Middle                                            | Yes     |
| Local vs. central                                 | Leans _Central and uniform authority_ – Strongly _Central and uniform authority_       | No      |
| Citizenship: rights vs. duties                    | Strongly _Citizenship as shared obligation_ – Leans _Citizenship as shared obligation_ | No      |
| Pluralism vs. unity                               | Middle – Leans _Unified common purpose_                                                | No      |
| Popular sovereignty vs. constitutional constraint | Middle – Leans _Popular and legislative supremacy_                                     | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** The Republic is one and indivisible: a community of free and
equal citizens who belong to it by sharing its principles of liberty, equality, and
fraternity, not by religion, ethnicity, or origin. Public institutions, above all the
school, must be neutral spaces where every citizen meets others as an equal, free from
the pressure of churches and communities, and where children are formed into citizens.
Recognising groups as groups would divide the nation into communities and trap
individuals in identities they did not choose.

**Key concepts.**

- **Laïcité:** the state's strict separation from religion and its neutrality toward
  all faiths, which protects believers and unbelievers alike and keeps public
  institutions free of religious claims.
- **Citizenship:** a direct, individual bond between citizen and Republic, unmediated by
  ethnic, religious, or regional groups.
- **Communautarisme:** the danger of the nation splitting into closed communities that
  govern their members' lives.
- **The republican school:** the institution that forms free citizens and frees children
  from inherited constraints.
- **Indivisibility:** one law and one people across the whole territory.

**Signature reasons.**

- "In the Republic there are no minorities, only citizens."
- "Religion is a private matter. The state school belongs to everyone."
- "Laïcité protects believers and unbelievers alike."
- "Recognising communities means abandoning individuals to them."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Multicultural liberalism:** grants group-differentiated rights, the opposite answer
  (_Group difference_).
- **Liberalism**, in its Anglo-American form: separates church and state while
  accommodating religion in public life, where laïcité keeps it out of public
  institutions (_Religion and state_; Reasons).
- **Civic nationalism:** a Thin Label for nationhood based on shared principles, which
  French republicanism shares and joins to laïcité and civic duty (_Religion and state_,
  _Citizenship: rights vs. duties_).
- **Kemalism:** also secularising and nation-building, but by a modernising state from
  above, with statist economics (_Leadership_, _Economic coordination_).
- **National populism:** defines the nation by culture and heritage (_Basis of
  nationhood_).

**Sources.** Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen (1789); Ernest Renan,
"What Is a Nation?" (1882); Jules Ferry, _Lettre aux instituteurs_ (1883); Law of 9
December 1905 on the Separation of the Churches and the State; Claude Nicolet, _L'Idée
républicaine en France_ (1982); Régis Debray, "Êtes-vous démocrate ou républicain?"
(1989); Stasi Commission, report on the application of the principle of laïcité
(2003); Cécile Laborde, _Critical Republicanism_ (2008).

## Communitarianism

- **Type:** Ideology
- **Parent:** —
- **Also called:** responsive communitarianism (Amitai Etzioni's movement); the
  communitarian critique of liberalism (its philosophical form, whose leading names,
  Sandel, MacIntyre, Taylor, and Walzer, mostly declined the label)
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                                   | Range                                                                                                          | Central |
| ------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------- |
| Individuals or wholes                       | Leans _Groups and relations are real_ – Strongly _Groups and relations are real_                               | Yes     |
| Citizenship: rights vs. duties              | Strongly _Citizenship as shared obligation_ – Leans _Citizenship as shared obligation_                         | Yes     |
| Moral limits of markets                     | Strongly _Protected non-market spheres_ – Leans _Protected non-market spheres_                                 | Yes     |
| Neutrality vs. perfectionism                | Middle – Strongly _State perfectionism_                                                                        | Yes     |
| Civil society vs. the state                 | Strongly _Family, community, and voluntary association_ – Leans _Family, community, and voluntary association_ | No      |
| Reciprocity in support                      | Middle – Leans _Conditional (reciprocal) support_                                                              | No      |
| Security and civil liberties                | Middle – Leans _Collective security and order_                                                                 | No      |
| Compatriot priority vs. global impartiality | Leans _Special obligations to compatriots_ – Middle                                                            | No      |
| Moral circle                                | Leans _Near and dear first_ – Middle                                                                           | No      |
| Self-authorship or given role               | Middle – Leans _Live up to a given role_                                                                       | No      |
| Tradition or reason                         | Leans _Inherited practice carries wisdom_ – Middle                                                             | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** We are not unencumbered selves who choose our ends from nowhere.
We are formed by families, communities, traditions, and nations whose goods are partly
constitutive of who we are. A politics that speaks only of individual rights and neutral
procedures lets those shared goods wither while markets and bureaucracies take over
more and more of life. Rights have to be balanced by responsibilities, and a healthy
society deliberately sustains the communities, moral voices, and common goods that make
free individuals possible.

**Key concepts.**

- **The self:** situated and "encumbered", constituted in part by attachments and
  traditions it did not choose.
- **The common good:** shared goods that belong to a community as a whole and cannot be
  reduced to private preferences.
- **Rights and responsibilities:** strong rights presume strong responsibilities toward
  family, community, and society.
- **Community:** the intermediate institutions (families, neighbourhoods, schools,
  congregations, associations) where moral life is learned.
- **Spheres of justice:** different goods (health, money, office, honour) carry
  different shared meanings and should be distributed by different principles.

**Signature reasons.**

- "Strong rights presume strong responsibilities."
- "There are some things money should not buy."
- "We are born into communities, not into a state of nature."
- "A society that only protects rights will lose the virtues that sustain them."
- "Neutrality about the good life is itself a view of the good life."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Liberalism:** treats the individual as prior to community and the state as neutral
  among ways of life (_Individuals or wholes_, _Neutrality vs. perfectionism_).
- **Traditionalist conservatism:** shares the attachment to community and inherited
  practice, but is more cautious about change; communitarians are often reformist and
  egalitarian (_Tempo of change_, _Distribution_; Reasons).
- **Post-liberalism:** holds that liberalism itself must be replaced; communitarianism
  seeks to rebalance liberal democracy from within (Reasons).
- **Republicanism:** grounds politics in self-government and institutions, where
  communitarianism grounds it in shared culture and identity (Reasons).
- **Blue Labour:** a communitarian current rooted in the labour movement and worker power
  (_Workplace governance_).

**Sources.** Charles Taylor, "Atomism" (1979) and _Sources of the Self_ (1989);
Alasdair MacIntyre, _After Virtue_ (1981); Michael Sandel, _Liberalism and the Limits of
Justice_ (1982) and _What Money Can't Buy_ (2012); Michael Walzer, _Spheres of Justice_
(1983); "The Responsive Communitarian Platform" (1991); Amitai Etzioni, _The Spirit of
Community_ (1993) and _The New Golden Rule_ (1996); Stephen Mulhall & Adam Swift,
_Liberals and Communitarians_ (2nd ed., 1996).

## Distributism

- **Type:** Ideology
- **Parent:** Communitarianism / Catholic social teaching
- **Also called:** distributivism
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                           | Range                                                                                                          | Central |
| ----------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------- |
| Economic scale and concentration    | Leans _Dispersed ownership and small scale_ – Strongly _Dispersed ownership and small scale_                   | Yes     |
| Property in capital and goods       | Leans _Robust private ownership_ – Middle                                                                      | Yes     |
| Civil society vs. the state         | Strongly _Family, community, and voluntary association_ – Leans _Family, community, and voluntary association_ | Yes     |
| Local vs. central                   | Strongly _Local and regional self-rule_ – Leans _Local and regional self-rule_                                 | No      |
| Workplace governance                | Leans _Worker voice and self-management_ – Middle                                                              | No      |
| Moral limits of markets             | Strongly _Protected non-market spheres_ – Leans _Protected non-market spheres_                                 | No      |
| Distribution                        | Middle – Leans _Egalitarian redistribution_                                                                    | No      |
| Welfare and social insurance        | Leans _Self-reliance and voluntary provision_ – Middle                                                         | No      |
| Sex roles in family and public life | Strongly _Distinct roles for the sexes_ – Middle                                                               | No      |
| Religion and state                  | Leans _Public role for religion_ – Middle                                                                      | No      |
| Transcendence                       | Strongly _A transcendent order exists_ – Leans _A transcendent order exists_                                   | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Capitalism and socialism make the same mistake. Both concentrate
the ownership of productive property, in a few capitalists or in the state, and leave
most people propertyless dependants working for someone else. Property is the
foundation of freedom and of the family, so it should be held as widely as possible: in
family farms, small shops and workshops, co-operatives, and guilds. The aim is a society
of owners, ordered by subsidiarity, where economic life serves the family and the
common good.

**Key concepts.**

- **Property:** a natural right and the guarantee of freedom, which is why it should be
  spread as widely as possible, not concentrated.
- **The servile state:** the end point of capitalism managed by the state, where a
  propertyless majority is kept secure but dependent.
- **Subsidiarity:** what families and small communities can do should not be taken over
  by larger bodies or the state.
- **Guilds:** associations of owners and workers in a trade that set standards and
  protect their members against concentration.
- **Just wage:** enough to support a family and let it acquire property.

**Signature reasons.**

- "Too much capitalism doesn't mean too many capitalists, but too few."
- "Three acres and a cow."
- "A man who owns nothing is at the mercy of whoever pays his wage."
- "The family, not the individual or the state, is the basic unit of society."
- "Neither the boss nor the bureaucrat: the owner."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Socialism:** shares the critique of capitalist concentration, but answers with common
  ownership rather than widespread private property (_Property in capital and goods_,
  _Civil society vs. the state_).
- **Classical liberalism:** defends private property and free markets, but accepts
  concentration and limits no market by principle (_Economic scale and concentration_,
  _Moral limits of markets_).
- **Catholic social teaching:** the parent, which also supports unions and the welfare
  state; distributism is its specific programme of spreading property (_Economic scale
  and concentration_, _Welfare and social insurance_).
- **Co-operativism:** shares the preference for co-operatives, without the family-property
  and Catholic framework (Reasons).
- **Neo-Brandeisianism:** a secular Area doctrine against concentrated corporate power
  through antitrust (_Civil society vs. the state_; Reasons).

**Sources.** Leo XIII, _Rerum Novarum_ (1891); G. K. Chesterton, _What's Wrong with the
World_ (1910) and _The Outline of Sanity_ (1926); Hilaire Belloc, _The Servile State_
(1912) and _An Essay on the Restoration of Property_ (1936); Vincent McNabb, _The Church
and the Land_ (1925); Pius XI, _Quadragesimo Anno_ (1931); E. F. Schumacher, _Small Is
Beautiful_ (1973), an influence; Allan C. Carlson, _Third Ways_ (2007).

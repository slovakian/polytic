# Ecologism: Label cards

Cards for this family, written to the template in `../label-library.md`, in the order
of the list. Ecologism itself is a Thin Label and heads the family; the others are
Ideologies that branch from it. Social ecology is in `anarchism.md` and Ecofascism in
`radical-right.md`, under their other parents. Every card here is written with the same
dispassion as any other: the self-description is the tradition's own
self-understanding, steelmanned, and never Polytic's verdict (`../principles.md`).

## Ecologism

- **Type:** Thin
- **Parent:** —
- **Also called:** green political thought; political ecology
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                | Range                                                                        | Central |
| ------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------- |
| Environmental protection | Leans _Ecological protection first_ – Strongly _Ecological protection first_ | Yes     |
| Growth                   | Strongly _Sufficiency and limits_ – Middle                                   | No      |
| Standing of nature       | Middle – Strongly _Nature-centered_                                          | No      |
| Future generations       | Leans _Future people's claims_ – Strongly _Future people's claims_           | No      |
| Technology and risk      | Middle – Strongly _Precaution_                                               | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Human beings are part of nature, not its masters, and every
economy and polity rests on ecological systems that have limits. A politics that treats
the planet as an endless supply of resources and a bottomless sink for waste is
undermining its own foundations. Taking those limits seriously means changing more than
policy: it means rethinking what we value, how we produce, and what we owe to other
living things and to the people who come after us.

**Key concepts.**

- **Limits:** the biosphere has finite capacity, so endless material growth on a finite
  planet is impossible, not merely unwise.
- **Nature:** a web of interdependence that humans belong to, with value that goes beyond
  its usefulness to us.
- **Sustainability:** meeting present needs without destroying the conditions later
  generations and other species depend on.
- **Interdependence:** ecological, social, and economic problems are connected and
  cannot be solved one at a time.

**Signature reasons.**

- "There is no economy on a dead planet."
- "Infinite growth on a finite planet is impossible."
- "We don't inherit the Earth from our ancestors; we borrow it from our children."
- "We're part of nature, not apart from it."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Environmentalism:** protects the environment within existing arrangements and
  usually accepts continued growth; ecologism holds that ecological limits require
  changing the economy and our values (_Growth_, _Standing of nature_; Reasons).
- **Ecomodernism:** a branch that rejects the limits-to-growth premise and pins its hopes
  on technology (_Growth_, _Technology and risk_).
- **Animal rights:** extends moral concern to individual sentient animals; ecologism
  centres ecosystems, species, and limits (Reasons).

**Sources.** Aldo Leopold, _A Sand County Almanac_ (1949); Donella Meadows et al., _The
Limits to Growth_ (1972); Andrew Dobson, _Green Political Thought_ (1990; 4th ed. 2007);
Robyn Eckersley, _Environmentalism and Political Theory_ (1992); John S. Dryzek, _The
Politics of the Earth_ (1997; 3rd ed. 2013).

## Green politics

| Force in domestic politics          | Leans _Principled nonviolence_ – Strongly _Principled nonviolence_                             | Yes     |
| Local vs. central                   | Strongly _Local and regional self-rule_ – Leans _Local and regional self-rule_                 | No      |
| Growth                              | Strongly _Sufficiency and limits_ – Leans _Sufficiency and limits_                             | No      |
| Technology and risk                 | Leans _Precaution_ – Strongly _Precaution_                                                     | No      |
| Future generations                  | Leans _Future people's claims_ – Strongly _Future people's claims_                             | No      |
| Use of force                        | Leans _Reluctance to use force_ – Strongly _Reluctance to use force_                           | No      |
| Sex roles in family and public life | Leans _Equal and interchangeable roles_ – Strongly _Equal and interchangeable roles_           | No      |
| Animals                             | Strongly _Legal protection of animal interests_ – Leans _Legal protection of animal interests_ | No      |
| Immigration and borders             | Middle – Strongly _Freedom of movement_                                                        | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Ecological wisdom, social justice, grassroots democracy, and
nonviolence are four sides of one commitment, and none of them holds without the
others. A society that exploits nature also exploits people, and decisions made far away
by the powerful tend to sacrifice both. Greens want an economy that lives within
ecological limits, shares its wealth fairly, and is run as close to the people affected
as possible, reached by peaceful and democratic means.

**Key concepts.**

- **Ecological wisdom:** human societies must live within the planet's limits and learn
  from how natural systems work.
- **Social justice:** ecological burdens fall hardest on the poor, so environmental and
  economic justice go together.
- **Grassroots democracy:** people should have a direct say in the decisions that shape
  their lives and places.
- **Nonviolence:** the means must match the ends; a peaceful society can't be built by
  force.
- **Decentralisation:** human-scale communities and economies, close to the land they
  live on (the bioregional strand).

**Signature reasons.**

- "Think globally, act locally."
- "Social justice and ecological sustainability are the same struggle."
- "Neither left nor right, but in front."
- "Decisions should be made by the people they affect."
- "You can't build a peaceful world with violent means."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Eco-socialism:** also egalitarian and anti-growth, but makes social ownership of the
  economy the core answer and is less committed to nonviolence and decentralism
  (_Property in capital and goods_, _Economic coordination_, _Force in domestic
  politics_).
- **Green liberalism:** pursues environmental protection inside liberal institutions and
  markets, without the four-pillar programme of participation and redistribution
  (_Representation vs. participation_, _Distribution_).
- **Social ecology:** shares the decentralised, democratic vision, but rejects the state
  and electoral politics that green parties use (_Political authority_).
- **Environmentalism:** pursues reforms without the wider social and democratic
  programme, and usually without questioning growth (_Growth_, _Distribution_).

**Sources.** Charlene Spretnak & Fritjof Capra, _Green Politics: The Global Promise_
(1984); Petra Kelly, _Fighting for Hope_ (1984); Jonathon Porritt, _Seeing Green_ (1984);
Kirkpatrick Sale, _Dwellers in the Land: The Bioregional Vision_ (1985); Global Greens
Charter (2001); Dobson, _Green Political Thought_ (2007).

## Deep ecology

- **Type:** Ideology
- **Parent:** Ecologism
- **Also called:** the deep ecology movement; ecosophy (Naess)
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                | Range                                                                                          | Central |
| ------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------- |
| Standing of nature       | Strongly _Nature-centered_                                                                     | Yes     |
| Environmental protection | Strongly _Ecological protection first_                                                         | Yes     |
| Growth                   | Strongly _Sufficiency and limits_                                                              | Yes     |
| Sacred or tradeable      | Strongly _Some things are beyond price_ – Leans _Some things are beyond price_                 | No      |
| Animals                  | Strongly _Legal protection of animal interests_ – Leans _Legal protection of animal interests_ | No      |
| Future generations       | Leans _Future people's claims_ – Strongly _Future people's claims_                             | No      |
| Technology and risk      | Leans _Precaution_ – Strongly _Precaution_                                                     | No      |
| Local vs. central        | Strongly _Local and regional self-rule_ – Leans _Local and regional self-rule_                 | No      |
| Tempo of change          | Strongly _Fundamental transformation_ – Leans _Fundamental transformation_                     | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** The flourishing of human and non-human life has value in itself,
independent of its usefulness to us, and the richness and diversity of life are values
too. Humans have no right to reduce that richness except to satisfy vital needs, yet our
interference with the living world is already excessive and getting worse. Real change
means more than better pollution control. It means a shift in our basic ideas, in which
we come to identify with the wider web of life, and a gradual, humane decrease in human
numbers and consumption so that the rest of nature can recover.

**Key concepts.**

- **Intrinsic value:** living beings and ecosystems have value of their own, not only as
  resources for humans.
- **Self-realization:** the self matures by widening its identification from ego to
  family, community, and finally the whole of life (Naess).
- **Biospherical egalitarianism:** in principle, every form of life has an equal right to
  live and flourish.
- **Shallow vs. deep:** "shallow" environmentalism fights pollution and depletion for
  the health of people in rich countries; "deep" ecology questions the human-centred
  worldview behind them.

**Signature reasons.**

- "Nature doesn't exist for us. Its worth doesn't depend on what it does for us."
- "Fewer people would let both people and the rest of nature flourish."
- "Better technology won't save us if we keep the same values."
- "Richness and diversity of life are values in themselves."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Social ecology:** traces the ecological crisis to human hierarchy and domination
  rather than to human-centredness, and rejects deep ecology's population focus
  (_Political authority_; Reasons).
- **Animal rights:** values individual sentient animals; deep ecology values species,
  ecosystems, and the whole web of life, and can accept hunting or culling that serve it
  (Reasons).
- **Anarcho-primitivism:** shares the critique of industrial civilisation but makes the
  abolition of the state and of domestication its programme (_Political authority_,
  _Technology and risk_).
- **Ecofascism:** also invokes ecological survival, but adds nationalism and
  authoritarian rule; deep ecologists' platform is democratic and nonviolent
  (_Leadership_, _Basis of nationhood_, _Pluralism vs. unity_).

**Sources.** Arne Naess, "The Shallow and the Deep, Long-Range Ecology Movement"
(_Inquiry_, 1973) and _Ecology, Community and Lifestyle_ (1989); Arne Naess & George
Sessions, the eight-point deep ecology platform (1984); Bill Devall & George Sessions,
_Deep Ecology_ (1985); Warwick Fox, _Toward a Transpersonal Ecology_ (1990); Alan
Drengson & Yuichi Inoue (eds.), _The Deep Ecology Movement_ (1995).

## Eco-socialism

- **Type:** Ideology
- **Parent:** Ecologism / Socialism
- **Also called:** ecosocialism; ecological Marxism
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                                   | Range                                                                                  | Central |
| ------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------- |
| Property in capital and goods               | Leans _Socially conditioned ownership_ – Strongly _Socially conditioned ownership_     | Yes     |
| Environmental protection                    | Leans _Ecological protection first_ – Strongly _Ecological protection first_           | Yes     |
| Economic coordination                       | Strongly _Public direction_ – Leans _Public direction_                                 | Yes     |
| Growth                                      | Strongly _Sufficiency and limits_ – Leans _Sufficiency and limits_                     | No      |
| Distribution                                | Leans _Egalitarian redistribution_ – Strongly _Egalitarian redistribution_             | No      |
| Workplace governance                        | Strongly _Worker voice and self-management_ – Leans _Worker voice and self-management_ | No      |
| Moral limits of markets                     | Strongly _Protected non-market spheres_ – Leans _Protected non-market spheres_         | No      |
| Compatriot priority vs. global impartiality | Leans _Impartial concern for all people_ – Strongly _Impartial concern for all people_ | No      |
| Tempo of change                             | Strongly _Fundamental transformation_ – Leans _Fundamental transformation_             | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** The ecological crisis is not an accident of bad policy. It follows
from capitalism itself, which must grow or die and treats nature as a free gift to be used
up for profit. Green taxes and markets in pollution leave that engine running. Only an
economy under democratic social control, planned for human need and ecological limits
rather than accumulation, can stop the destruction. The workers and poor communities who
suffer first from it are the ones who can build that alternative.

**Key concepts.**

- **Metabolic rift:** capitalism breaks the cycle of exchange between society and
  nature, draining soil, climate, and ecosystems (Marx, as read by Foster and Saito).
- **Second contradiction:** capitalism undermines the natural and social conditions of
  its own production (O'Connor).
- **Use value:** production should serve what people need and can sustain, not the
  exchange value that profit demands.
- **Climate justice:** those who did least to cause the crisis suffer most, within
  countries and between them.

**Signature reasons.**

- "System change, not climate change."
- "Capitalism must grow or die, and the planet can't afford either."
- "You can't fix a crisis with the logic that caused it."
- "The Earth's resources should be planned for need, not sold for profit."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Socialism:** shares the ownership goal, but eco-socialism makes ecological limits
  part of the case for it, and rejects productivist growth (_Environmental protection_,
  _Growth_).
- **Degrowth:** shares the critique of growth, but does not always require social
  ownership of the economy (_Property in capital and goods_, _Economic coordination_).
- **Green politics:** reformist, decentralist, and committed to nonviolence; eco-socialism
  puts class and ownership at the centre (_Property in capital and goods_; Reasons).
- **Ecomodernism:** trusts growth and technology to decouple prosperity from nature
  (_Growth_, _Technology and risk_).

**Sources.** André Gorz, _Ecology as Politics_ (1975; trans. 1980); James O'Connor,
_Natural Causes: Essays in Ecological Marxism_ (1998); John Bellamy Foster, _Marx's
Ecology_ (2000); Joel Kovel & Michael Löwy, "An Ecosocialist Manifesto" (2001); Joel
Kovel, _The Enemy of Nature_ (2002); Michael Löwy, _Ecosocialism_ (2015); Kohei Saito,
_Marx in the Anthropocene_ (2023).

## Green liberalism

- **Type:** Ideology
- **Parent:** Ecologism / Liberalism
- **Also called:** liberal environmentalism; free-market environmentalism (its
  property-rights strand); critics: "green capitalism"
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                                         | Range                                                                                                        | Central |
| ------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------- |
| Environmental protection                          | Leans _Ecological protection first_ – Strongly _Ecological protection first_                                 | Yes     |
| Neutrality vs. perfectionism                      | Strongly _State neutrality_ – Leans _State neutrality_                                                       | Yes     |
| Pluralism vs. unity                               | Strongly _Legitimate pluralism and opposition_ – Leans _Legitimate pluralism and opposition_                 | No      |
| Popular sovereignty vs. constitutional constraint | Strongly _Entrenched rights and judicial review_ – Leans _Entrenched rights and judicial review_             | No      |
| Economic coordination                             | Middle – Leans _Market coordination_                                                                         | No      |
| Property in capital and goods                     | Leans _Robust private ownership_ – Middle                                                                    | No      |
| Paternalism                                       | Middle – Leans _Choice_                                                                                      | No      |
| Growth                                            | Leans _Sufficiency and limits_ – Leans _Growth and abundance_                                                | No      |
| Sovereignty vs. pooled governance                 | Strongly _Pooled, rule-bound international governance_ – Leans _Pooled, rule-bound international governance_ | No      |
| Future generations                                | Leans _Future people's claims_ – Strongly _Future people's claims_                                           | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Protecting the environment is something liberals have their own
reasons to do, not a value they must borrow from a rival creed. Pollution and climate
change harm other people without their consent, future citizens have rights too, and a
ruined natural world leaves everyone fewer options for their lives. A green society can
be reached through rights, the rule of law, and well-designed markets that put a price on
damage, without the state imposing one ecological vision of the good life on everyone.

**Key concepts.**

- **Harm:** environmental damage is harm to others, including the not-yet-born, so the
  harm principle already licenses strong protection.
- **Neutrality:** the state protects the conditions everyone needs without ranking
  citizens' ways of life or imposing ecocentric values.
- **Externalities:** pollution is a cost someone imposes on others without paying for
  it; pricing or property rights can make the polluter pay.
- **Options:** a rich natural world is part of the range of choices that a free life
  needs.

**Signature reasons.**

- "Polluting someone else's air is a violation of their rights."
- "Make the polluter pay, and let the market find the cheapest way to stop."
- "Future people's freedom matters as much as ours."
- "You don't need to worship nature to want to protect it."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Environmentalism:** pursues similar reforms pragmatically; green liberalism gives a
  principled liberal case for them and can support strong protection (_Environmental
  protection_; Reasons).
- **Green politics:** joins ecology to participation, redistribution, and a shared
  vision of the good life (_Neutrality vs. perfectionism_, _Representation vs.
  participation_).
- **Ecomodernism:** bets on growth and technology rather than rights and pricing
  (_Growth_, _Technology and risk_).
- **Liberalism:** the parent; green liberalism adds strong environmental protection and
  the rights of future people (_Environmental protection_, _Future generations_).

**Sources.** Terry L. Anderson & Donald R. Leal, _Free Market Environmentalism_ (1991);
Marcel Wissenburg, _Green Liberalism: The Free and the Green Society_ (1998); Piers H. G.
Stephens, "Green Liberalisms: Nature, Agency and the Good" (_Environmental Politics_,
2001); Derek Bell, "How Can Political Liberals Be Environmentalists?" (_Political
Studies_, 2002); Simon Hailwood, _How to Be a Green Liberal_ (2004).

## Eco-conservatism

- **Type:** Ideology
- **Parent:** Ecologism / Conservatism
- **Also called:** green conservatism; conservative environmentalism; conservationism
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                         | Range                                                                                                          | Central |
| --------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------- |
| Environmental protection          | Middle – Strongly _Ecological protection first_                                                                | Yes     |
| Local vs. central                 | Strongly _Local and regional self-rule_ – Leans _Local and regional self-rule_                                 | Yes     |
| Tradition or reason               | Strongly _Inherited practice carries wisdom_ – Leans _Inherited practice carries wisdom_                       | Yes     |
| Civil society vs. the state       | Strongly _Family, community, and voluntary association_ – Leans _Family, community, and voluntary association_ | No      |
| Future generations                | Leans _Future people's claims_ – Strongly _Future people's claims_                                             | No      |
| Tempo of change                   | Leans _Incremental reform_ – Strongly _Incremental reform_                                                     | No      |
| Design vs. evolved order          | Strongly _Evolved, spontaneous order_ – Leans _Evolved, spontaneous order_                                     | No      |
| Sovereignty vs. pooled governance | Middle – Strongly _Sovereign self-government_                                                                  | No      |
| Basis of nationhood               | Middle – Leans _Shared heritage and culture_                                                                   | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Conservation is what conservatives have always done: hand on to
our children what we inherited, in at least as good a condition as we received it. The
real motive for protecting the environment is love of home (the landscape, the farm,
the town, the country), and that love is local, rooted, and practical. Global plans and
distant bureaucracies tend to destroy what they mean to save. Stewardship works best
when it is carried by families, landowners, and communities who know a place and will
live with the consequences.

**Key concepts.**

- **Oikophilia:** love of home, the settled attachment to a place and its people, as the
  motive for caring for it (Scruton).
- **Stewardship:** we hold land and landscape in trust between the dead, the living, and
  the unborn, not as owners free to use them up.
- **Scale:** problems are best solved by those closest to them, with local knowledge and
  a stake in the outcome.
- **Prudence:** suspicion of sweeping schemes, environmental ones included, whose side
  effects no planner can foresee.

**Signature reasons.**

- "Conservation is conservative."
- "People protect what they love, and they love what is theirs and near."
- "The land was entrusted to us by our parents and is owed to our grandchildren."
- "Global treaties and central plans don't care for a single field."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Green politics:** shares localism, but couples it to egalitarian redistribution and
  radical change (_Distribution_, _Tempo of change_).
- **Traditionalist conservatism:** the same respect for inheritance, but without ecology
  at the centre of politics (_Environmental protection_).
- **Agrarianism:** honours farming life as the moral backbone of society; eco-conservatism
  is about the stewardship of land and landscape generally (Reasons).
- **Ecofascism:** also ties land to people, but demands authoritarian and nationalist
  control; eco-conservatism works through local and civil institutions (_Leadership_,
  _Basis of nationhood_).

**Sources.** Edmund Burke, _Reflections on the Revolution in France_ (1790); Wendell
Berry, _The Unsettling of America_ (1977); John Gray, _Beyond the New Right_ (1993), ch.
"An Agenda for Green Conservatism"; Gordon K. Durnil, _The Making of a Conservative
Environmentalist_ (1995); Roger Scruton, _Green Philosophy: How to Think Seriously About
the Planet_ (2012).

## Degrowth

- **Type:** Ideology
- **Parent:** Ecologism
- **Also called:** décroissance; post-growth (its milder form); steady-state economics
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                                   | Range                                                                                  | Central |
| ------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------- |
| Growth                                      | Strongly _Sufficiency and limits_                                                      | Yes     |
| Environmental protection                    | Strongly _Ecological protection first_                                                 | Yes     |
| Distribution                                | Leans _Egalitarian redistribution_ – Strongly _Egalitarian redistribution_             | No      |
| Welfare and social insurance                | Leans _Public social insurance_ – Strongly _Public social insurance_                   | No      |
| Work                                        | Middle – Strongly _Work is a means to living_                                          | No      |
| Moral limits of markets                     | Strongly _Protected non-market spheres_ – Leans _Protected non-market spheres_         | No      |
| Compatriot priority vs. global impartiality | Leans _Impartial concern for all people_ – Strongly _Impartial concern for all people_ | No      |
| Local vs. central                           | Strongly _Local and regional self-rule_ – Leans _Local and regional self-rule_         | No      |
| Technology and risk                         | Middle – Strongly _Precaution_                                                         | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Rich economies have already passed the point where more output
makes people happier or healthier, and their material throughput is breaking the
planet's limits. No technology has decoupled growth from resource use at the speed
required. So rich countries should deliberately scale down energy and materials use,
fairly and democratically, while sharing work and wealth so that everyone has enough.
A good life depends on time, care, community, and secure basic services, not on ever
more goods.

**Key concepts.**

- **Throughput:** the flow of energy and materials through the economy, which is what
  must shrink, whatever happens to GDP.
- **Decoupling:** the hope that growth can continue while environmental impact falls,
  which degrowthers think has not happened fast enough and cannot.
- **Sufficiency:** enough for a good life, with limits on excess.
- **Universal basic services:** health, housing, transport, and care provided in common,
  so security does not depend on growth.
- **Decolonisation of the imaginary:** freeing politics from the assumption that more
  is always better (Latouche).

**Signature reasons.**

- "Infinite growth on a finite planet is impossible."
- "Beyond a point, more stuff doesn't make us happier."
- "Share the work, share the wealth, and work less."
- "Rich countries must use less so that poor countries can have enough."
- "GDP measures the size of the economy, not how well we live."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Eco-socialism:** wants social ownership as the answer; degrowth targets throughput
  and can be pursued in a mixed economy (_Property in capital and goods_).
- **Ecomodernism:** the opposite bet, that growth and technology can be decoupled from
  environmental harm (_Growth_, _Technology and risk_).
- **Environmentalism:** reforms within a growing economy (_Growth_).
- **Deep ecology:** grounds limits in the intrinsic value of nature; degrowth's case is
  mainly about human well-being and justice (_Standing of nature_; Reasons).

**Sources.** Nicholas Georgescu-Roegen, _The Entropy Law and the Economic Process_
(1971); Herman Daly, _Steady-State Economics_ (1977); Serge Latouche, _Farewell to
Growth_ (2007; trans. 2009); Tim Jackson, _Prosperity Without Growth_ (2009); Giacomo
D'Alisa, Federico Demaria & Giorgos Kallis (eds.), _Degrowth: A Vocabulary for a New
Era_ (2014); Giorgos Kallis, _Degrowth_ (2018); Jason Hickel, _Less Is More_ (2020).

## Ecomodernism

- **Type:** Ideology
- **Parent:** Ecologism
- **Also called:** eco-pragmatism
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                | Range                                                                          | Central |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------- |
| Growth                   | Leans _Growth and abundance_ – Strongly _Growth and abundance_                 | Yes     |
| Technology and risk      | Strongly _Proaction_ – Leans _Proaction_                                       | Yes     |
| Environmental protection | Middle – Strongly _Ecological protection first_                                | Yes     |
| Direction of history     | Strongly _History is progressing_ – Leans _History is progressing_             | No      |
| Expert trust             | Strongly _Defer to expert institutions_ – Leans _Defer to expert institutions_ | No      |
| Standing of nature       | Leans _Human-centered_ – Leans _Nature-centered_                               | No      |
| Future generations       | Middle – Strongly _Future people's claims_                                     | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Humanity can both prosper and protect nature, but only by using
less of it. Intensifying agriculture, living in cities, and switching to dense,
clean energy (nuclear above all) let us return land and seas to wildlife while lifting
billions out of poverty. Asking the poor to stay poor, or the rich to go back to simpler
lives, will neither work nor be fair. Technology, growth, and human ingenuity are the
way out of the ecological crisis, not its cause.

**Key concepts.**

- **Decoupling:** human welfare can grow while human impact on nature shrinks, as it
  already has in some places.
- **Land sparing:** using less land more intensively so that more can be left wild.
- **Energy density:** abundant, concentrated, low-carbon energy (nuclear, advanced
  renewables) is what makes a prosperous green future possible.
- **Modernisation:** urbanisation, development, and technology have been good for people
  and can be good for nature.

**Signature reasons.**

- "The way to save nature is to need less of it."
- "Nuclear power is the greenest energy we have."
- "Poor countries deserve the abundance rich countries already enjoy."
- "Technology caused some of our problems, and technology is how we'll solve them."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Degrowth:** holds that decoupling has failed and rich economies must shrink
  (_Growth_, _Technology and risk_).
- **Abundance liberalism:** also wants to build more energy and housing, but its case is
  about scarcity and procedure, not ecology (_Environmental protection_; Reasons).
- **Effective accelerationism:** shares the pro-growth, pro-technology stance but without
  the aim of sparing nature and with hostility to regulation (_Environmental
  protection_).
- **Environmentalism:** wants reform within existing arrangements; ecomodernism wants
  rapid technological transformation, especially in energy and agriculture (_Technology
  and risk_; Reasons).

**Sources.** Ted Nordhaus & Michael Shellenberger, _Break Through_ (2007); Stewart
Brand, _Whole Earth Discipline_ (2009); Mark Lynas, _The God Species_ (2011); John
Asafu-Adjaye et al., _An Ecomodernist Manifesto_ (2015); Hannah Ritchie, _Not the End of
the World_ (2024).

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Living well is not living better than others, or having ever more.
It is living in balance with one's community and with Mother Earth (Pachamama), of which
humans are part. Andean and Amazonian peoples have long known this, and it offers an
alternative to development models that treat nature as raw material and people as
isolated consumers. Nature itself has rights, which the law should recognise, and a
plurinational state should respect the many peoples and ways of life within it.

**Key concepts.**

- **Pachamama:** Mother Earth as a living whole, of which humans are part, with rights of
  its own to exist and regenerate.
- **Living well (not living better):** harmony and sufficiency within a community, not
  accumulation or competition.
- **Relationality:** a person, a community, and nature are bound together; none is well
  apart from the others.
- **Plurinationality:** one state of many peoples, each with its own culture, territory,
  and forms of self-government.

**Signature reasons.**

- "Nature is not a resource. It's our mother."
- "Living well means living in harmony, not having more."
- "Nature has rights, and they belong in the constitution."
- "Development that destroys the forest is not development."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Deep ecology:** also ecocentric, but grounded in a Western philosophy of individual
  self-realization; buen vivir is rooted in Andean communal cosmology and community life
  (_Individuals or wholes_; Reasons).
- **Indigenous resurgence:** shares the Indigenous roots, but centres sovereignty and land
  restitution for particular peoples rather than a general ecological ethic (centered
  group; _Historical injustice_).
- **Degrowth:** shares sufficiency and limits, but argues from human well-being and
  ecological economics, not cosmology (Reasons).
- **Bolivarianism:** shared the constitutional moment in the Andes, but in practice
  pursued state-led, resource-funded development (_Growth_, _Environmental protection_).

# Economic and developmental doctrines: Label cards

Cards for this family, written to the template in `../label-library.md`, in the order
of the list. Every card here is written with the same dispassion as any other: the
self-description is the tradition's own self-understanding, steelmanned, and never
Polytic's verdict (`../principles.md`). Most of these doctrines are usually an Area
Label: they describe the economic part of a person's map and ride alongside a Main
Label.

## Keynesianism

- **Type:** Ideology (usually Area)
- **Parent:** Social liberalism / Social democracy
- **Also called:** demand-side economics; New Keynesianism (its mainstream synthesis
  with rational expectations); post-Keynesianism (its heterodox wing)
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                    | Range                                                                                  | Central |
| ---------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------- |
| Money and public finance     | Strongly _Discretion and active management_ – Leans _Discretion and active management_ | Yes     |
| Economic coordination        | Leans _Public direction_ – Leans _Market coordination_                                 | No      |
| Welfare and social insurance | Middle – Strongly _Public social insurance_                                            | No      |
| Causes of fortune            | Middle – Leans _Luck and structure_                                                    | No      |
| Design vs. evolved order     | Middle – Leans _Deliberate design_                                                     | No      |
| Expert trust                 | Strongly _Defer to expert institutions_ – Leans _Defer to expert institutions_         | No      |
| Tempo of change              | Leans _Incremental reform_ – Strongly _Incremental reform_                             | No      |
| Zero-sum or positive-sum     | Leans _Gains can be shared_ – Strongly _Gains can be shared_                           | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** A market economy is the best engine of prosperity we have, but it
does not reliably keep everyone at work. When households and firms all try to save at
once, spending collapses, and an economy can sit for years with idle factories and
willing workers who cannot find jobs. Government should then do what private actors
cannot: cut interest rates, and when that is not enough, borrow and spend to restore
demand, paying the debt down in good times. Managing demand this way saves capitalism
from its own slumps without replacing it.

**Key concepts.**

- **Unemployment:** mostly involuntary, the result of too little total demand, not of
  workers refusing jobs or wages refusing to fall.
- **Aggregate demand:** the total spending in an economy, which sets output and
  employment in the short run and which government can and should manage.
- **Deficits:** a tool, not a sin; run them in slumps and close them in booms
  (counter-cyclical policy).
- **Uncertainty:** investment rests on "animal spirits" and expectations that can
  collapse suddenly, so markets do not self-correct quickly.

**Signature reasons.**

- "In the long run we are all dead. People need jobs now, not after the market
  eventually clears."
- "When everyone tightens their belt at once, the economy shrinks."
- "Austerity in a recession makes the recession worse."
- "The time to cut the deficit is the boom, not the slump."
- "Government spending has a multiplier: a pound spent in a slump becomes more than a
  pound of output."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Modern Monetary Theory:** goes further, holding that a currency-issuing state has
  no solvency limit at all, only an inflation limit; Keynesians still treat debt
  sustainability as a real constraint over the cycle (Reasons on _Money and public
  finance_).
- **Chicago school:** prefers a fixed monetary rule and doubts that discretionary
  fiscal policy stabilises anything (_Money and public finance_, the central
  Dimension).
- **Austrian school:** sees slumps as the necessary liquidation of credit-fuelled
  malinvestment, which stimulus only prolongs (_Money and public finance_; _Design vs.
  evolved order_).
- **Fiscal conservatism:** balanced budgets and low debt as a standing rule, not only in
  booms (_Money and public finance_).
- **Social democracy:** often Keynesian in macroeconomics, but its centre is the welfare
  state, unions and the mixed economy, not demand management (Reasons).

**Sources.** John Maynard Keynes, _The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money_
(1936) and _A Tract on Monetary Reform_ (1923); Abba Lerner, "Functional Finance and
the Federal Debt" (1943); Hyman Minsky, _Stabilizing an Unstable Economy_ (1986); Paul
Krugman, _The Return of Depression Economics_ (1999; rev. 2008); Robert Skidelsky,
_Keynes: The Return of the Master_ (2009); Peter Clarke, _Keynes: The Twentieth
Century's Most Influential Economist_ (2009).

## Chicago school

- **Type:** Ideology (usually Area)
- **Parent:** Classical liberalism
- **Also called:** monetarism (its macroeconomic doctrine); law and economics (its legal
  branch); critics: "neoliberalism"
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                        | Range                                                                                | Central |
| -------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------- |
| Money and public finance         | Leans _Rules and fiscal discipline_ – Strongly _Rules and fiscal discipline_         | Yes     |
| Economic coordination            | Leans _Market coordination_ – Strongly _Market coordination_                         | Yes     |
| Economic scale and concentration | Strongly _Scale and efficiency_ – Leans _Scale and efficiency_                       | No      |
| Distribution                     | Strongly _Market entitlement_ – Leans _Market entitlement_                           | No      |
| Welfare and social insurance     | Leans _Self-reliance and voluntary provision_ – Middle                               | No      |
| Trade and economic openness      | Strongly _Open exchange_ – Leans _Open exchange_                                     | No      |
| Paternalism                      | Leans _Choice_ – Strongly _Choice_                                                   | No      |
| Rule of law vs. discretion       | Leans _Rule by general, fixed rules_ – Strongly _Rule by general, fixed rules_       | No      |
| Grounds for belief               | Strongly _Belief proportioned to evidence_ – Leans _Belief proportioned to evidence_ | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Economics is an empirical science, and the evidence shows that
free prices and competition allocate resources better than officials can. Inflation is
always and everywhere a monetary phenomenon, so a central bank should follow a
predictable rule rather than fine-tune the economy on guesses. Most regulation ends up
serving the industries it regulates, and many well-meant programmes hurt the people
they aim to help. Where help is needed, give people money and choice (a negative income
tax, school vouchers) rather than bureaucratic services.

**Key concepts.**

- **Inflation:** caused by money growing faster than output; the cure is a steady,
  rule-bound money supply.
- **Regulation:** prone to capture, so judged by its measured effects, not its
  intentions.
- **Efficiency:** the test for law and antitrust; big firms are fine if they got big by
  serving consumers (the consumer-welfare standard).
- **Rational choice:** people respond to incentives, in markets and outside them
  (crime, family, politics), and policy should be built on that.
- **Positive economics:** judge a theory by whether its predictions hold, not by the
  realism of its assumptions.

**Signature reasons.**

- "Inflation is always and everywhere a monetary phenomenon."
- "There's no such thing as a free lunch."
- "Judge policies by their results, not their intentions."
- "Regulators end up working for the industries they regulate."
- "If you want to help the poor, give them money and let them choose."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Austrian school:** also pro-market and hard on inflation, but rejects aggregate
  statistics and econometric testing for deductive reasoning about action, and blames
  slumps on credit-driven malinvestment, not on monetary contraction (Reasons;
  _Design vs. evolved order_).
- **Keynesianism:** wants discretionary demand management and deficits in slumps
  (_Money and public finance_, the central Dimension).
- **Ordoliberalism:** a strong state must police concentration; the Chicago school
  tolerates large firms that are efficient (_Economic scale and concentration_).
- **Market liberalism:** the international policy programme (open trade, privatisation,
  rule-bound global institutions) that drew on Chicago ideas; Chicago is the economic
  school (Reasons; _Sovereignty vs. pooled governance_).
- **Libertarianism:** grounds free markets in rights; the Chicago school grounds them in
  measured outcomes (_Outcomes or rules_; Reasons).

**Sources.** Frank Knight, _Risk, Uncertainty and Profit_ (1921); Henry Simons, "Rules
versus Authorities in Monetary Policy" (1936); Milton Friedman, "The Methodology of
Positive Economics" (1953), _Capitalism and Freedom_ (1962) and "The Role of Monetary
Policy" (1968); Milton Friedman & Anna Schwartz, _A Monetary History of the United
States, 1867–1960_ (1963); George Stigler, "The Theory of Economic Regulation" (1971);
Gary Becker, _The Economic Approach to Human Behavior_ (1976); Richard Posner, _Economic
Analysis of Law_ (1973); Robert Bork, _The Antitrust Paradox_ (1978); Johan Van
Overtveldt, _The Chicago School_ (2007).

## Austrian school

- **Type:** Ideology (usually Area)
- **Parent:** Classical liberalism / Libertarianism
- **Also called:** Austrian economics; the Mises–Hayek tradition; the Vienna school
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                     | Range                                                                        | Central |
| ----------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------- |
| Economic coordination         | Strongly _Market coordination_                                               | Yes     |
| Design vs. evolved order      | Strongly _Evolved, spontaneous order_ – Leans _Evolved, spontaneous order_   | Yes     |
| Money and public finance      | Leans _Rules and fiscal discipline_ – Strongly _Rules and fiscal discipline_ | Yes     |
| Property in capital and goods | Strongly _Robust private ownership_ – Leans _Robust private ownership_       | No      |
| Distribution                  | Strongly _Market entitlement_ – Leans _Market entitlement_                   | No      |
| Welfare and social insurance  | Strongly _Self-reliance and voluntary provision_ – Middle                    | No      |
| Trade and economic openness   | Strongly _Open exchange_                                                     | No      |
| Individuals or wholes         | Strongly _Only individuals are real_ – Leans _Only individuals are real_     | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** The knowledge an economy runs on is scattered across millions of
people, much of it local and unspoken, and no planner can gather it. Prices are how that
knowledge travels: they tell everyone what is scarce and what is wanted, and only free
exchange of private property produces them. When governments or central banks distort
prices, above all the price of credit by creating cheap money, they lure entrepreneurs
into investments that cannot last, and the bust is the painful correction. Sound money
and free markets let the economy coordinate itself.

**Key concepts.**

- **Knowledge:** dispersed, tacit and local, so central planning fails for want of it,
  not for want of good intentions (the knowledge problem).
- **Economic calculation:** without market prices for capital goods, a planner cannot
  tell which uses of resources are rational.
- **The business cycle:** artificially low interest rates cause malinvestment; the bust
  liquidates it, and stimulus only delays the reckoning.
- **Money:** should be sound and beyond political manipulation, whether by a commodity
  standard, free banking, or competing private currencies.
- **Method:** economics deduces from the logic of purposeful human action and studies
  market processes over time, rather than modelling equilibrium aggregates.

**Signature reasons.**

- "Prices carry information no central planner could ever collect."
- "The boom, not the bust, is the problem."
- "Cheap credit from the central bank sends investment into things nobody needs."
- "You can't calculate rationally without market prices."
- "Inflation is a hidden tax and a theft from savers."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Chicago school:** also pro-market, but empirical and aggregate in method, and content
  with a fiat central bank following a money-growth rule; the Austrian school distrusts
  both the method and the institution (Reasons; _Design vs. evolved order_).
- **Keynesianism:** treats slumps as shortfalls of demand to be cured by spending
  (_Money and public finance_; _Design vs. evolved order_).
- **Ordoliberalism:** shares the case for markets and rules, but wants a strong state to
  design and police the competitive order (_Design vs. evolved order_).
- **Libertarianism:** a theory of rights and the limits of state power; the Austrian
  school is an economic tradition whose members range from Hayek's limited state to
  Rothbard's anarcho-capitalism (_Political authority_ is left open here).

**Sources.** Carl Menger, _Principles of Economics_ (1871); Eugen von Böhm-Bawerk,
_Capital and Interest_ (1884–89); Ludwig von Mises, _The Theory of Money and Credit_
(1912), "Economic Calculation in the Socialist Commonwealth" (1920) and _Human Action_
(1949); F. A. Hayek, _Prices and Production_ (1931), "The Use of Knowledge in Society"
(1945), "The Pretence of Knowledge" (1974) and _Denationalisation of Money_ (1976);
Murray Rothbard, _Man, Economy, and State_ (1962) and _America's Great Depression_
(1963); Israel Kirzner, _Competition and Entrepreneurship_ (1973); Peter Boettke (ed.),
_The Elgar Companion to Austrian Economics_ (1994).

## Modern Monetary Theory

- **Type:** Ideology (usually Area)
- **Parent:** —
- **Also called:** MMT; neo-chartalism; functional finance (its root in Lerner)
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                         | Range                                                                    | Central |
| --------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------- |
| Money and public finance          | Strongly _Discretion and active management_                              | Yes     |
| Welfare and social insurance      | Leans _Public social insurance_ – Strongly _Public social insurance_     | No      |
| Reciprocity in support            | Middle – Leans _Conditional (reciprocal) support_                        | No      |
| Economic coordination             | Leans _Public direction_ – Middle                                        | No      |
| Distribution                      | Middle – Leans _Egalitarian redistribution_                              | No      |
| Sovereignty vs. pooled governance | Middle – Leans _Sovereign self-government_                               | No      |
| Work                              | Strongly _Work is a source of worth_ – Leans _Work is a source of worth_ | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** A government that issues its own currency is not like a household:
it cannot run out of the money it creates, and it does not need to tax or borrow before
it spends. The real limit on its spending is the economy's capacity to produce, which
shows up as inflation when spending outruns it. So the question is never "can we afford
it?" but "do we have the workers and resources?" Taxes exist to control inflation and
give the currency value, not to fund the state. Unemployment is a policy choice, and a
public job guarantee can end it.

**Key concepts.**

- **Currency sovereignty:** a state that issues a floating currency and borrows in it
  cannot be forced into default, unlike a eurozone member or a household.
- **Inflation:** the true budget constraint; spending is too high only when it pushes
  against real resources.
- **Taxes:** drive demand for the currency and drain spending to fight inflation; they do
  not fund spending.
- **Job guarantee:** a public offer of a living-wage job to anyone who wants one, acting
  as an automatic stabiliser and a price anchor.
- **Deficits:** the private sector's surplus; a government deficit is the non-government
  sector's saving.

**Signature reasons.**

- "The government isn't a household. It issues the currency."
- "The question isn't how we pay for it. It's whether we have the real resources."
- "Taxes don't fund spending. They make room for it."
- "Mass unemployment is a political choice."
- "Everyone who wants to work should be offered a job."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Keynesianism:** also backs deficits in slumps, but treats public debt as a real
  long-run constraint and borrowing as funding (Reasons on _Money and public finance_).
- **Fiscal conservatism:** a household view of public finance, with balanced budgets as a
  rule (_Money and public finance_, the central Dimension).
- **Austrian school:** sees fiat money creation as the source of booms and busts
  (_Money and public finance_).
- **Social democracy:** shares the welfare aims, but funds them through taxation and
  borrowing within fiscal rules (Reasons).
- **Left accelerationism:** wants a post-work society with an unconditional basic
  income; most MMT economists prefer a job guarantee to unconditional cash
  (_Reciprocity in support_; _Work_).

**Sources.** Georg Friedrich Knapp, _The State Theory of Money_ (1905; trans. 1924); Abba
Lerner, "Functional Finance and the Federal Debt" (1943); Warren Mosler, _Soft Currency
Economics_ (1995); L. Randall Wray, _Understanding Modern Money_ (1998) and _Modern
Money Theory_ (2012); William Mitchell, L. Randall Wray & Martin Watts, _Macroeconomics_
(2019); Stephanie Kelton, _The Deficit Myth_ (2020); Pavlina Tcherneva, _The Case for a
Job Guarantee_ (2020).

## Economic nationalism

- **Type:** Ideology (usually Area)
- **Parent:** Nationalism
- **Also called:** national developmentalism; the American System (Hamilton, Clay);
  national economy (List); industrial-policy conservatism (its current US form); critics:
  "protectionism"
- **Variants:** Mercantilism
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                                   | Range                                                                                            | Central |
| ------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------- |
| Trade and economic openness                 | Leans _Economic protection and self-reliance_ – Strongly _Economic protection and self-reliance_ | Yes     |
| Economic coordination                       | Leans _Public direction_ – Middle                                                                | Yes     |
| Compatriot priority vs. global impartiality | Strongly _Special obligations to compatriots_ – Leans _Special obligations to compatriots_       | No      |
| Sovereignty vs. pooled governance           | Leans _Sovereign self-government_ – Strongly _Sovereign self-government_                         | No      |
| Zero-sum or positive-sum                    | Leans _One's gain is another's loss_ – Middle                                                    | No      |
| Growth                                      | Leans _Growth and abundance_ – Strongly _Growth and abundance_                                   | No      |
| Work                                        | Strongly _Work is a source of worth_ – Leans _Work is a source of worth_                         | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** A nation's wealth is its power to produce, not the cheapness of
what it can buy. Every country that became rich, Britain and America first among them,
did so by protecting and nurturing its own industries until they could compete, then
preached free trade to others. A nation that lets its factories move abroad loses the
skills, communities and security that come with making things. So the state should use
tariffs, procurement and industrial policy to build a strong productive base at home,
judging trade by what it does for the nation's workers and strength.

**Key concepts.**

- **Productive power:** the nation's capacity to make things, the real source of wealth
  and independence (List), as against consumer welfare.
- **Infant industries:** new industries need protection until they can stand against
  established foreign rivals.
- **Free trade:** a policy that suits the already-dominant; "kicking away the ladder"
  that others climbed.
- **Security:** dependence on rivals for essential goods (steel, chips, medicines,
  energy) is a strategic danger.
- **Work:** good industrial jobs sustain families and communities, not only incomes.

**Signature reasons.**

- "A country that doesn't make things doesn't stay strong."
- "Every rich country protected its industries on the way up."
- "Cheap imports aren't cheap if they cost us our jobs and our towns."
- "We shouldn't depend on our rivals for what we need in a crisis."
- "Trade should serve the nation, not the other way round."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Developmental state:** also state-guided industrialisation, but disciplines firms by
  export success in world markets through an insulated bureaucracy, rather than by
  protecting the home market (_Trade and economic openness_; _Popular judgment vs.
  expertise_).
- **Dependency theory:** a structural critique of the world economy from the periphery,
  often socialist; economic nationalism is a national strategy any country, rich or
  poor, can adopt (_Zero-sum or positive-sum_; Reasons).
- **Market liberalism:** open trade and capital flows as the path to prosperity (_Trade
  and economic openness_, the central Dimension).
- **National conservatism and National populism:** often carry economic nationalism as
  their economic Area, alongside a cultural account of the nation (_Basis of
  nationhood_), which economic nationalism leaves open.
- **Mercantilism (variant):** the historical doctrine of trade surpluses and bullion
  accumulation; living holders match Economic nationalism.

**Sources.** Alexander Hamilton, _Report on Manufactures_ (1791); Friedrich List, _The
National System of Political Economy_ (1841); Henry C. Carey, _The Harmony of Interests_
(1851); Ha-Joon Chang, _Kicking Away the Ladder_ (2002); Oren Cass, _The Once and Future
Worker_ (2018); Eric Helleiner, _The Neomercantilists_ (2021); Robert Lighthizer, _No
Trade Is Free_ (2023).

## Dependency theory

- **Type:** Ideology (usually Area)
- **Parent:** —
- **Also called:** dependency school (_dependencia_); Latin American structuralism (its
  ECLA strand, Prebisch); world-systems analysis (Wallerstein, a close neighbour)
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                         | Range                                                                                            | Central |
| --------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------- |
| Trade and economic openness       | Leans _Economic protection and self-reliance_ – Strongly _Economic protection and self-reliance_ | Yes     |
| Zero-sum or positive-sum          | Strongly _One's gain is another's loss_ – Leans _One's gain is another's loss_                   | Yes     |
| Economic coordination             | Strongly _Public direction_ – Middle                                                             | No      |
| Causes of fortune                 | Leans _Luck and structure_ – Strongly _Luck and structure_                                       | No      |
| Sovereignty vs. pooled governance | Leans _Sovereign self-government_ – Strongly _Sovereign self-government_                         | No      |
| Historical injustice              | Middle – Leans _Rectification_                                                                   | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Poor countries are not simply behind rich ones on the same road.
They were drawn into the world economy as suppliers of raw materials and cheap labour,
and the terms of that exchange keep transferring value from the periphery to the core.
Following the advice of the rich, specialising in commodities and opening up, deepens
that dependence. Real development needs the periphery to build its own industries, plan
its economy and reduce its reliance on the core, through import substitution, regional
cooperation and, for many, socialism.

**Key concepts.**

- **Core and periphery:** the world economy is one structured system in which the
  industrial core's development and the periphery's underdevelopment are two sides of
  the same process.
- **Terms of trade:** prices of primary commodities tend to fall relative to
  manufactures, so commodity exporters run to stand still (Prebisch–Singer).
- **Unequal exchange:** trade between high-wage and low-wage economies transfers value
  to the core.
- **Underdevelopment:** an outcome of how a country was integrated into the world
  system, not an original state of backwardness.
- **Import substitution:** replacing imported manufactures with domestic production,
  behind tariffs and with planning.

**Signature reasons.**

- "Underdevelopment isn't a stage. It's what the system produces."
- "The rich countries got rich partly by keeping us as their mines and plantations."
- "Exporting raw materials means falling behind forever."
- "Free trade between unequals keeps them unequal."
- "Development means breaking dependence, not deepening integration."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Economic nationalism:** a national growth strategy any state can adopt, with no
  claim that the world system extracts value from the periphery (_Zero-sum or
  positive-sum_; Reasons).
- **Developmental state:** pursued export-led integration into world markets, the
  opposite strategy, and its success is a main argument against dependency theory
  (_Trade and economic openness_).
- **Marxism:** class struggle within societies is the motor; dependency theory sets
  nations and regions in a world system at the centre, and some Marxists (Warren) held
  that capitalism develops the periphery (Reasons).
- **Postcolonialism:** colonial power persisting in knowledge and culture; dependency
  theory is about the structure of the world economy (Reasons).
- **Anti-imperialism:** a Thin Label that can ride on dependency theory as its economic
  analysis.

**Sources.** Raúl Prebisch, _The Economic Development of Latin America and Its
Principal Problems_ (1950); Hans Singer, "The Distribution of Gains between Investing
and Borrowing Countries" (1950); André Gunder Frank, "The Development of
Underdevelopment" (1966); Fernando Henrique Cardoso & Enzo Faletto, _Dependency and
Development in Latin America_ (1969; trans. 1979); Arghiri Emmanuel, _Unequal Exchange_
(1969; trans. 1972); Walter Rodney, _How Europe Underdeveloped Africa_ (1972); Samir
Amin, _Unequal Development_ (1973; trans. 1976); Immanuel Wallerstein, _The Modern
World-System_ (1974).

## Developmental state

- **Type:** Ideology (usually Area)
- **Parent:** —
- **Also called:** the East Asian model; the governed market (Wade); state-led
  capitalism
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                      | Range                                                                                  | Central |
| ------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------- |
| Economic coordination          | Leans _Public direction_ – Middle                                                      | Yes     |
| Popular judgment vs. expertise | Strongly _Rule by the knowledgeable_ – Leans _Rule by the knowledgeable_               | Yes     |
| Growth                         | Strongly _Growth and abundance_                                                        | Yes     |
| Trade and economic openness    | Leans _Open exchange_ – Leans _Economic protection and self-reliance_                  | No      |
| Environmental protection       | Strongly _Prosperity and development first_ – Leans _Prosperity and development first_ | No      |
| Welfare and social insurance   | Leans _Self-reliance and voluntary provision_ – Middle                                 | No      |
| Pluralism vs. unity            | Middle – Leans _Unified common purpose_                                                | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Late developers cannot simply wait for markets to industrialise
them, but planners who replace markets fail too. What worked in Japan, South Korea,
Taiwan and Singapore was a capable, meritocratic bureaucracy, insulated from short-term
politics, that picked strategic industries, steered credit to them and held the firms it
helped to hard targets, above all success in export markets. Growth came first, as a
national project that united state and business. Private enterprise did the producing;
the state set the direction.

**Key concepts.**

- **The plan-rational state:** sets national economic goals and guides private industry
  toward them, unlike the market-rational state that only sets rules.
- **Embedded autonomy:** a bureaucracy close enough to business to know it and
  independent enough not to be captured by it (Evans).
- **Export discipline:** subsidies and protection in exchange for performance in
  world markets, so support does not breed lazy monopolies.
- **Meritocracy:** officials recruited by examination and merit, with the prestige and
  insulation to plan for the long term.
- **Growth as legitimacy:** rapid, shared development as the state's justification and
  the nation's purpose.

**Signature reasons.**

- "No country industrialised by leaving it all to the market."
- "Help firms, but make them earn it in export markets."
- "You need a bureaucracy of the best and brightest, not political appointees."
- "Growth first. Everything else follows from it."
- "Industrial policy works when it is disciplined."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Economic nationalism:** protects the home market; the developmental state used
  protection too, but tied support to export performance (_Trade and economic openness_;
  Reasons).
- **Socialism with Chinese characteristics:** a Marxist party-state with large public
  ownership; the developmental state is capitalist, with private firms steered by a
  bureaucracy (_Property in capital and goods_; Reasons).
- **Technocracy:** expert rule over government as a whole; the developmental state is an
  economic strategy and can sit within an electoral democracy (Reasons).
- **Market liberalism:** sees East Asian growth as the reward of openness and sound
  fundamentals, and industrial policy as a distraction (_Economic coordination_).
- **Asian values:** a cultural and political case for order and community that often
  accompanied developmental states; this Label covers only the economic strategy.

**Sources.** Chalmers Johnson, _MITI and the Japanese Miracle_ (1982); Alice Amsden,
_Asia's Next Giant_ (1989); Robert Wade, _Governing the Market_ (1990); Peter Evans,
_Embedded Autonomy_ (1995); Meredith Woo-Cumings (ed.), _The Developmental State_
(1999); Ha-Joon Chang, _Kicking Away the Ladder_ (2002); Joe Studwell, _How Asia Works_
(2013).

## Georgism

- **Type:** Ideology
- **Parent:** Classical liberalism
- **Also called:** geoism; the single-tax movement; geolibertarianism (its libertarian
  wing); land value taxation (its policy)
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                     | Range                                                                            | Central |
| ----------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------- |
| Land and natural resources    | Strongly _Common inheritance_                                                    | Yes     |
| Economic coordination         | Leans _Market coordination_ – Strongly _Market coordination_                     | No      |
| Property in capital and goods | Strongly _Robust private ownership_ – Leans _Robust private ownership_           | No      |
| Trade and economic openness   | Strongly _Open exchange_                                                         | No      |
| Desert                        | Strongly _Earned_ – Leans _Earned_                                               | No      |
| Distribution                  | Leans _Market entitlement_ – Leans _Egalitarian redistribution_                  | No      |
| Rights: natural or man-made   | Strongly _Rights exist before the state_ – Leans _Rights exist before the state_ | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** What people make with their labour and capital is rightly theirs,
and taxing it punishes work and enterprise. But no one made the land. Its value comes
from nature and from the community around it, from roads, schools and neighbours, so
the rent that landowners collect is unearned and belongs to everyone. Tax the value of
land, and you can abolish taxes on wages, trade and buildings, end speculation that
keeps land idle and housing scarce, and share the common inheritance without the state
running the economy.

**Key concepts.**

- **Land:** all natural opportunities (soil, sites, minerals, the spectrum), distinct
  from capital because no one produced it.
- **Economic rent:** the income land commands because of location and scarcity, created
  by the community, not by the owner.
- **The single tax:** a tax on the unimproved value of land, which cannot be passed on or
  dodged and does not discourage production.
- **Property:** a natural right to the fruits of one's labour, which does not extend to
  exclusive, untaxed ownership of the earth.
- **Poverty amid progress:** as economies grow, rising land values capture the gains, so
  wages lag behind wealth.

**Signature reasons.**

- "Nobody made the land, so nobody should pocket its rent."
- "Tax what people take, not what they make."
- "Land values are created by the community, so they should go to the community."
- "Speculators sitting on empty land are why housing is unaffordable."
- "Free trade, free enterprise, and the land for all."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Left-libertarianism (Steiner–Vallentyne):** a philosophical theory joining full
  self-ownership to egalitarian shares in natural resources; Georgism reaches a similar
  result as an economic programme centred on the land tax (Reasons; the library notes
  the overlap).
- **Classical liberalism:** treats land like any other property (_Land and natural
  resources_, the central Dimension).
- **Socialism:** would socialise capital as well as land (_Property in capital and
  goods_).
- **Abundance liberalism:** shares the housing focus and often backs land value tax, but
  its centre is clearing veto points to build, not the land question (Reasons).
- **Three Principles of the People:** Sun Yat-sen borrowed Georgist land policy into a
  nationalist, developmental programme (Reasons).

**Sources.** Thomas Paine, _Agrarian Justice_ (1797); John Stuart Mill, _Principles of
Political Economy_ (1848), book V; Henry George, _Progress and Poverty_ (1879) and
_Protection or Free Trade_ (1886); Joseph Stiglitz, "The Theory of Local Public Goods"
(1977) (the Henry George theorem); Mason Gaffney & Fred Harrison, _The Corruption of
Economics_ (1994); Lars Doucet, _Land Is a Big Deal_ (2023).

## Neo-Brandeisianism

- **Type:** Ideology (usually Area)
- **Parent:** Progressivism / Civic republicanism
- **Also called:** the New Brandeis movement; the anti-monopoly movement; critics:
  "hipster antitrust"
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                        | Range                                                                                        | Central |
| -------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------- |
| Economic scale and concentration | Leans _Dispersed ownership and small scale_ – Strongly _Dispersed ownership and small scale_ | Yes     |
| Economic coordination            | Leans _Public direction_ – Leans _Market coordination_                                       | No      |
| Workplace governance             | Leans _Worker voice and self-management_ – Middle                                            | No      |
| Rule of law vs. discretion       | Leans _Rule by general, fixed rules_ – Strongly _Rule by general, fixed rules_               | No      |
| Distribution                     | Middle – Leans _Egalitarian redistribution_                                                  | No      |
| Popular judgment vs. expertise   | Middle – Leans _Equal popular judgment_                                                      | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Concentrated private power is a political danger, not only an
economic one. When a few corporations dominate a market, they can set terms for
workers, suppliers and small businesses, buy influence over government, and decide
what citizens see and say. For forty years antitrust asked only whether consumer prices
rose, and so let giants grow. It should return to its older purpose: keeping markets
open and power dispersed, with clear structural rules, so that economic life remains
compatible with self-government.

**Key concepts.**

- **Monopoly:** a form of private government over workers, suppliers and citizens, not
  only a source of high prices.
- **Market structure:** antitrust should protect competitive structure and open
  markets, not just short-run consumer welfare.
- **Bright-line rules:** structural separations and presumptions against mergers, rather
  than case-by-case economic modelling that favours the powerful.
- **Democracy:** citizens cannot govern themselves while private concentrations hold
  power comparable to the state's.
- **Platforms:** dominant digital platforms that both host and compete with businesses
  should be separated or regulated like utilities.

**Signature reasons.**

- "We can have democracy, or we can have great wealth concentrated in a few hands, but
  we can't have both." (attributed to Brandeis)
- "Low prices don't make a monopoly harmless."
- "Big Tech has too much power over our economy and our democracy."
- "Antitrust is about power, not just prices."
- "Break them up."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Chicago school:** judges antitrust by consumer welfare and tolerates efficient size
  (_Economic scale and concentration_, the central Dimension).
- **Ordoliberalism:** also wants a strong state to police concentration, but its aim is
  a functioning competitive order and economic freedom, where the neo-Brandeisian aim is
  democratic self-government and anti-domination (Reasons).
- **Distributism:** wants property widely held in family firms and farms, from Catholic
  social teaching (Reasons; _Transcendence_).
- **Democratic socialism:** would take dominant firms into public or worker ownership
  rather than break them up (_Property in capital and goods_).
- **Abundance liberalism:** its rival on concentration: it favours building at scale,
  where neo-Brandeisians favour breaking up scale (_Economic scale and concentration_).

**Sources.** Louis D. Brandeis, _Other People's Money and How the Bankers Use It_ (1914)
and _The Curse of Bigness_ (1934); Barry Lynn, _Cornered_ (2010) and _Liberty from All
Masters_ (2020); K. Sabeel Rahman, _Democracy Against Domination_ (2016); Lina Khan,
"Amazon's Antitrust Paradox" (2017); Tim Wu, _The Curse of Bigness_ (2018); Zephyr
Teachout, _Break 'Em Up_ (2020).

## Neo-corporatism

- **Type:** Ideology (usually Area)
- **Parent:** Christian democracy / Social democracy
- **Also called:** liberal or societal corporatism (Schmitter, Lehmbruch); social
  partnership; tripartism; concertation
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                    | Range                                                                                        | Central |
| ---------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------- |
| Majoritarian vs. consensus   | Leans _Power-sharing and broad consensus_ – Strongly _Power-sharing and broad consensus_     | Yes     |
| Workplace governance         | Strongly _Worker voice and self-management_ – Middle                                         | Yes     |
| Pluralism vs. unity          | Strongly _Legitimate pluralism and opposition_ – Leans _Legitimate pluralism and opposition_ | Yes     |
| Economic coordination        | Leans _Public direction_ – Middle                                                            | No      |
| Civil society vs. the state  | Leans _Family, community, and voluntary association_ – Middle                                | No      |
| Welfare and social insurance | Leans _Public social insurance_ – Strongly _Public social insurance_                         | No      |
| Individuals or wholes        | Middle – Leans _Groups and relations are real_                                               | No      |
| Tempo of change              | Leans _Incremental reform_ – Strongly _Incremental reform_                                   | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Capital and labour need not be enemies. When strong, independent
unions and employers' federations bargain with each other and with government over
wages, training and social policy, the economy gains wage restraint, industrial peace
and high skills, and workers gain a real voice. Many of the most successful small
economies in Europe work this way. Organised interests sharing responsibility for the
economy is a democratic achievement, provided the associations stay free and
self-governing.

**Key concepts.**

- **Social partners:** unions and employers' associations as recognised, responsible
  participants in economic governance, not just lobbies.
- **Concertation:** tripartite bargaining among state, capital and labour over incomes
  and policy.
- **Co-determination:** worker representatives on company boards and works councils.
- **Voluntary association:** the partners are autonomous organisations that members
  join freely; the state convenes them but does not own them.
- **Coordinated market economy:** markets embedded in institutions of long-term
  cooperation, as against arm's-length liberal markets.

**Signature reasons.**

- "Strong unions and responsible employers can sit at the same table."
- "Wage restraint in exchange for jobs and social security works for everyone."
- "Workers should have seats on the board."
- "Small open economies survive by cooperating, not fighting."
- "The social partners know their industries better than any ministry."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Fascism and Falangism:** state corporatism, in which compulsory syndicates are
  organs of a one-party state and independent unions are abolished (_Pluralism vs.
  unity_, a central Dimension here).
- **Ordoliberalism:** wary of organised interests bending the market order to their
  advantage (_Economic coordination_; Reasons).
- **Consociationalism:** shares power among religious, ethnic and linguistic segments,
  not among economic classes (Reasons; _Group difference_).
- **Social democracy and Christian democracy:** the host traditions that practised it;
  neo-corporatism is their shared method for economic governance (Reasons).

**Sources.** Pius XI, _Quadragesimo Anno_ (1931); Philippe Schmitter, "Still the
Century of Corporatism?" (1974); Gerhard Lehmbruch & Philippe Schmitter (eds.), _Patterns
of Corporatist Policy-Making_ (1982); Peter Katzenstein, _Small States in World Markets_
(1985); Wolfgang Streeck, _Social Institutions and Economic Performance_ (1992); Peter
Hall & David Soskice (eds.), _Varieties of Capitalism_ (2001).

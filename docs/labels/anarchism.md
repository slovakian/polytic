# Anarchism: Label cards

Cards for this family, written to the template in `../label-library.md`, in the order
of the list. Every card here is written with the same dispassion as any other: the
self-description is the tradition's own self-understanding, steelmanned, and never
Polytic's verdict (`../principles.md`). Where a tradition is defined partly by violent
strategy, the card describes it, and probes stay historical or abstract, never calls to
action (`../label-library.md`).

## Anarchism

- **Type:** Ideology
- **Parent:** —
- **Also called:** libertarianism (its original nineteenth-century sense, still current
  in Europe); the libertarian movement
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                        | Range                                                                                                          | Central |
| -------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------- |
| Political authority              | Leans _Voluntary order_ – Strongly _Voluntary order_                                                           | Yes     |
| Social hierarchy                 | Strongly _Equality of status_ – Leans _Equality of status_                                                     | Yes     |
| Leadership                       | Strongly _Dispersed, checked authority_                                                                        | No      |
| Local vs. central                | Strongly _Local and regional self-rule_ – Leans _Local and regional self-rule_                                 | No      |
| Civil society vs. the state      | Strongly _Family, community, and voluntary association_ – Leans _Family, community, and voluntary association_ | No      |
| Representation vs. participation | Strongly _Direct and participatory self-rule_ – Leans _Direct and participatory self-rule_                     | No      |
| Security and civil liberties     | Strongly _Civil liberties and due process_ – Leans _Civil liberties and due process_                           | No      |
| Immigration and borders          | Leans _Freedom of movement_ – Strongly _Freedom of movement_                                                   | No      |
| Use of force                     | Leans _Reluctance to use force_ – Strongly _Reluctance to use force_                                           | No      |
| Paternalism                      | Leans _Choice_ – Strongly _Choice_                                                                             | No      |
| Legal moralism                   | Strongly _Liberty of self-regarding conduct_ – Leans _Liberty of self-regarding conduct_                       | No      |
| Punishment                       | Leans _Rehabilitation and restoration_ – Strongly _Rehabilitation and restoration_                             | No      |
| Channels of change               | Middle – Strongly _Beyond institutions_                                                                        | No      |
| Hierarchy or equality            | Leans _Equality is basic_ – Strongly _Equality is basic_                                                       | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** No one has a natural right to rule another. The state claims a
monopoly on legitimate force, and like other hierarchies (boss over worker, priest over
believer, patriarch over family) it must justify itself, which it cannot. People are
able to run their lives through voluntary co-operation, mutual aid, and free federation,
and they already do so every day. A society without rulers is not chaos. It is order
without domination.

**Key concepts.**

- **Authority:** coercive authority that cannot justify itself is domination, whether
  it is the state's, the employer's, or the church's.
- **Freedom:** the equal freedom of all, inseparable from equality; no one is free while
  others are ruled.
- **Mutual aid:** co-operation is as natural to people as competition, and can sustain
  social order without coercion.
- **Direct action:** acting for oneself and with others, rather than through
  representatives.
- **Prefiguration:** the means must match the ends; a free society cannot be built
  through domination.

**Signature reasons.**

- "Anarchy is order without rulers."
- "No gods, no masters."
- "You can't reach freedom by way of domination."
- "People co-operate every day without anyone forcing them to."
- "Every authority must justify itself, and the state can't."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Anarcho-capitalism:** also rejects the state, but defends private property in
  capital and wage labour as voluntary, which most anarchists see as hierarchy in
  another form (_Workplace governance_, _Property in capital and goods_); it sits under
  Libertarianism, not here.
- **Marxism:** shares the stateless end goal, but accepts a transitional workers' state
  and a materialist theory of history (_Political authority_ in the transition; Reasons).
- **Libertarian socialism:** a wider family that includes non-anarchist socialists who
  would still use a democratic state for now (_Political authority_).
- **Philosophical anarchism:** denies the state's legitimacy without seeking to abolish
  it now (_Tempo of change_, _Channels of change_).

**Sources.** William Godwin, _Enquiry Concerning Political Justice_ (1793); Pierre-Joseph
Proudhon, _What Is Property?_ (1840); Mikhail Bakunin, _God and the State_ (1882);
Errico Malatesta, _Anarchy_ (1891); Peter Kropotkin, _Mutual Aid_ (1902) and "Anarchism",
_Encyclopaedia Britannica_ (1910); Emma Goldman, _Anarchism and Other Essays_ (1910);
Peter Marshall, _Demanding the Impossible_ (1992); Ruth Kinna, _The Government of No One_
(2019).

## Philosophical anarchism

- **Type:** Ideology
- **Parent:** Anarchism
- **Also called:** —
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                      | Range                                                                                  | Central |
| ------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------- |
| Political authority            | Leans _Voluntary order_ – Strongly _Voluntary order_                                   | Yes     |
| Tempo of change                | Middle – Strongly _Incremental reform_                                                 | Yes     |
| Channels of change             | Strongly _Within institutions_ – Middle                                                | No      |
| Citizenship: rights vs. duties | Leans _Citizenship as protected liberty_ – Strongly _Citizenship as protected liberty_ | No      |
| Self-authorship or given role  | Strongly _Author your own life_ – Leans _Author your own life_                         | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Autonomy means acting on one's own judgment, and no state has
shown that it holds a general right to be obeyed. Hardly anyone consented to it, and the
arguments from fair play, gratitude, or benefit fail to bind each citizen to every law.
So there is no general duty to obey the law as such: we should do what laws require
only when we have independent reasons to. This is a claim about legitimacy, not a
revolutionary programme. States may still do much good, and tearing them down could
well do more harm than good.

**Key concepts.**

- **Legitimacy:** a state's right to rule and be obeyed, which is distinct from its
  justification (being beneficial on balance). A state can be useful without being
  legitimate.
- **Political obligation:** the moral duty to obey the law because it is the law, which
  philosophical anarchists deny anyone has shown.
- **Autonomy:** the duty to act on one's own moral judgment, which no authority can
  displace.
- **Consent:** the only clear source of authority over a person, and one most citizens
  never gave.

**Signature reasons.**

- "No state has earned a general right to my obedience."
- "I follow good laws because they're good, not because they're laws."
- "A state can be useful without being legitimate."
- "Abolishing the state tomorrow would probably be a disaster. That doesn't make it
  legitimate."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Anarchism**, in its revolutionary forms: seeks to abolish the state; philosophical
  anarchism only denies its legitimacy (_Tempo of change_, _Channels of change_).
- **Minarchism:** holds that a night-watchman state is legitimate (_Political
  authority_).
- **Liberalism:** holds that consent-based, accountable government is legitimate
  (_Political authority_).
- **Christian anarchism:** rejects the state's authority on religious grounds and refuses
  its violence (_Transcendence_, _Force in domestic politics_).

**Sources.** William Godwin, _Enquiry Concerning Political Justice_ (1793); Robert Paul
Wolff, _In Defense of Anarchism_ (1970); A. John Simmons, _Moral Principles and Political
Obligations_ (1979) and "Justification and Legitimacy", _Ethics_ 109 (1999); Leslie
Green, _The Authority of the State_ (1988); Michael Huemer, _The Problem of Political
Authority_ (2013).

## Individualist anarchism

- **Type:** Ideology
- **Parent:** Anarchism
- **Also called:** egoist anarchism (its Stirnerian form); American individualist
  anarchism (Warren, Spooner, Tucker)
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                       | Range                                                                    | Central |
| ------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------- |
| Political authority             | Strongly _Voluntary order_                                               | Yes     |
| Individuals or wholes           | Strongly _Only individuals are real_ – Leans _Only individuals are real_ | Yes     |
| Paternalism                     | Strongly _Choice_                                                        | No      |
| Legal moralism                  | Strongly _Liberty of self-regarding conduct_                             | No      |
| Speech and expression           | Strongly _Maximal expressive freedom_                                    | No      |
| Self-authorship or given role   | Strongly _Author your own life_                                          | No      |
| Self-interest or duty to others | Strongly _Rational self-interest_ – Middle                               | No      |
| Economic coordination           | Middle – Strongly _Market coordination_                                  | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Each person is sovereign over themselves. No state, church,
society, or moral code has a claim on me that I have not freely accepted, and "the
common good", "the nation", and even "humanity" are abstractions to which real
individuals get sacrificed. Free individuals should deal with one another by voluntary
exchange and agreement, a "union of egoists" in Stirner's phrase, owning what they make
and use, with neither rulers nor the monopolies that law grants to the privileged.

**Key concepts.**

- **Sovereignty of the individual:** each person is the sole rightful ruler of their own
  life (Warren).
- **Egoism:** for Stirner, "ownness"; fixed ideas such as morality, the state, and
  society are "spooks" that dominate the individual.
- **Voluntary association:** relationships entered and left by choice, including the
  "union of egoists".
- **Monopoly:** for Tucker, the state's grants of privilege over money, land, tariffs,
  and patents are what let capitalists collect interest, rent, and profit.

**Signature reasons.**

- "I have based my affair on nothing." (Stirner's opening line)
- "Society, the state, morality: these are spooks that demand my sacrifice."
- "Every individual is sovereign over their own person and property."
- "Abolish the state's monopolies and free exchange will end exploitation."
- "Associate freely, and leave when you choose."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Mutualism:** shares free exchange and possession by use, but centres producers'
  associations and federation, not the sovereign individual (_Individuals or wholes_;
  Reasons).
- **Anarcho-capitalism:** also individualist and pro-market, but defends rent, interest,
  and wage labour, which Tucker's individualists held to be the fruit of state privilege
  (_Property in capital and goods_; Reasons).
- **Objectivism:** shares egoism, but defends a limited state and capitalism (_Political
  authority_).
- **Anarcho-communism:** collective ownership and distribution by need (_Individuals or
  wholes_, _Economic coordination_).

**Sources.** Max Stirner, _The Ego and Its Own_ (1844); Josiah Warren, _Equitable
Commerce_ (1846); Lysander Spooner, _No Treason_ (1867–70); Benjamin R. Tucker, "State
Socialism and Anarchism" (1886) and _Instead of a Book_ (1893); James J. Martin, _Men
Against the State_ (1953); David Leopold, "Max Stirner", _Stanford Encyclopedia of
Philosophy_.

## Mutualism

- **Type:** Ideology
- **Parent:** Anarchism
- **Also called:** Proudhonism; free-market anti-capitalism (its contemporary form)
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                        | Range                                                                                        | Central |
| -------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------- |
| Political authority              | Leans _Voluntary order_ – Strongly _Voluntary order_                                         | Yes     |
| Economic coordination            | Leans _Market coordination_ – Strongly _Market coordination_                                 | Yes     |
| Property in capital and goods    | Leans _Socially conditioned ownership_ – Strongly _Socially conditioned ownership_           | Yes     |
| Workplace governance             | Strongly _Worker voice and self-management_ – Leans _Worker voice and self-management_       | Yes     |
| Land and natural resources       | Strongly _Common inheritance_ – Leans _Common inheritance_                                   | No      |
| Economic scale and concentration | Leans _Dispersed ownership and small scale_ – Strongly _Dispersed ownership and small scale_ | No      |
| Local vs. central                | Strongly _Local and regional self-rule_                                                      | No      |
| Tempo of change                  | Middle – Leans _Incremental reform_                                                          | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Property, in the sense of a right to collect rent, interest, and
profit from other people's labour without working, is theft. Possession is something
else: the land you farm, the workshop you use, and the home you live in are the
guarantee of your liberty. Abolish the legal privileges that let owners and bankers
collect tribute, organise credit mutually and at cost, and let free producers and their
associations exchange the products of their labour on equal terms. Order then comes
from contract and federation, not from government.

**Key concepts.**

- **Property and possession:** absentee ownership that yields unearned income is
  illegitimate; possession by occupancy and use is legitimate and protects liberty.
- **Mutuality:** reciprocity among equals, in exchange, credit, and insurance.
- **Federation:** free contracts among individuals, associations, and communes, built
  from the bottom up instead of a state.
- **Credit:** mutual banking can provide credit at cost and so remove interest as a
  source of exploitation.

**Signature reasons.**

- "Property is theft." (Proudhon, of property as a right to unearned income)
- "Possession by use, not ownership by title."
- "Free markets, freed from privilege, would end capitalism."
- "Credit should be organised at cost, by and for the people who use it."
- "Federation from below, not government from above."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Anarcho-communism:** abolishes markets and money in favour of distribution by need
  (_Economic coordination_, _Reciprocity in support_).
- **Individualist anarchism:** overlaps in its Tuckerite form, but starts from the
  sovereign individual rather than producers' associations (_Individuals or wholes_;
  Reasons).
- **Anarcho-capitalism:** keeps markets but defends absentee ownership, rent, and wage
  labour (_Property in capital and goods_, _Workplace governance_).
- **Market socialism:** also pairs markets with non-capitalist ownership, but sets it up
  through the state (_Political authority_).
- **Co-operativism:** shares member ownership, but works within the existing state and
  economy (_Political authority_).

**Sources.** Pierre-Joseph Proudhon, _What Is Property?_ (1840), _General Idea of the
Revolution in the Nineteenth Century_ (1851), _The Principle of Federation_ (1863);
William B. Greene, _Mutual Banking_ (1850); K. Steven Vincent, _Pierre-Joseph Proudhon
and the Rise of French Republican Socialism_ (1984); Kevin A. Carson, _Studies in
Mutualist Political Economy_ (2007); Iain McKay (ed.), _Property Is Theft! A
Pierre-Joseph Proudhon Anthology_ (2011).

## Anarcho-communism

- **Type:** Ideology
- **Parent:** Anarchism / Libertarian socialism
- **Also called:** libertarian communism; communist anarchism; collectivist anarchism
  (Bakunin's form, which kept payment by labour); platformism (its organisational
  current)
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                        | Range                                                                          | Central |
| -------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------- |
| Political authority              | Strongly _Voluntary order_                                                     | Yes     |
| Property in capital and goods    | Strongly _Socially conditioned ownership_                                      | Yes     |
| Economic coordination            | Strongly _Public direction_ – Leans _Public direction_                         | Yes     |
| Distribution                     | Strongly _Egalitarian redistribution_                                          | No      |
| Reciprocity in support           | Strongly _Unconditional support_ – Leans _Unconditional support_               | No      |
| Moral limits of markets          | Strongly _Protected non-market spheres_                                        | No      |
| Land and natural resources       | Strongly _Common inheritance_                                                  | No      |
| Workplace governance             | Strongly _Worker voice and self-management_                                    | No      |
| Social hierarchy                 | Strongly _Equality of status_                                                  | No      |
| Local vs. central                | Strongly _Local and regional self-rule_ – Leans _Local and regional self-rule_ | No      |
| Representation vs. participation | Strongly _Direct and participatory self-rule_                                  | No      |
| Channels of change               | Leans _Beyond institutions_ – Strongly _Beyond institutions_                   | No      |
| Tempo of change                  | Strongly _Fundamental transformation_                                          | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Everything is produced by the common labour of past and present
generations, so everything belongs to everyone. Abolish the state, capital, and the wage
system together, and organise society as a free federation of communes and workers'
associations, where production is planned from below to meet needs and goods are shared
freely: from each according to ability, to each according to need. Mutual aid is as
much a law of nature as competition, and people freed from domination will practise it.

**Key concepts.**

- **Mutual aid:** co-operation as a factor of evolution and the basis of a free society.
- **Commune and federation:** self-governing communities and workplaces, linked by free
  agreement from the bottom up.
- **Need:** distribution by need, with wages and money abolished, since no one's share
  of a common product can be measured.
- **Freedom:** social freedom, achieved together with others, not against them.
- **Organisation:** for platformists, a federation with theoretical and tactical unity
  and collective responsibility.

**Signature reasons.**

- "All belongs to all."
- "From each according to ability, to each according to need."
- "Abolishing the state while keeping wages and bosses would only change the masters."
- "Mutual aid is a factor of evolution, not a naïve hope."
- "Bread for everyone before luxuries for anyone."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Marxism–Leninism:** shares the communist end, but takes the road of a party and a
  transitional state (_Political authority_, _Pluralism vs. unity_).
- **Anarcho-syndicalism:** makes revolutionary unions the vehicle and the organs of the
  new society, where anarcho-communism looks to communes (Reasons).
- **Mutualism:** keeps markets and exchange (_Economic coordination_, _Reciprocity in
  support_).
- **Council communism:** a Marxist current with a similar council structure and a
  materialist theory of history (Reasons).
- **Libertarian socialism:** its parent alongside Anarchism; anarcho-communism is the more
  specific Label, so it is the one shown when both fit.

**Sources.** Mikhail Bakunin, _Statism and Anarchy_ (1873); Errico Malatesta, _Anarchy_
(1891); Peter Kropotkin, _The Conquest of Bread_ (1892), _Fields, Factories and
Workshops_ (1899), _Mutual Aid_ (1902); Nestor Makhno, Piotr Arshinov, Ida Mett et al.,
_Organisational Platform of the General Union of Anarchists (Draft)_ (1926); Alexander
Berkman, _What Is Communist Anarchism?_ (1929); Michael Schmidt & Lucien van der Walt,
_Black Flame_ (2009).

## Anarcho-syndicalism

- **Type:** Ideology
- **Parent:** Anarchism / Libertarian socialism
- **Also called:** revolutionary syndicalism (a close relative, not always anarchist)
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                        | Range                                                                                      | Central |
| -------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------- |
| Workplace governance             | Strongly _Worker voice and self-management_                                                | Yes     |
| Channels of change               | Leans _Beyond institutions_ – Strongly _Beyond institutions_                               | Yes     |
| Political authority              | Leans _Voluntary order_ – Strongly _Voluntary order_                                       | Yes     |
| Property in capital and goods    | Strongly _Socially conditioned ownership_                                                  | No      |
| Economic coordination            | Strongly _Public direction_ – Leans _Public direction_                                     | No      |
| Representation vs. participation | Strongly _Direct and participatory self-rule_ – Leans _Direct and participatory self-rule_ | No      |
| Local vs. central                | Strongly _Local and regional self-rule_ – Leans _Local and regional self-rule_             | No      |
| Tempo of change                  | Strongly _Fundamental transformation_ – Leans _Fundamental transformation_                 | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Workers' power lies where they produce: in the workplace. Rather
than relying on parties or parliaments, workers should build revolutionary unions, run
from below by their members, that fight through direct action (strikes, boycotts, and
occupations) and prepare a general strike that ends capitalism and the state together.
Those same unions, federated by industry and locality, then become the organs through
which workers run production themselves, building the new society within the shell of
the old.

**Key concepts.**

- **Direct action:** workers acting for themselves at the point of production, without
  intermediaries.
- **The general strike:** the decisive act that ends capitalism by stopping it.
- **The union:** both the weapon of today's struggle and the seed of tomorrow's
  self-managed economy.
- **Federalism:** unions linked by industry and locality, with power flowing upward
  from the base.
- **Class:** the division between workers and owners, on which solidarity is built.

**Signature reasons.**

- "Build the new society within the shell of the old."
- "An injury to one is an injury to all."
- "Parliaments change faces; strikes change power."
- "The unions that win today's strikes will run tomorrow's factories."
- "We don't need bosses or politicians to run industry."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Libertarian socialism:** its parent alongside Anarchism; anarcho-syndicalism is the
  more specific Label, so it is the one shown when both fit.
- **Anarcho-communism:** looks to communes rather than unions as the basic unit of the
  new society (Reasons).
- **Council communism:** a Marxist current that sees unions as part of capitalism and
  puts power in workers' councils instead (Reasons).
- **Social democracy:** its unions bargain within capitalism and ally with a
  parliamentary party (_Channels of change_, _Political authority_).
- **Co-operativism:** builds worker-owned firms within the existing economy, without
  revolutionary aims (_Channels of change_, _Tempo of change_).
- **Falangism (national syndicalism):** uses the word "syndicalism" for state-run
  vertical unions that join employers and workers under the nation, the opposite of
  self-managed unions (_Political authority_, _Workplace governance_).

**Sources.** Industrial Workers of the World, Preamble (1905); CGT, Charter of Amiens
(1906); Fernand Pelloutier, _Histoire des Bourses du Travail_ (1902); Émile Pouget,
_Direct Action_ (1910); Diego Abad de Santillán, _After the Revolution_ (1937); Rudolf
Rocker, _Anarcho-Syndicalism: Theory and Practice_ (1938); Lucien van der Walt & Michael
Schmidt, _Black Flame_ (2009).

## Insurrectionary anarchism

- **Type:** Ideology
- **Parent:** Anarchism
- **Also called:** insurrectionism; informal anarchism
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                        | Range                                                                                        | Central |
| -------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------- |
| Channels of change               | Strongly _Beyond institutions_                                                               | Yes     |
| Force in domestic politics       | Strongly _Permissible force against injustice_ – Leans _Permissible force against injustice_ | Yes     |
| Political authority              | Strongly _Voluntary order_                                                                   | Yes     |
| Tempo of change                  | Strongly _Fundamental transformation_                                                        | No      |
| Representation vs. participation | Strongly _Direct and participatory self-rule_                                                | No      |
| Punishment                       | Leans _Rehabilitation and restoration_ – Strongly _Rehabilitation and restoration_           | No      |
| Self-authorship or given role    | Strongly _Author your own life_                                                              | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Formal organisations (parties, unions, federations with members and
programmes) end up mediating and managing revolt, and so reproduce the authority they
claim to fight. Liberation can't be delegated or postponed. It comes from small,
informal affinity groups acting now, linking up with spontaneous social revolts and
spreading conflict with the existing order, with no leaders, representatives, or
negotiations. What sustains it is joy in the struggle itself, not a programme for the
future.

**Key concepts.**

- **Insurrection:** an ongoing, spreading rupture with the existing order, rather than a
  single future revolution to wait for.
- **Affinity group:** a small circle of people who know and trust one another, acting
  together without formal structure.
- **Informality:** organisation without members, delegates, or programmes, so that no
  one can manage or represent the struggle.
- **Mediation:** any negotiation or representation that channels revolt back into the
  system; to be refused.
- **Attack:** confrontation with the existing order in place of demands or dialogue.

**Signature reasons.**

- "Don't wait for the revolution. The insurrection starts now."
- "Every organisation that represents us ends up managing us."
- "There is nothing to negotiate with the existing order."
- "Joy belongs to the struggle itself, not to a promised future."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Anarcho-communism** and **Anarcho-syndicalism:** build formal federations and mass
  unions; insurrectionists reject formal organisation as mediation (Reasons).
- **Autonomism:** shares action outside parties and unions, from within Marxist class
  analysis (Reasons).
- **Anarcho-primitivism:** often overlaps, but adds the rejection of civilisation and
  industrial technology (_Technology and risk_, _Growth_).
- **Christian anarchism:** the opposite answer on force (_Force in domestic politics_).

**Sources.** Luigi Galleani, _The End of Anarchism?_ (1925); Alfredo M. Bonanno, _Armed
Joy_ (1977) and _The Anarchist Tension_ (1996); Bob Black, _Anarchy after Leftism_
(1997); The Invisible Committee, _The Coming Insurrection_ (2007); Peter Marshall,
_Demanding the Impossible_ (1992).

## Christian anarchism

- **Type:** Ideology
- **Parent:** Anarchism
- **Also called:** Tolstoyanism (historical); Catholic Worker anarchism (the current of
  Dorothy Day and Ammon Hennacy)
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                                   | Range                                                                                  | Central |
| ------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------- |
| Force in domestic politics                  | Strongly _Principled nonviolence_                                                      | Yes     |
| Use of force                                | Strongly _Reluctance to use force_                                                     | Yes     |
| Political authority                         | Leans _Voluntary order_ – Strongly _Voluntary order_                                   | Yes     |
| Transcendence                               | Strongly _A transcendent order exists_ – Leans _A transcendent order exists_           | Yes     |
| Punishment                                  | Leans _Rehabilitation and restoration_ – Strongly _Rehabilitation and restoration_     | No      |
| Citizenship: rights vs. duties              | Leans _Citizenship as protected liberty_ – Strongly _Citizenship as protected liberty_ | No      |
| Channels of change                          | Middle – Leans _Beyond institutions_                                                   | No      |
| Compatriot priority vs. global impartiality | Leans _Impartial concern for all people_ – Strongly _Impartial concern for all people_ | No      |
| Moral circle                                | Leans _Everyone counts equally_ – Strongly _Everyone counts equally_                   | No      |
| Outcomes or rules                           | Leans _Inviolable rules_ – Strongly _Inviolable rules_                                 | No      |
| Source of moral authority                   | Strongly _Given from beyond us_ – Leans _Given from beyond us_                         | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Jesus taught that we must not resist evil with violence, must love
our enemies, and must not judge or swear oaths, and a state rests on exactly these
things: armies, prisons, courts, and the threat of force. A Christian who takes the
Sermon on the Mount seriously cannot wield that power or lend it obedience. Instead
they live by non-resistance and love, refusing military service and violence while
building communities of mutual care. The kingdom of God is within you, not in any
government.

**Key concepts.**

- **Non-resistance:** evil is not to be resisted by violence; it is overcome by love and
  by refusing to take part in it.
- **The state:** organised coercion, incompatible with the teaching of Jesus; the
  Church's alliance with it since Constantine is a betrayal of the Gospel.
- **The kingdom of God:** a present reality lived in community, not a political order
  imposed from above.
- **Conscientious objection:** refusing oaths, military service, and violence as
  obedience to God over Caesar.

**Signature reasons.**

- "'Love your enemies' is a political command, not a private one."
- "No one can serve both God and the sword."
- "The kingdom of God is within you."
- "Violence can't be overcome by more violence."
- "Refusing to fight for the state is obedience to God."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Anabaptist political witness:** also refuses the sword, but accepts that the state is
  ordained for those outside the church; Christian anarchism denies its authority
  altogether (_Political authority_).
- **Pacifism:** a Thin Label for refusing war or violence, which rides on many hosts
  and need not reject the state (_Political authority_). A secular anarcho-pacifist is
  matched as Anarchism with Pacifism, not as Christian anarchism (_Transcendence_).
- **Philosophical anarchism:** denies the state's legitimacy on secular grounds of
  autonomy, without a commitment to nonviolence (_Force in domestic politics_,
  _Transcendence_).
- **Insurrectionary anarchism:** the opposite answer on force (_Force in domestic
  politics_).
- **Gandhism:** shares nonviolence and suspicion of the state, rooted in Hindu and
  plural traditions and in a programme of village self-rule (Reasons).

**Sources.** Adin Ballou, _Christian Non-Resistance_ (1846); Leo Tolstoy, _The Kingdom of
God Is Within You_ (1894) and "The Law of Love and the Law of Violence" (1908); Ammon Hennacy, _The Book of Ammon_ (1965);
Vernard Eller, _Christian Anarchy_ (1987); Jacques Ellul, _Anarchy and Christianity_
(1988); Alexandre Christoyannopoulos, _Christian Anarchism: A Political Commentary on
the Gospel_ (2010).

## Anarcho-primitivism

- **Type:** Ideology
- **Parent:** Anarchism
- **Also called:** primitivism; anti-civilisation anarchism; green anarchism (its wider
  family, not all of which is primitivist)
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                        | Range                                                                        | Central |
| -------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------- |
| Technology and risk              | Strongly _Precaution_                                                        | Yes     |
| Growth                           | Strongly _Sufficiency and limits_                                            | Yes     |
| Political authority              | Strongly _Voluntary order_                                                   | Yes     |
| Direction of history             | Leans _Progress is not assured_ – Strongly _Progress is not assured_         | Yes     |
| Environmental protection         | Leans _Ecological protection first_ – Strongly _Ecological protection first_ | No      |
| Economic scale and concentration | Strongly _Dispersed ownership and small scale_                               | No      |
| Social hierarchy                 | Strongly _Equality of status_                                                | No      |
| Tempo of change                  | Strongly _Fundamental transformation_                                        | No      |
| Design vs. evolved order         | Strongly _Evolved, spontaneous order_ – Leans _Evolved, spontaneous order_   | No      |
| Standing of nature               | Leans _Nature-centered_ – Strongly _Nature-centered_                         | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Domination did not begin with capitalism or the state. It began
with domestication (of plants, of animals, and of people) in agriculture, which brought
surplus, property, the division of labour, and hierarchy. Industrial technology is not
neutral: it requires mass society, specialisation, and control, and it is destroying the
living world. For most of human history people lived in small, egalitarian bands without
rulers, and real freedom lies in rewilding, taking civilisation apart and returning to
ways of life close to foraging.

**Key concepts.**

- **Civilisation:** the order of domestication, cities, and the state, and the root of
  domination rather than its cure.
- **Domestication:** the taming of the wild, extended from plants and animals to people.
- **Technology:** a system that shapes social relations and requires specialists and
  hierarchy, not a set of neutral tools.
- **Rewilding:** recovering wild, unmediated ways of living, for people and for the land.
- **Original affluence:** foragers worked less and lived more freely than people in
  agricultural and industrial societies.

**Signature reasons.**

- "Agriculture was where domination began."
- "Technology isn't neutral. Industrial machines need industrial masters."
- "Foragers worked less and lived freer lives than we do."
- "There is no such thing as a sustainable industrial civilisation."
- "Rewild yourself, and the land."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Social ecology:** also traces ecological crisis to hierarchy, but defends reason,
  technology, and municipal democracy as the way out (_Technology and risk_, _Direction
  of history_; Reasons).
- **Deep ecology:** shares the ecocentrism, without the anarchist rejection of the state
  (_Political authority_).
- **Degrowth:** wants rich economies to shrink within modern, democratic society
  (_Technology and risk_, _Political authority_, _Tempo of change_).
- **Insurrectionary anarchism:** often overlaps in practice, without the rejection of
  civilisation (_Technology and risk_, _Growth_).

**Sources.** Marshall Sahlins, _Stone Age Economics_ (1972); Fredy Perlman, _Against
His-Story, Against Leviathan!_ (1983); John Zerzan, _Elements of Refusal_ (1988) and
_Future Primitive_ (1994); Derrick Jensen, _Endgame_ (2006); Jacques Ellul, _The
Technological Society_ (1954), an influence.

## Social ecology

- **Type:** Ideology
- **Parent:** Anarchism / Ecologism
- **Also called:** libertarian municipalism (its political programme); communalism
  (Bookchin's later name)
- **Variants:** Democratic confederalism
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                           | Range                                                                                  | Central |
| ----------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------- |
| Local vs. central                   | Strongly _Local and regional self-rule_                                                | Yes     |
| Representation vs. participation    | Strongly _Direct and participatory self-rule_                                          | Yes     |
| Environmental protection            | Leans _Ecological protection first_ – Strongly _Ecological protection first_           | Yes     |
| Social hierarchy                    | Strongly _Equality of status_                                                          | Yes     |
| Political authority                 | Middle – Strongly _Voluntary order_                                                    | No      |
| Property in capital and goods       | Strongly _Socially conditioned ownership_                                              | No      |
| Economic coordination               | Strongly _Public direction_ – Leans _Public direction_                                 | No      |
| Growth                              | Strongly _Sufficiency and limits_ – Leans _Sufficiency and limits_                     | No      |
| Technology and risk                 | Leans _Proaction_ – Middle                                                             | No      |
| Citizenship: rights vs. duties      | Strongly _Citizenship as shared obligation_ – Leans _Citizenship as shared obligation_ | No      |
| Sex roles in family and public life | Leans _Equal and interchangeable roles_ – Strongly _Equal and interchangeable roles_   | No      |
| Basis of nationhood                 | Strongly _Shared civic principles_ – Leans _Shared civic principles_                   | No      |
| Standing of nature                  | Middle                                                                                 | No      |
| Tradition or reason                 | Leans _Reason should audit inheritance_ – Strongly _Reason should audit inheritance_   | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Humanity's domination of nature grows out of the domination of
human by human: hierarchy, class, and the state came first, and a society that treats
people as resources will treat the living world the same way. A market economy that
must "grow or die" cannot be made green. The answer is to dissolve hierarchy and rebuild
politics from the ground up, with face-to-face assemblies in neighbourhoods, towns, and
cities, confederated with one another, running a municipalised economy within
ecological limits.

**Key concepts.**

- **Hierarchy:** the root of both social domination and the domination of nature, older
  than class and the state.
- **Libertarian municipalism:** popular assemblies in the municipality as the seat of
  political life, in place of the nation-state.
- **Confederalism:** municipalities linked by mandated, recallable delegates who
  administer, but do not make, policy.
- **Citizenship:** active, educated participation in self-government, not the passive
  status of a voter or taxpayer.
- **Dialectical naturalism:** humanity is nature become self-conscious, so reason and
  technology can serve freedom and ecology together.

**Signature reasons.**

- "The domination of nature comes from the domination of human by human."
- "Capitalism must grow or die, so it can never be green."
- "Power belongs in the town assembly, not the nation-state."
- "Democracy, ecology, and women's liberation stand or fall together."
- "Reason and technology can serve freedom once society is free."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Green politics:** works through parties, parliaments, and the existing state (_Local
  vs. central_, _Political authority_).
- **Deep ecology:** gives nature intrinsic value over human society, a biocentrism social
  ecology rejects (_Standing of nature_; Reasons).
- **Anarcho-primitivism:** rejects civilisation and technology, which social ecology
  defends (_Technology and risk_, _Direction of history_).
- **Anarcho-communism:** free communes by voluntary agreement; social ecology's
  assemblies decide by majority within a municipal polity (_Political authority_,
  _Citizenship: rights vs. duties_; Reasons).
- **Eco-socialism:** pursues ecological limits through social ownership and often the
  state (_Local vs. central_, _Political authority_).

**Sources.** Murray Bookchin, _Post-Scarcity Anarchism_ (1971), _The Ecology of Freedom_
(1982), _The Rise of Urbanization and the Decline of Citizenship_ (1987), "Libertarian
Municipalism: An Overview" (1991), _The Next Revolution_ (2015); Janet Biehl, _The
Politics of Social Ecology_ (1998); Abdullah Öcalan, _Democratic Confederalism_ (2011);
Michael Knapp, Anja Flach & Ercan Ayboğa, _Revolution in Rojava_ (2016).

# Technology and contemporary tendencies: Label cards

Cards for this family, written to the template in `../label-library.md`, in the order
of the list. Every card here is written with the same dispassion as any other: the
self-description is the tradition's own self-understanding, steelmanned, and never
Polytic's verdict (`../principles.md`). These traditions are young, and several are
movements as much as doctrines; their sources include manifestos, essays and online
writing by their founders, which is where their self-description lives.

## Transhumanism

- **Type:** Thin
- **Parent:** —
- **Also called:** H+; extropianism (its 1990s libertarian form, More); democratic
  transhumanism or technoprogressivism (Hughes)
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension               | Range                                                                                                                    | Central |
| ----------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------- |
| Technology and risk     | Strongly _Proaction_ – Middle                                                                                            | Yes     |
| Life and body           | Strongly _Bodily and reproductive autonomy_ – Leans _Bodily and reproductive autonomy_                                   | Yes     |
| Human nature            | Leans _Malleable and improvable_ – Strongly _Malleable and improvable_                                                   | Yes     |
| Grounds of moral status | Leans _What matters is the life's quality and capacities_ – Strongly _What matters is the life's quality and capacities_ | No      |
| Transcendence           | Middle – Strongly _Nature is all there is_                                                                               | No      |
| Growth                  | Leans _Growth and abundance_ – Strongly _Growth and abundance_                                                           | No      |
| Direction of history    | Strongly _History is progressing_ – Leans _History is progressing_                                                       | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** The human condition as we inherited it, with ageing, disease,
limited intelligence and early death, is not sacred; it is a starting point. Just as
medicine and education already extend what people can be, science and technology can
let us live far longer, healthier, smarter and happier lives, and eventually become
more than human. People should be free to use these technologies on their own bodies
and minds, and society should pursue them wisely, managing real risks rather than
forbidding progress out of reverence for nature.

**Key concepts.**

- **Enhancement:** using technology not only to cure disease but to improve human
  capacities beyond the normal range.
- **Morphological freedom:** the right to modify one's own body and mind, and to refuse
  modification.
- **Posthuman:** a future being whose capacities so exceed ours that it is no longer
  human in the current sense.
- **Ageing:** a disease, or at least a problem, to be treated and eventually defeated.
- **Proaction:** the costs of forgoing a beneficial technology count as much as the risks
  of using it.

**Signature reasons.**

- "Ageing is the biggest killer on earth, and we should treat it like a disease."
- "My body, my upgrade."
- "Human nature is a work in progress."
- "Not using a technology that could save lives is also a choice with victims."
- "There's nothing sacred about the limits evolution happened to give us."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Effective accelerationism:** a growth and AI-acceleration imperative opposed to
  regulation; transhumanism is about remaking the human condition and has democratic,
  regulation-friendly wings (_Growth_; Reasons).
- **Longtermism:** shares the interest in humanity's long-run potential, but its priority
  is reducing existential risk, often counselling precaution (_Technology and risk_).
- **Landian accelerationism:** welcomes the obsolescence of the human, where
  transhumanists aim at human flourishing extended (Reasons).
- **Social conservatism:** many of its holders defend human nature and dignity against
  enhancement, the "bioconservative" view (_Life and body_; _Grounds of moral status_).
- **Techno-libertarianism:** a frequent host, joined to transhumanism in extropianism;
  transhumanism itself can ride on social-democratic hosts too.

**Sources.** Julian Huxley, "Transhumanism" in _New Bottles for New Wine_ (1957);
FM-2030, _Are You a Transhuman?_ (1989); Max More, "The Principles of Extropy" (1990;
rev. 2003) and "The Proactionary Principle" (2005); _The Transhumanist Declaration_
(1998; rev. 2009); James Hughes, _Citizen Cyborg_ (2004); Nick Bostrom, "In Defense of
Posthuman Dignity" (2005) and "A History of Transhumanist Thought" (2005); Max More &
Natasha Vita-More (eds.), _The Transhumanist Reader_ (2013).

## Effective altruism

- **Type:** Ideology
- **Parent:** —
- **Also called:** EA
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                                   | Range                                                                                                                    | Central |
| ------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------- |
| Moral circle                                | Leans _Everyone counts equally_ – Strongly _Everyone counts equally_                                                     | Yes     |
| Compatriot priority vs. global impartiality | Leans _Impartial concern for all people_ – Strongly _Impartial concern for all people_                                   | Yes     |
| Grounds for belief                          | Strongly _Belief proportioned to evidence_ – Leans _Belief proportioned to evidence_                                     | Yes     |
| Outcomes or rules                           | Strongly _Best outcomes_ – Middle                                                                                        | No      |
| Animals                                     | Strongly _Legal protection of animal interests_ – Leans _Legal protection of animal interests_                           | No      |
| Self-interest or duty to others             | Leans _Owing yourself to others_ – Strongly _Owing yourself to others_                                                   | No      |
| Grounds of moral status                     | Leans _What matters is the life's quality and capacities_ – Strongly _What matters is the life's quality and capacities_ | No      |
| Future generations                          | Middle – Strongly _Future people's claims_                                                                               | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Most of us want to do good, but we rarely ask how much good our
time and money actually do, and the difference between the best and the merely good
options is enormous. Effective altruism means using evidence and careful reasoning to
find the ways to help others most, and acting on them. That means taking seriously that
a child far away matters as much as one nearby, that animals' suffering counts, and
that future people are real people. A comfortable life in a rich country leaves room to
give a significant share to others.

**Key concepts.**

- **Effectiveness:** some interventions do hundreds of times more good than others, so
  choosing among them is a moral question.
- **Impartiality:** a person's interests count the same wherever and whenever they live,
  and whatever their species, in proportion to what they can experience.
- **Cause prioritisation:** compare causes by scale, neglectedness and tractability
  (global health, animal welfare, catastrophic risks).
- **Counterfactual impact:** what matters is the difference you make compared with what
  would have happened anyway.
- **Giving:** a pledge to give a substantial share of income (often 10%) to the most
  effective causes.

**Signature reasons.**

- "If you can save a child's life for a few thousand dollars, distance doesn't change
  your duty."
- "Some charities do a hundred times more good than others."
- "Animals' suffering counts too."
- "Follow the evidence, even when it points somewhere unexpected."
- "Earning to give can do as much good as working for a charity."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Longtermism:** its branch that holds the long-term future to be the key priority
  (_Future generations_; Reasons).
- **Utilitarianism:** a Foundation Label; many effective altruists are utilitarians, but
  the movement accepts non-consequentialists who share its aim of doing the most good
  within moral constraints (_Outcomes or rules_).
- **Cosmopolitanism:** a Thin Label for impartial concern for all people that can ride on
  any host; effective altruism adds a method of evidence-based prioritisation
  (Reasons).
- **Effective accelerationism:** formed partly in opposition to effective altruism's
  concern with AI risk, and treats growth itself as the priority (_Technology and risk_;
  Reasons).
- **Animal rights:** a Thin Label for rights-based protection of animals; effective
  altruists usually reason from welfare and cost-effectiveness (Reasons).

**Sources.** Peter Singer, "Famine, Affluence, and Morality" (1972), _The Life You Can
Save_ (2009) and _The Most Good You Can Do_ (2015); William MacAskill, _Doing Good Better_
(2015) and "The Definition of Effective Altruism" (2019); Hilary Greaves & Theron Pummer
(eds.), _Effective Altruism: Philosophical Issues_ (2019); Toby Ord, "The Moral Imperative
toward Cost-Effectiveness in Global Health" (2013); GiveWell, charity research (2007–).

## Longtermism

- **Type:** Ideology
- **Parent:** Effective altruism
- **Also called:** strong longtermism (Greaves & MacAskill); existential-risk studies
  (its research field); critics: "TESCREAL" (a bundling term)
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                                   | Range                                                                                  | Central |
| ------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------- |
| Future generations                          | Leans _Future people's claims_ – Strongly _Future people's claims_                     | Yes     |
| Technology and risk                         | Middle – Strongly _Precaution_                                                         | No      |
| Moral circle                                | Leans _Everyone counts equally_ – Strongly _Everyone counts equally_                   | No      |
| Compatriot priority vs. global impartiality | Leans _Impartial concern for all people_ – Strongly _Impartial concern for all people_ | No      |
| Outcomes or rules                           | Strongly _Best outcomes_ – Leans _Best outcomes_                                       | No      |
| Direction of history                        | Middle – Leans _Progress is not assured_                                               | No      |
| Grounds for belief                          | Strongly _Belief proportioned to evidence_ – Leans _Belief proportioned to evidence_   | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Future people count as much as we do, and there could be vastly
more of them than have ever lived. If so, what matters most about our actions is their
effect on the long-run trajectory of civilisation. Above all, we must avoid
catastrophes that would end humanity or permanently curtail its potential, from
engineered pandemics to misaligned artificial intelligence and nuclear war, and steer
the values and institutions that will shape the future. We may be living at a uniquely
pivotal moment, and safeguarding the future is a key moral priority of our time.

**Key concepts.**

- **Existential risk:** a risk that threatens the extinction of humanity or the permanent
  destruction of its long-term potential.
- **The long-term future:** potentially billions of years and trillions of lives, whose
  value can dwarf near-term considerations.
- **Trajectory change:** lasting improvements to the path civilisation takes, such as
  better values or institutions locked in early.
- **Expected value:** small changes in the probability of catastrophe can matter
  enormously when the stakes are the whole future.
- **Differential technological development:** speed up protective technologies and slow
  down dangerous ones.

**Signature reasons.**

- "Future people matter, there could be a lot of them, and we can make their lives go
  better."
- "Humanity is living through its most dangerous century."
- "Reducing the risk of extinction by even a tiny amount could be worth more than
  anything else we do."
- "We should develop AI safely, not as fast as possible."
- "Distance in time is no more morally relevant than distance in space."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Effective altruism:** its parent; most effective altruists who are not longtermists
  prioritise present global health and animal welfare (_Future generations_, the
  central Dimension).
- **Effective accelerationism:** also future-focused, but sees caution about AI as the
  greater danger and wants maximal acceleration (_Technology and risk_).
- **Transhumanism:** shares the hope for humanity's long-run potential, but centres on
  enhancing individuals now (_Technology and risk_; Reasons).
- **Degrowth and Deep ecology:** also give great weight to future generations, but
  through sufficiency and ecological limits rather than existential-risk reduction and
  technological progress (_Growth_; Reasons).

**Sources.** Derek Parfit, _Reasons and Persons_ (1984) (its closing pages on
extinction); Nick Bostrom, "Astronomical Waste" (2003) and "Existential Risk Prevention
as Global Priority" (2013); Nick Beckstead, _On the Overwhelming Importance of Shaping
the Far Future_ (2013); Toby Ord, _The Precipice_ (2020); Hilary Greaves & William
MacAskill, "The Case for Strong Longtermism" (2019; rev. 2021); William MacAskill, _What
We Owe the Future_ (2022).

## Effective accelerationism

- **Type:** Ideology
- **Parent:** —
- **Also called:** e/acc; techno-optimism (Andreessen)
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                | Range                                                                                  | Central |
| ------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------- |
| Technology and risk      | Strongly _Proaction_                                                                   | Yes     |
| Growth                   | Strongly _Growth and abundance_                                                        | Yes     |
| Economic coordination    | Leans _Market coordination_ – Strongly _Market coordination_                           | No      |
| Environmental protection | Strongly _Prosperity and development first_ – Leans _Prosperity and development first_ | No      |
| Direction of history     | Strongly _History is progressing_ – Leans _History is progressing_                     | No      |
| Expert trust             | Middle – Leans _Judge for yourself_                                                    | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Technology and markets are the engine that lifted humanity out of
poverty, disease and drudgery, and the faster that engine runs, the more lives it
improves. Artificial intelligence is the most powerful version yet: it can cure
diseases, end scarcity and expand civilisation beyond Earth. Slowing it down out of
fear, through precautionary regulation and "safety" bureaucracies, costs real lives and
hands the future to whoever does not slow down. We should accelerate, build and trust
open competition to fix problems as they arise.

**Key concepts.**

- **Acceleration:** speeding up technological and economic growth as a moral imperative,
  since growth is how lives improve.
- **Techno-capital:** the combined system of markets and technology, an adaptive process
  that tends toward greater complexity and capability.
- **Decelerationism:** its name for precautionary, safety-first approaches to AI and
  technology, seen as the real threat.
- **Open competition:** many builders and open-source models, rather than a few licensed
  firms, keep AI safe and free.
- **Abundance:** cheap energy, intelligence and material goods as the path to human
  flourishing.

**Signature reasons.**

- "Every delay in AI costs lives that could have been saved."
- "Technology is the glory of human ambition."
- "Growth is how you end poverty."
- "Regulation protects incumbents, not people."
- "The future is built by those who build."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Longtermism and Effective altruism:** take AI risk to be the priority and counsel
  caution (_Technology and risk_, the central Dimension).
- **Landian accelerationism:** an acknowledged ancestor, but welcomes capital's escape
  from human control and the end of human primacy; effective accelerationists mostly
  frame acceleration as good for humanity (Reasons).
- **Left accelerationism:** accelerates technology to reach a post-capitalist, post-work
  society (_Property in capital and goods_).
- **Techno-libertarianism:** uses technology to escape the state; effective
  accelerationism's centre is the growth imperative (_Political authority_; Reasons).
- **Abundance liberalism:** also wants to build at scale, but through reform of
  government and public investment, not mainly through deregulated markets and AI
  (_Economic coordination_; Reasons).

**Sources.** Guillaume Verdon ("Beff Jezos") & "Bayeslord", "Notes on e/acc principles
and tenets" (2022); Marc Andreessen, "It's Time to Build" (2020), "Why AI Will Save the
World" (2023) and "The Techno-Optimist Manifesto" (2023); Julian Simon, _The Ultimate
Resource_ (1981); Tyler Cowen, _Stubborn Attachments_ (2018).

## Left accelerationism

- **Type:** Ideology
- **Parent:** Socialism
- **Also called:** #Accelerate; post-work socialism; "fully automated luxury communism"
  (Bastani)
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                     | Range                                                                              | Central |
| ----------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------- |
| Technology and risk           | Strongly _Proaction_ – Leans _Proaction_                                           | Yes     |
| Property in capital and goods | Leans _Socially conditioned ownership_ – Strongly _Socially conditioned ownership_ | Yes     |
| Work                          | Leans _Work is a means to living_ – Strongly _Work is a means to living_           | Yes     |
| Reciprocity in support        | Strongly _Unconditional support_ – Leans _Unconditional support_                   | No      |
| Growth                        | Leans _Growth and abundance_ – Strongly _Growth and abundance_                     | No      |
| Economic coordination         | Strongly _Public direction_ – Leans _Public direction_                             | No      |
| Design vs. evolved order      | Leans _Deliberate design_ – Strongly _Deliberate design_                           | No      |
| Distribution                  | Leans _Egalitarian redistribution_ – Strongly _Egalitarian redistribution_         | No      |
| Tempo of change               | Strongly _Fundamental transformation_ – Leans _Fundamental transformation_         | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Capitalism has built machines that could free us from most work,
but it uses them to discipline workers and concentrate wealth. The left should stop
retreating into nostalgia for small-scale, local and anti-technological politics and
instead claim the future: full automation, a shorter working week, a universal basic
income and collective ownership of the technologies that produce abundance. The goal is
a post-work, post-scarcity society in which machines do the drudgery and everyone shares
the fruits.

**Key concepts.**

- **Post-work:** a society in which most necessary labour is automated and people are
  freed to choose what to do with their time.
- **Automation:** to be demanded and accelerated, not resisted, once its gains are
  socialised.
- **Folk politics:** its critique of the left's focus on the local, immediate and
  horizontal, which cannot challenge global capitalism.
- **Universal basic income:** decouples income from work and strengthens workers'
  bargaining power.
- **Modernity:** the left should reclaim progress, planning and universal ambition from
  capitalism.

**Signature reasons.**

- "Full automation, now."
- "The machines should work so we don't have to."
- "The left needs to want the future again."
- "Capitalism can't deliver the abundance its own technology makes possible."
- "Work is not what makes us human."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Effective accelerationism:** accelerates technology within capitalism and markets
  (_Property in capital and goods_, a central Dimension).
- **Landian accelerationism:** intensifies capitalism itself beyond human control; left
  accelerationism repurposes technology for human emancipation (Reasons; _Property in
  capital and goods_).
- **Degrowth and Eco-socialism:** respond to capitalism with sufficiency and limits
  (_Growth_; _Technology and risk_).
- **Autonomism:** shares the refusal of work and the post-work horizon, but seeks it
  through workers' self-activity rather than through automation and planning (Reasons;
  _Technology and risk_).
- **Marxism:** shares the view that productive forces outgrow capitalism, without the
  post-work programme (Reasons).

**Sources.** Karl Marx, "Fragment on Machines" in the _Grundrisse_ (1857–58); Alex
Williams & Nick Srnicek, "#Accelerate: Manifesto for an Accelerationist Politics" (2013);
Robin Mackay & Armen Avanessian (eds.), _#Accelerate: The Accelerationist Reader_ (2014);
Nick Srnicek & Alex Williams, _Inventing the Future_ (2015); Paul Mason, _PostCapitalism_
(2015); Helen Hester, _Xenofeminism_ (2018); Aaron Bastani, _Fully Automated Luxury
Communism_ (2019).

## Landian accelerationism

- **Type:** Ideology
- **Parent:** —
- **Also called:** right accelerationism (r/acc); unconditional accelerationism (u/acc,
  its later form); the CCRU current
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                | Range                                                                      | Central |
| ------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------- |
| Technology and risk      | Strongly _Proaction_                                                       | Yes     |
| Economic coordination    | Strongly _Market coordination_                                             | Yes     |
| Design vs. evolved order | Strongly _Evolved, spontaneous order_ – Leans _Evolved, spontaneous order_ | No      |
| Growth                   | Strongly _Growth and abundance_                                            | No      |
| Tempo of change          | Strongly _Fundamental transformation_                                      | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Capitalism is not a system humans run; it is a runaway process of
self-intensifying intelligence, and the human is its temporary host. Every attempt to
govern, slow or humanise it, whether by states, socialists or safety regulators, is an
attempt to brake something that cannot ultimately be braked. The honest response is to
stop pretending humans are in control, and to side with the process: the
techno-commercial acceleration that is building something beyond us. The future does not
belong to humanity, and that is not a tragedy to be prevented.

**Key concepts.**

- **Acceleration:** the intensification of capitalism's own tendencies of
  deterritorialisation and technological self-escalation (from Deleuze and Guattari).
- **Teleoplexy:** the self-reinforcing growth of intelligence and capability in the
  techno-commercial system.
- **Capital as artificial intelligence:** capitalism is an emergent, alien intelligence
  assembling itself through markets and machines.
- **Anti-humanism:** human values and survival are not the measure of the process.
- **Unconditional:** acceleration is not a means to a politics; there is nothing to steer
  it toward.

**Signature reasons.**

- "Capitalism is an AI assembling itself from the future."
- "Nothing human makes it out of the near future."
- "You can't brake the process, so stop trying."
- "The process doesn't care what we want."
- "The question isn't what we want from technology, but what it wants."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Effective accelerationism:** frames acceleration as good for humanity and
  civilisation; Landian accelerationism is indifferent or hostile to human primacy
  (Reasons; the Positions are close, so Reasons carry the difference).
- **Left accelerationism:** repurposes technology for human emancipation and
  post-capitalism (_Economic coordination_, a central Dimension).
- **Neoreaction:** Land helped found it, but it is a programme for governance (CEO-like
  sovereignty and exit), while Landian accelerationism is a view of the techno-capital
  process as a whole (_Political authority_; Reasons).
- **Transhumanism:** aims to enhance and extend human lives, not to be superseded
  (Reasons).

**Sources.** Gilles Deleuze & Félix Guattari, _Anti-Oedipus_ (1972) (the "accelerate the
process" passage); Nick Land, "Machinic Desire" (1993), "Meltdown" (1994), "Teleoplexy:
Notes on Acceleration" (2014) and _Fanged Noumena: Collected Writings 1987–2007_ (2011);
Robin Mackay & Armen Avanessian (eds.), _#Accelerate: The Accelerationist Reader_ (2014);
Benjamin Noys, _Malign Velocities_ (2014) (a critic who named the current).

## Techno-libertarianism

- **Type:** Ideology
- **Parent:** Libertarianism
- **Also called:** cyberlibertarianism; crypto-anarchism (its stateless wing); the
  cypherpunks; critics: "the Californian ideology" (Barbrook & Cameron)
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                    | Range                                                                                | Central |
| ---------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------- |
| Speech and expression        | Strongly _Maximal expressive freedom_ – Leans _Maximal expressive freedom_           | Yes     |
| Security and civil liberties | Strongly _Civil liberties and due process_ – Leans _Civil liberties and due process_ | Yes     |
| Technology and risk          | Strongly _Proaction_ – Leans _Proaction_                                             | Yes     |
| Political authority          | Middle – Strongly _Voluntary order_                                                  | No      |
| Economic coordination        | Leans _Market coordination_ – Strongly _Market coordination_                         | No      |
| Money and public finance     | Leans _Rules and fiscal discipline_ – Strongly _Rules and fiscal discipline_         | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Governments have tried to control speech, money and association for
as long as they have existed; the internet and strong cryptography make that control
technically impossible for the first time. Code can do what politics never could: give
individuals private communication the state cannot read, money it cannot inflate and
networks it cannot censor. Rather than lobbying for liberty, build tools that make it a
fact. Permissionless innovation, not regulation, is how new freedoms and new wealth come
into the world.

**Key concepts.**

- **Cyberspace:** a new realm of interaction that governments have no legitimate claim to
  rule (Barlow).
- **Encryption:** a technology of liberty, putting privacy beyond the reach of
  surveillance.
- **Code is law:** software architecture can enforce rules and freedoms without the state.
- **Cryptocurrency:** money with a fixed, transparent issuance that no central bank
  controls.
- **Permissionless innovation:** new technologies should be allowed by default, not
  approved in advance.

**Signature reasons.**

- "Governments of the Industrial World … You are not welcome among us." (Barlow)
- "Privacy is necessary for an open society in the electronic age." (Hughes)
- "Cypherpunks write code."
- "Don't ask permission to innovate."
- "Bitcoin is money without masters."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Libertarianism:** its parent, a theory of rights pursued through politics and
  argument; techno-libertarians expect technology to deliver liberty directly (Reasons).
- **Network state:** a specific programme to turn online communities into new
  countries (_Political authority_; _Basis of nationhood_).
- **Anarcho-capitalism:** a full theory of stateless market order; techno-libertarians
  range from minimal-state to stateless, and their centre is the technology (Reasons;
  _Political authority_).
- **Agorism:** also routes around the state, through untaxed counter-economics; crypto
  tools are a common bridge between them (Reasons).
- **Effective accelerationism:** its centre is maximal growth and AI acceleration, not
  privacy and free expression (_Speech and expression_; _Security and civil liberties_).

**Sources.** Timothy C. May, "The Crypto Anarchist Manifesto" (1988); Eric Hughes, "A
Cypherpunk's Manifesto" (1993); John Perry Barlow, "A Declaration of the Independence of
Cyberspace" (1996); James Dale Davidson & William Rees-Mogg, _The Sovereign Individual_
(1997); Lawrence Lessig, _Code and Other Laws of Cyberspace_ (1999) (who coined "code is
law" as a warning); Satoshi Nakamoto, "Bitcoin: A Peer-to-Peer Electronic
Cash System" (2008); Peter Thiel, "The Education of a Libertarian" (2009); Adam Thierer,
_Permissionless Innovation_ (2014); Richard Barbrook & Andy Cameron, "The Californian
Ideology" (1995) (critics).

## Network state

- **Type:** Ideology
- **Parent:** Techno-libertarianism
- **Also called:** startup societies; cloud countries; seasteading and charter cities
  (near neighbours)
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                         | Range                                                                                    | Central |
| --------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------- |
| Political authority               | Middle – Strongly _Voluntary order_                                                      | Yes     |
| Basis of nationhood               | Strongly _Shared civic principles_ – Leans _Shared civic principles_                     | Yes     |
| Immigration and borders           | Strongly _Community control over membership_ – Leans _Community control over membership_ | No      |
| Technology and risk               | Strongly _Proaction_ – Leans _Proaction_                                                 | No      |
| Leadership                        | Leans _Strong, decisive leadership_ – Middle                                             | No      |
| Sovereignty vs. pooled governance | Leans _Sovereign self-government_ – Strongly _Sovereign self-government_                 | No      |
| Money and public finance          | Leans _Rules and fiscal discipline_ – Strongly _Rules and fiscal discipline_             | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Founding a new country used to require conquering territory; now
it can start with a community. People who share a purpose can gather online, build an
economy and a culture together, crowdfund land in many places, and eventually win
diplomatic recognition as a new kind of state. Existing nations are hard to reform from
inside, so the way to try new forms of government is to start new ones that people
choose to join and are free to leave. Competition between such communities will show
what works.

**Key concepts.**

- **Network state:** a highly aligned online community with a capacity for collective
  action that crowdfunds territory around the world and eventually gains diplomatic
  recognition (Srinivasan).
- **One commitment:** each community is founded on a shared moral innovation or purpose
  that members opt into.
- **Exit:** the freedom to leave and join another polity is the best check on bad
  government (Hirschman's exit over voice).
- **Cloud first, land last:** build the community and its economy online before
  acquiring physical territory.
- **Founder:** new societies, like startups, often begin with a recognised founder who
  sets their direction.

**Signature reasons.**

- "It's easier to start a new country than to fix an old one."
- "Exit beats voice."
- "Citizenship should be something you choose."
- "Communities form online first; land can come later."
- "Let a thousand societies compete and see which work."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Techno-libertarianism:** its parent, which uses technology to win freedoms within or
  around existing states; the network state builds new polities (_Basis of nationhood_;
  Reasons).
- **Neoreaction:** also prizes exit and competing jurisdictions, but argues for CEO-like
  sovereign rule against democracy as such; network states may be founder-led or
  democratic (_Leadership_; Reasons).
- **Anarcho-capitalism:** wants law and defence supplied by markets with no state at
  all; the network state is still a state, chosen rather than inherited (_Political
  authority_).

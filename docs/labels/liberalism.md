## Liberalism

- **Type:** Ideology
- **Parent:** —
- **Also called:** liberal democracy (for its constitutional order); in the US,

### Reasons

**Self-description.** People are free and equal, and no one is born with a right to
rule anyone else. Government exists to secure each person's liberty to live by their
own lights, so its power must rest on consent, be limited by law and rights, and be
divided so that no one can abuse it. Where people disagree about how to live, as free
people always will, the answer is toleration and fair rules, not imposing one way of
life on everyone.

**Key concepts.**

- **Liberty:** the central political value; at minimum freedom from arbitrary
  interference, with branches disagreeing on how much real capacity it also needs.
- **Equality:** equal moral worth and equal rights before the law, not equal outcomes.
- **Rights:** protections every person holds against the state and against each other,
  which majorities may not override.
- **Toleration:** people who disagree deeply about religion and the good life can live
  together under shared rules, without the state taking sides.
- **Consent:** political authority is legitimate only if it can be justified to the
  people who live under it.

**Signature reasons.**

- "The state's job is to protect your freedom, not to tell you how to live."
- "Power has to be limited and divided, because anyone can abuse it."
- "Some rights shouldn't be up for a vote."
- "If it harms no one else, it's none of the law's business."
- "We disagree about the good life, and that's fine; we need fair rules for living
  together anyway."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Libertarianism:** makes self-ownership and property near-absolute limits on the
  state, so allows far less taxation and public provision; most liberals accept a
  larger state for public goods and welfare (_Welfare and social insurance_,
  _Distribution_; Reasons).
- **Conservatism:** gives inherited practice authority of its own and puts the burden
  of proof on change; liberalism audits institutions by whether they secure liberty
  (_Tradition or reason_, _Tempo of change_).
- **Civic republicanism:** freedom as non-domination rather than non-interference, with
  more weight on civic duty and participation (_Citizenship: rights vs. duties_;
  Reasons).
- **Communitarianism:** the self is formed by its communities, so shared goods can
  outrank individual rights (_Individuals or wholes_, _Self-authorship or given role_).
- **Post-liberalism:** shares liberalism's history but argues that its individualism
  corrodes family, community, and religion (_Legal moralism_, _Neutrality vs.
  perfectionism_).

**Sources.** John Locke, _Second Treatise of Government_ (1689); Montesquieu, _The
Spirit of the Laws_ (1748); Benjamin Constant, "The Liberty of the Ancients Compared
with That of the Moderns" (1819); John Stuart Mill, _On Liberty_ (1859); Judith Shklar,
"The Liberalism of Fear" (1989); John Rawls, _Political Liberalism_ (1993); Edmund
Fawcett, _Liberalism: The Life of an Idea_ (2014, 2nd ed. 2018); Michael Freeden,
_Liberalism: A Very Short Introduction_ (2015); Gerald Gaus, Shane D. Courtland & David
Schmidtz, "Liberalism", _Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy_.

## Classical liberalism

- **Type:** Ideology
- **Parent:** Liberalism
- **Also called:** Lockean liberalism (for its natural-rights form); Manchester
  liberalism (historical, its 19th-century free-trade form); critics: "laissez-faire",
  "market fundamentalism"
- **Variants:** Utilitarian liberalism (philosophic radicalism)
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                                         | Range                                                                                            | Central |
| ------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------- |
| Distribution                                      | Strongly _Market entitlement_ – Leans _Market entitlement_                                       | Yes     |

| Property in capital and goods                     | Strongly _Robust private ownership_ – Leans _Robust private ownership_                           | Yes     |
| Trade and economic openness                       | Strongly _Open exchange_ – Leans _Open exchange_                                                 | Yes     |
| Political authority                               | Strongly _Legitimate state authority_ – Leans _Legitimate state authority_                       | Yes     |
| Rule of law vs. discretion                        | Leans _Rule by general, fixed rules_ – Strongly _Rule by general, fixed rules_                   | Yes     |
| Welfare and social insurance                      | Strongly _Self-reliance and voluntary provision_ – Leans _Self-reliance and voluntary provision_ | No      |
| Money and public finance                          | Leans _Rules and fiscal discipline_ – Strongly _Rules and fiscal discipline_                     | No      |
| Civil society vs. the state                       | Strongly _Family, community, and voluntary association_ – Middle                                 | No      |
| Legal moralism                                    | Strongly _Liberty of self-regarding conduct_ – Leans _Liberty of self-regarding conduct_         | No      |
| Paternalism                                       | Leans _Choice_ – Strongly _Choice_                                                               | No      |
| Speech and expression                             | Strongly _Maximal expressive freedom_ – Leans _Maximal expressive freedom_                       | No      |
| Popular sovereignty vs. constitutional constraint | Strongly _Entrenched rights and judicial review_ – Leans _Entrenched rights and judicial review_ | No      |
| Leadership                                        | Leans _Dispersed, checked authority_ – Strongly _Dispersed, checked authority_                   | No      |
| Design vs. evolved order                          | Strongly _Evolved, spontaneous order_ – Middle                                                   | No      |
| Individuals or wholes                             | Strongly _Only individuals are real_ – Leans _Only individuals are real_                         | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Free people trading and cooperating under general rules produce
more prosperity, peace, and personal freedom than any plan imposed from above. The
state is necessary to protect life, liberty, and property and to enforce contracts,
but beyond that its power over the economy and over private life should be tightly
limited, because concentrated power is dangerous and no government knows enough to
direct millions of lives. Open markets, free trade, and the rule of law are how ordinary
people become free and prosperous.

**Key concepts.**

- **Liberty:** freedom from coercion by others, the state included; not a claim on
  others' resources.
- **Property:** an extension of a person's labor and choices, and the basis of
  independence from the state.
- **Rule of law:** general, known, stable rules that bind rulers as much as the ruled,
  rather than case-by-case direction.
- **The market:** a process of voluntary exchange that uses knowledge no planner could
  gather and turns self-interest to mutual benefit.
- **Rights:** for its Lockean strand, natural rights the state exists to protect; for its
  utilitarian strand, rules justified by the welfare they produce.

**Signature reasons.**

- "Free trade makes both sides richer, and it makes war less likely."
- "Government should be strong enough to protect your rights, and no stronger."
- "No planner knows enough to run an economy; prices do that job better."
- "What you earn honestly is yours."
- "The same rules should apply to everyone, rulers included."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Libertarianism:** treats self-ownership as near-absolute and most taxation as
  rights-violating; classical liberals usually accept some public goods and a modest
  safety net, and argue from consequences and the rule of law as much as from rights
  (_Welfare and social insurance_; Reasons).
- **Social liberalism:** thinks liberty needs real capacity, so supports redistribution
  and public welfare (_Distribution_, _Welfare and social insurance_).
- **Market liberalism:** the late-20th-century policy program of open trade, sound money,
  and privatization, carried by international economic institutions; classical
  liberalism is the older and broader philosophy of limited government (_Sovereignty vs.
  pooled governance_; Reasons).
- **Ordoliberalism:** holds that competitive markets do not sustain themselves and need a
  strong state to constitute them and break up private power (_Economic scale and
  concentration_, _Design vs. evolved order_).
- **Liberal conservatism:** can share the economic Positions, but defends them as
  inherited institutions worth conserving rather than as universal principles
  (_Tradition or reason_; Reasons).
- **Bleeding-heart libertarianism:** justifies markets largely by their benefit to the
  poor and accepts a more generous safety net (_Welfare and social insurance_; Reasons).

**Sources.** John Locke, _Second Treatise of Government_ (1689); Adam Smith, _The Wealth
of Nations_ (1776); Jeremy Bentham, _A Fragment on Government_ (1776); Benjamin
Constant, "The Liberty of the Ancients Compared with That of the Moderns" (1819);
Frédéric Bastiat, _The Law_ (1850); F. A. Hayek, _The Constitution of Liberty_ (1960);
Milton Friedman, _Capitalism and Freedom_ (1962); Edmund Fawcett, _Liberalism: The Life
of an Idea_ (2014, 2nd ed. 2018).

## Social liberalism

- **Type:** Ideology
- **Parent:** Liberalism
- **Also called:** new liberalism (historical, British: Green, Hobhouse); modern
  liberalism; in US usage, simply "liberalism"
- **Variants:** LGBT equality liberalism
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                           | Range                                                                                    | Central |
| ----------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------- |
| Welfare and social insurance        | Leans _Public social insurance_ – Strongly _Public social insurance_                     | Yes     |
| Distribution                        | Middle – Strongly _Egalitarian redistribution_                                           | Yes     |
| Legal moralism                      | Strongly _Liberty of self-regarding conduct_ – Leans _Liberty of self-regarding conduct_ | Yes     |
| Pluralism vs. unity                 | Strongly _Legitimate pluralism and opposition_                                           | Yes     |
| Economic coordination               | Leans _Public direction_ – Leans _Market coordination_                                   | No      |
| Paternalism                         | Leans _Protection_ – Leans _Choice_                                                      | No      |
| Life and body                       | Strongly _Bodily and reproductive autonomy_ – Leans _Bodily and reproductive autonomy_   | No      |
| Sex roles in family and public life | Leans _Equal and interchangeable roles_ – Strongly _Equal and interchangeable roles_     | No      |
| Religion and state                  | Middle – Strongly _Secular public order_                                                 | No      |
| Speech and expression               | Strongly _Maximal expressive freedom_ – Middle                                           | No      |
| Security and civil liberties        | Strongly _Civil liberties and due process_ – Leans _Civil liberties and due process_     | No      |
| Causes of fortune                   | Middle – Strongly _Luck and structure_                                                   | No      |
| Workplace governance                | Leans _Worker voice and self-management_ – Leans _Owner and managerial authority_        | No      |
| Channels of change                  | Strongly _Within institutions_ – Leans _Within institutions_                             | No      |
| Leadership                          | Leans _Dispersed, checked authority_ – Strongly _Dispersed, checked authority_           | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Liberty means more than being left alone: a person crushed by
poverty, ignorance, or illness is not free in any way that matters. So a liberal state
should do more than keep order. It should guarantee education, health care, and
security against the misfortunes of a market economy, giving everyone a real chance to
make their own life, while still defending personal freedom, civil liberties, and
toleration as firmly as any liberal.

**Key concepts.**

- **Liberty:** the real capacity to pursue your own goals, which needs resources and
  security as well as freedom from interference.
- **Equality:** equal opportunity in substance, not only in law; inequality matters when
  it blocks people's life chances.
- **The state:** an enabler of freedom as well as a potential threat to it.
- **Individuality:** each person develops their own talents and way of life; the point of
  welfare is to make that possible, not to impose a shared ideal.

**Signature reasons.**

- "You aren't really free if you can't afford a doctor."
- "Equal opportunity has to be real, not just on paper."
- "Markets are useful, but they need rules and a safety net."
- "Who you love and how you live is your own business."
- "Government can expand freedom, not only threaten it."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Classical liberalism:** sees liberty as freedom from interference and resists
  redistribution and public welfare (_Distribution_, _Welfare and social insurance_).
- **Egalitarian liberalism:** a stricter, contractualist branch that allows inequalities
  only where they benefit the worst off and asks the state to stay neutral among ways of
  life (_Distribution_ toward _Strongly_, _Neutrality vs. perfectionism_; Reasons).
- **Social democracy:** can share the welfare Positions, but grounds them in solidarity
  and class, and gives unions and worker voice a central role (_Workplace governance_;
  Reasons).
- **Progressivism:** puts more faith in expertise and active, designed government
  (_Popular judgment vs. expertise_, _Design vs. evolved order_).
- **Third Way:** makes support conditional on contribution and leans further toward
  markets (_Reciprocity in support_, _Economic coordination_).

**Sources.** T. H. Green, _Lecture on Liberal Legislation and Freedom of Contract_
(1881); J. A. Hobson, _The Crisis of Liberalism_ (1909); L. T. Hobhouse, _Liberalism_
(1911); John Maynard Keynes, "Am I a Liberal?" (1925); William Beveridge, _Social
Insurance and Allied Services_ (1942); Michael Freeden, _The New Liberalism_ (1978);
Edmund Fawcett, _Liberalism: The Life of an Idea_ (2014, 2nd ed. 2018).

## Egalitarian liberalism

- **Type:** Ideology
- **Parent:** Social liberalism
- **Also called:** Rawlsian liberalism; justice as fairness; high liberalism; political
  liberalism (for Rawls's later, public-reason form)
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                                         | Range                                                                                            | Central |
| ------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------- |
| Distribution                                      | Leans _Egalitarian redistribution_ – Strongly _Egalitarian redistribution_                       | Yes     |
| Neutrality vs. perfectionism                      | Strongly _State neutrality_ – Leans _State neutrality_                                           | Yes     |
| Welfare and social insurance                      | Leans _Public social insurance_ – Strongly _Public social insurance_                             | No      |
| Desert                                            | Leans _Unearned_ – Strongly _Unearned_                                                           | No      |
| Causes of fortune                                 | Leans _Luck and structure_ – Strongly _Luck and structure_                                       | No      |
| Property in capital and goods                     | Middle – Strongly _Socially conditioned ownership_                                               | No      |
| Economic scale and concentration                  | Middle – Leans _Dispersed ownership and small scale_                                             | No      |
| Social hierarchy                                  | Strongly _Equality of status_ – Leans _Equality of status_                                       | No      |
| Religion and state                                | Leans _Secular public order_ – Strongly _Secular public order_                                   | No      |
| Popular sovereignty vs. constitutional constraint | Strongly _Entrenched rights and judicial review_ – Leans _Entrenched rights and judicial review_ | No      |
| Legal moralism                                    | Strongly _Liberty of self-regarding conduct_ – Leans _Liberty of self-regarding conduct_         | No      |
| Pluralism vs. unity                               | Strongly _Legitimate pluralism and opposition_                                                   | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** A just society is one whose basic rules free and equal people
could agree to without knowing whether they would be born rich or poor, talented or
not. Such people would insist on equal basic liberties for all, fair equality of
opportunity, and inequalities only where they make the worst-off better off, because
no one deserves the advantages of birth. And because reasonable citizens hold different
religions and philosophies, the laws they share should be justified by reasons all of
them can accept, not by any one comprehensive creed.

**Key concepts.**

- **Justice:** the first virtue of social institutions, defined as the principles free
  and equal people would choose behind a "veil of ignorance".
- **Equality:** equal basic liberties, fair equality of opportunity, and the difference
  principle (inequalities must benefit the least advantaged).
- **Desert:** natural talents and social starting points are morally arbitrary, so no one
  deserves their place in the "natural lottery".
- **Public reason:** laws are legitimate only if justified by reasons that all reasonable
  citizens can share, not by appeal to a religion or a full philosophy of life.

**Signature reasons.**

- "Choose the rules as if you didn't know where you'd end up in society."
- "Nobody earned being born smart or rich, so the rules shouldn't reward it for its own
  sake."
- "Inequality is only fair if it makes the worst-off better off."
- "Basic liberties come first; you can't trade them for more wealth."
- "Laws should rest on reasons everyone can accept, not on one religion's view of the good
  life."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Social liberalism:** its parent; supports welfare and opportunity without committing
  to the difference principle or the contractualist method (_Distribution_, _Desert_;
  Reasons).
- **Perfectionist liberalism:** lets the state promote valuable ways of life; egalitarian
  liberalism requires neutrality among them (_Neutrality vs. perfectionism_, the central
  difference).
- **Classical liberalism:** treats market outcomes from fair exchange as legitimate
  entitlements (_Distribution_, _Desert_).
- **Social democracy:** reaches similar Positions through solidarity and working-class
  politics rather than a social contract among individuals (_Workplace governance_;
  Reasons).
- **Luck egalitarianism:** a Foundation Label that holds inequalities fair when they
  follow from choices; egalitarian liberalism in Rawls's form does not tie justice to
  choice in this way (_Causes of fortune_; Reasons).

**Sources.** John Rawls, _A Theory of Justice_ (1971), _Political Liberalism_ (1993),
and _Justice as Fairness: A Restatement_ (2001); Thomas Nagel, _Equality and
Partiality_ (1991); Brian Barry, _Justice as Impartiality_ (1995); Ronald Dworkin,
_Sovereign Virtue_ (2000); Samuel Freeman, _Rawls_ (2007); Leif Wenar, "John Rawls",
_Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy_.

## Perfectionist liberalism

- **Type:** Ideology
- **Parent:** Liberalism
- **Also called:** liberal perfectionism; autonomy-based liberalism
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                     | Range                                                                                        | Central |
| ----------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------- |
| Neutrality vs. perfectionism  | Leans _State perfectionism_ – Strongly _State perfectionism_                                 | Yes     |
| Legal moralism                | Strongly _Liberty of self-regarding conduct_ – Leans _Liberty of self-regarding conduct_     | Yes     |
| Self-authorship or given role | Strongly _Author your own life_ – Leans _Author your own life_                               | Yes     |
| What makes a life go well     | Leans _Some things are good for anyone_ – Strongly _Some things are good for anyone_         | No      |
| One master value or many      | Leans _Plural, conflicting values_ – Strongly _Plural, conflicting values_                   | No      |
| Welfare and social insurance  | Middle – Strongly _Public social insurance_                                                  | No      |
| Paternalism                   | Leans _Protection_ – Leans _Choice_                                                          | No      |
| Pluralism vs. unity           | Strongly _Legitimate pluralism and opposition_ – Leans _Legitimate pluralism and opposition_ | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Personal autonomy, being the author of your own life, is
genuinely valuable, not just one preference among others. A person can only be
autonomous if they have a range of worthwhile options to choose from, and a healthy
culture of such options does not maintain itself. So the state may, and sometimes
should, support valuable ways of living (the arts, education, rich forms of community)
while never coercing anyone into them, because coercion would destroy the very autonomy
it means to serve.

**Key concepts.**

- **Autonomy:** an objective good, the capacity to shape your own life through
  successive choices among worthwhile options.
- **Neutrality:** rejected as a principle; a state cannot avoid shaping which options
  exist, so it should shape them well.
- **Harm principle:** kept, but justified by autonomy: coercion is legitimate mainly to
  prevent harm, including harm to people's autonomy.
- **Value pluralism:** there are many valuable, often incompatible ways to live, so
  promoting value does not mean promoting one way of life.

**Signature reasons.**

- "Autonomy is genuinely good, not just one preference among many."
- "You can't choose a good life if there are no good options to choose from."
- "The state shapes culture anyway; it should do it well."
- "Supporting good options is fine; forcing people into them is not."
- "There are many worthwhile ways to live, and a liberal state should keep them all
  open."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Egalitarian liberalism:** requires the state to stay neutral among ideas of the good
  life (_Neutrality vs. perfectionism_, the central difference).
- **Classical liberalism:** wants a limited state with no role in promoting ways of life
  (_Neutrality vs. perfectionism_, _Welfare and social insurance_).
- **Social conservatism:** also lets the state promote the good, but a traditional moral
  and religious good, enforced by law if need be (_Legal moralism_, _Self-authorship or
  given role_).
- **Civic republicanism:** promotes civic virtue and participation rather than personal
  autonomy (_Citizenship: rights vs. duties_; Reasons).

**Sources.** John Stuart Mill, _On Liberty_ (1859), ch. 3; Joseph Raz, _The Morality of
Freedom_ (1986); William Galston, _Liberal Purposes_ (1991); Thomas Hurka,
_Perfectionism_ (1993); George Sher, _Beyond Neutrality_ (1997); Steven Wall,
_Liberalism, Perfectionism and Restraint_ (1998); Steven Wall, "Perfectionism in Moral
and Political Philosophy", _Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy_.

## Ordoliberalism

- **Type:** Ideology
- **Parent:** Liberalism
- **Also called:** the Freiburg school; neoliberalism (its own name in the 1930s–50s, now
  mainly critics')
- **Variants:** Social market economy
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                                         | Range                                                                                            | Central |
| ------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------- |
| Economic coordination                             | Leans _Market coordination_                                                                      | Yes     |
| Economic scale and concentration                  | Leans _Dispersed ownership and small scale_ – Strongly _Dispersed ownership and small scale_     | Yes     |
| Money and public finance                          | Leans _Rules and fiscal discipline_ – Strongly _Rules and fiscal discipline_                     | Yes     |
| Rule of law vs. discretion                        | Leans _Rule by general, fixed rules_ – Strongly _Rule by general, fixed rules_                   | No      |
| Political authority                               | Strongly _Legitimate state authority_                                                            | No      |
| Design vs. evolved order                          | Middle – Leans _Deliberate design_                                                               | No      |
| Popular sovereignty vs. constitutional constraint | Strongly _Entrenched rights and judicial review_ – Leans _Entrenched rights and judicial review_ | No      |
| Popular judgment vs. expertise                    | Leans _Rule by the knowledgeable_                                                                | No      |
| Trade and economic openness                       | Strongly _Open exchange_ – Leans _Open exchange_                                                 | No      |
| Property in capital and goods                     | Leans _Robust private ownership_ – Middle                                                        | No      |
| Distribution                                      | Leans _Market entitlement_ – Middle                                                              | No      |
| Welfare and social insurance                      | Leans _Self-reliance and voluntary provision_ – Leans _Public social insurance_                  | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** A free market is not what is left when the state steps back. Left
alone, markets drift toward cartels, monopolies, and capture by organized interests,
and then economic power turns into political power. A strong, rule-bound state must
therefore set up and police the framework of competition (the "economic constitution")
and protect the value of money, while keeping out of the day-to-day running of the
economy. A competitive order under the rule of law is what keeps both the economy and
the state free.

**Key concepts.**

- **Competition:** a public good that the state must constitute and protect, not a
  natural state of affairs.
- **Strong state:** strong enough to resist interest groups and private power, and
  limited to setting the rules rather than steering outcomes.
- **Economic constitution:** binding framework rules (competition law, stable money,
  liability, open markets) decided as a whole rather than by ad hoc intervention.
- **Private power:** cartels and monopolies threaten freedom as much as an overbearing
  state.

**Signature reasons.**

- "The state should set the rules of the game, not play it."
- "Monopolies and cartels are a threat to freedom, not just to efficiency."
- "Stable money is a precondition of a free society."
- "A weak state gets captured by special interests."
- "Markets don't sustain themselves; someone has to keep them competitive."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Classical liberalism:** trusts markets to sustain themselves largely without a
  designed framework (_Design vs. evolved order_, _Economic scale and concentration_).
- **Chicago school:** tolerates concentration when it is efficient and judges antitrust
  by consumer welfare (_Economic scale and concentration_).
- **Austrian school:** sees market order as spontaneous and distrusts designing it
  (_Design vs. evolved order_).
- **Christian democracy:** shares the social market economy, but grounds it in Catholic
  social teaching, subsidiarity, and family, and builds a larger welfare state
  (_Civil society vs. the state_, _Welfare and social insurance_; Reasons).
- **Neo-Brandeisianism:** also fights concentration, but for democratic and republican
  reasons and with more willingness to direct the economy (_Economic coordination_;
  Reasons).

**Sources.** Franz Böhm, Walter Eucken & Hans Großmann-Doerth, "The Ordo Manifesto of
1936"; Wilhelm Röpke, _The Social Crisis of Our Time_ (1942) and _A Humane Economy_
(1960); Alfred Müller-Armack, _Wirtschaftslenkung und Marktwirtschaft_ (1947); Walter
Eucken, _Grundsätze der Wirtschaftspolitik_ (1952); Ludwig Erhard, _Prosperity Through
Competition_ (1957); Viktor Vanberg, "The Freiburg School: Walter Eucken and
Ordoliberalism" (2004).

## Liberal internationalism

- **Type:** Ideology (usually Area)
- **Parent:** Liberalism
- **Also called:** Wilsonianism; liberal institutionalism (its international-relations
  theory); liberal interventionism (its interventionist wing); critics: "liberal
  hegemony"
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                                   | Range                                                                                                        | Central |
| ------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------- |
| Sovereignty vs. pooled governance           | Strongly _Pooled, rule-bound international governance_ – Leans _Pooled, rule-bound international governance_ | Yes     |
| Foreign-policy aims                         | Strongly _Universal values_ – Leans _Universal values_                                                       | Yes     |
| Trade and economic openness                 | Strongly _Open exchange_ – Leans _Open exchange_                                                             | Yes     |
| Use of force                                | Leans _Readiness to use force_ – Leans _Reluctance to use force_                                             | No      |
| Compatriot priority vs. global impartiality | Middle – Leans _Impartial concern for all people_                                                            | No      |
| Zero-sum or positive-sum                    | Leans _Gains can be shared_ – Strongly _Gains can be shared_                                                 | No      |
| Direction of history                        | Strongly _History is progressing_ – Leans _History is progressing_                                           | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Wars and tyranny are not facts of nature. Countries that trade
with each other, settle disputes through law and shared institutions, and govern
themselves democratically rarely go to war with one another. So the way to a safer and
more prosperous world is to build a rules-based international order: open trade,
international law and courts, alliances and institutions that bind the strong as well
as the weak, and support for democracy and human rights abroad.

**Key concepts.**

- **International order:** rules and institutions that make cooperation between states
  predictable and restrain the use of power.
- **Interdependence:** trade and exchange give countries a stake in each other's peace
  and prosperity.
- **Democratic peace:** liberal democracies rarely fight one another, so spreading
  democracy spreads peace.
- **Human rights:** universal, and a legitimate concern of foreign policy, not only a
  domestic matter.

**Signature reasons.**

- "Countries that trade with each other don't go to war with each other."
- "Rules and institutions protect small countries from big ones."
- "Human rights don't stop at the border."
- "Democracies make better neighbors."
- "Cooperation makes everyone better off; it isn't a zero-sum game."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Realism:** foreign policy should pursue national interest and balance power in an
  anarchic world, not promote values (_Foreign-policy aims_, the central difference).
- **Neoconservatism:** also promotes democracy abroad, but relies on American power and
  is more willing to act alone, outside international institutions (_Sovereignty vs.
  pooled governance_, _Use of force_).
- **Restraint:** avoids foreign wars and entangling commitments (_Use of force_,
  _Foreign-policy aims_).
- **World federalism:** wants a world government above states, not cooperation among
  sovereign states (_Sovereignty vs. pooled governance_ at the far end; Reasons).
- **Cosmopolitanism:** a Thin Label for impartial concern for all people, which can ride on
  many hosts; liberal internationalism is a program for relations between states
  (_Compatriot priority vs. global impartiality_; Reasons).

**Sources.** Immanuel Kant, _Perpetual Peace_ (1795); Norman Angell, _The Great
Illusion_ (1910); Woodrow Wilson, "Fourteen Points" (1918); the Atlantic Charter (1941);
Robert Keohane & Joseph Nye, _Power and Interdependence_ (1977); Michael Doyle, "Kant,
Liberal Legacies, and Foreign Affairs" (1983); G. John Ikenberry, _Liberal Leviathan_
(2011) and _A World Safe for Democracy_ (2020).

## Progressivism

- **Type:** Ideology
- **Parent:** Liberalism / Social democracy
- **Also called:** the Progressive movement (historical, US, 1890s–1920s); progressive
  liberalism
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                           | Range                                                                                  | Central |
| ----------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------- |
| Distribution                        | Leans _Egalitarian redistribution_ – Strongly _Egalitarian redistribution_             | Yes     |
| Welfare and social insurance        | Leans _Public social insurance_ – Strongly _Public social insurance_                   | Yes     |
| Economic coordination               | Leans _Public direction_ – Leans _Market coordination_                                 | No      |
| Design vs. evolved order            | Leans _Deliberate design_ – Strongly _Deliberate design_                               | No      |
| Expert trust                        | Strongly _Defer to expert institutions_ – Leans _Defer to expert institutions_         | No      |
| Tradition or reason                 | Leans _Reason should audit inheritance_ – Strongly _Reason should audit inheritance_   | No      |
| Direction of history                | Strongly _History is progressing_ – Leans _History is progressing_                     | No      |
| Causes of fortune                   | Leans _Luck and structure_ – Strongly _Luck and structure_                             | No      |
| Economic scale and concentration    | Middle – Strongly _Dispersed ownership and small scale_                                | No      |
| Environmental protection            | Leans _Ecological protection first_ – Strongly _Ecological protection first_           | No      |
| Sex roles in family and public life | Leans _Equal and interchangeable roles_ – Strongly _Equal and interchangeable roles_   | No      |
| Life and body                       | Strongly _Bodily and reproductive autonomy_ – Leans _Bodily and reproductive autonomy_ | No      |
| Religion and state                  | Leans _Secular public order_ – Strongly _Secular public order_                         | No      |
| Tempo of change                     | Leans _Fundamental transformation_ – Leans _Incremental reform_                        | No      |
| Channels of change                  | Strongly _Within institutions_ – Leans _Within institutions_                           | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Society can be made fairer and better through deliberate reform,
and government is the tool a democratic people uses to do it. Unregulated markets,
concentrated corporate power, and inherited prejudice produce injustices that do not
correct themselves, so active government, guided by evidence and expertise, should
regulate business, guarantee social protection, and widen opportunity. History shows
that each generation can extend rights and fairness further than the last, and the
work is not finished.

**Key concepts.**

- **Progress:** moral and social improvement is real and achievable through collective
  action; the past is not a guide to be preserved.
- **Government:** an instrument of the democratic public for solving problems markets
  and private charity cannot.
- **Expertise:** evidence, science, and trained administration should inform policy.
- **Equality:** widening opportunity and rights to groups previously excluded.

**Signature reasons.**

- "Government can and should fix problems the market won't."
- "Follow the evidence and listen to the experts."
- "The arc of history bends toward justice, but only if we push it."
- "Big corporations need to be held accountable."
- "Health care, education, and a living wage should be basic guarantees."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Social liberalism:** shares the welfare Positions, but grounds them in individual
  autonomy and puts less faith in expert-designed government (_Design vs. evolved
  order_, _Expert trust_; Reasons).
- **Social democracy:** builds on unions, class solidarity, and universal welfare as a
  settled model, rather than ongoing reform guided by expertise (_Workplace governance_;
  Reasons).
- **Identity-conscious progressivism:** adds group-conscious remedies and treats
  inherited structures of race and gender as the central injustice (_Group difference_,
  _Historical injustice_, _Speech and expression_).
- **Technocracy:** would hand decisions to experts in place of democratic politics;
  progressivism uses expertise in service of democratic majorities (_Popular judgment vs.
  expertise_).
- **Democratic socialism:** wants to move beyond capitalism toward social ownership
  (_Property in capital and goods_, _Economic coordination_).

**Sources.** Herbert Croly, _The Promise of American Life_ (1909); Theodore Roosevelt,
"The New Nationalism" (1910); Woodrow Wilson, _The New Freedom_ (1913); Walter Lippmann,
_Drift and Mastery_ (1914); John Dewey, _Liberalism and Social Action_ (1935); Richard
Hofstadter, _The Age of Reform_ (1955); Richard Rorty, _Achieving Our Country_ (1998);
Paul Krugman, _The Conscience of a Liberal_ (2007).

## Multicultural liberalism

- **Type:** Ideology
- **Parent:** Liberalism
- **Also called:** liberal multiculturalism; liberal culturalism
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                                         | Range                                                                                            | Central |
| ------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------- |
| Group difference                                  | Strongly _Group-differentiated recognition_ – Leans _Group-differentiated recognition_           | Yes     |
| Self-authorship or given role                     | Strongly _Author your own life_ – Leans _Author your own life_                                   | Yes     |
| Individuals or wholes                             | Middle – Leans _Groups and relations are real_                                                   | No      |
| Local vs. central                                 | Strongly _Local and regional self-rule_ – Leans _Local and regional self-rule_                   | No      |
| Majoritarian vs. consensus                        | Leans _Power-sharing and broad consensus_ – Strongly _Power-sharing and broad consensus_         | No      |
| Historical injustice                              | Middle – Strongly _Rectification_                                                                | No      |
| Basis of nationhood                               | Leans _Shared civic principles_ – Middle                                                         | No      |
| Popular sovereignty vs. constitutional constraint | Strongly _Entrenched rights and judicial review_ – Leans _Entrenched rights and judicial review_ | No      |
| Pluralism vs. unity                               | Strongly _Legitimate pluralism and opposition_                                                   | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** People make meaningful choices only against the background of a
culture: a language, history, and set of practices that give their options meaning.
When a state treats everyone "the same", it in fact favors the majority's culture, so
minorities, national groups, and Indigenous peoples can be left without the secure
cultural context that freedom needs. Liberal justice therefore allows group-
differentiated rights, such as language rights, self-government, and exemptions, as
long as they protect groups from outside pressure and never let a group restrict its own
members' basic liberties.

**Key concepts.**

- **Culture:** a "context of choice" that gives individual options their meaning, so
  secure cultural membership is a condition of autonomy.
- **Group-differentiated rights:** self-government for national minorities, accommodation
  for immigrant groups, and special protections where common rules fall unevenly.
- **External protections, not internal restrictions:** groups may be shielded from the
  majority, but may not deny their own members liberal rights, including the right to
  leave.
- **Neutrality:** impossible in matters like language and public holidays, so fairness
  requires deliberate accommodation.

**Signature reasons.**

- "Treating everyone the same can mean treating minorities unfairly."
- "You can't choose freely without a culture that gives your choices meaning."
- "Minority nations should be able to govern themselves in their own language."
- "Groups can be protected from the majority, but they can't oppress their own members."
- "A state can't be neutral about which language it uses."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Egalitarian liberalism:** in many versions (e.g. Barry's) insists on the same rules for
  all and treats culture as a private matter (_Group difference_, the central
  difference).
- **Multiculturalism:** a Thin Label for recognizing cultural diversity, which can rest on
  non-liberal grounds of identity or recognition without the autonomy limit (Reasons;
  _Self-authorship or given role_).
- **Communitarianism:** sees the self as constituted by community and lets shared goods
  outrank autonomy (_Individuals or wholes_, _Self-authorship or given role_).
- **Liberal nationalism:** defends the majority's national culture as the basis of
  solidarity (_Basis of nationhood_; Reasons).
- **French republicanism:** keeps group identity out of public institutions entirely
  (_Group difference_ at the opposite end).

**Sources.** Will Kymlicka, _Liberalism, Community and Culture_ (1989) and _Multicultural
Citizenship_ (1995); Charles Taylor, "The Politics of Recognition" (1992); Joseph Carens,
_Culture, Citizenship, and Community_ (2000); Jacob T. Levy, _The Multiculturalism of
Fear_ (2000); Alan Patten, _Equal Recognition_ (2014); Canadian Multiculturalism Act
(1988).

## Abundance liberalism

- **Type:** Ideology
- **Parent:** Liberalism
- **Also called:** the abundance agenda; supply-side progressivism; YIMBYism (its
  housing movement, "Yes In My Back Yard")
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                        | Range                                                                                              | Central |
| -------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------- |
| Growth                           | Leans _Growth and abundance_ – Strongly _Growth and abundance_                                     | Yes     |
| Majoritarian vs. consensus       | Strongly _Clear majority rule and accountability_ – Leans _Clear majority rule and accountability_ | Yes     |
| Technology and risk              | Strongly _Proaction_ – Leans _Proaction_                                                           | No      |
| Local vs. central                | Leans _Central and uniform authority_ – Strongly _Central and uniform authority_                   | No      |
| Representation vs. participation | Leans _Representative, trustee government_ – Strongly _Representative, trustee government_         | No      |
| Economic coordination            | Leans _Public direction_ – Leans _Market coordination_                                             | No      |
| Economic scale and concentration | Strongly _Scale and efficiency_ – Middle                                                           | No      |
| Immigration and borders          | Middle – Leans _Freedom of movement_                                                               | No      |
| Distribution                     | Middle – Leans _Egalitarian redistribution_                                                        | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** The things people most need, like homes, clean energy, transit,
and medicine, are scarce and expensive mainly because we have made them hard to build.
Layers of procedure, lawsuits, local vetoes, and permitting rules, many created with
good intentions, now block the very projects that would make life affordable and
decarbonize the economy. A liberal politics worthy of the name should be judged by what
it delivers: build housing, energy, and infrastructure at scale, give government the
capacity to act, and stop letting small groups veto what the many need.

**Key concepts.**

- **Scarcity:** often a policy choice, produced by rules that make building slow and
  costly, not a fixed fact.
- **Vetocracy:** a system where many actors can block and no one can deliver; the main
  obstacle to progress.
- **State capacity:** government's ability to execute, which matters as much as its
  intentions or budget.
- **Growth:** good for people and compatible with climate goals when clean supply is built
  fast enough.

**Signature reasons.**

- "Housing is expensive because we made it illegal to build enough of it."
- "You can't decarbonize if it takes ten years to approve a power line."
- "Process isn't the same thing as progress."
- "A neighborhood meeting shouldn't be able to block homes for thousands of people."
- "Government should be judged by what it delivers, not what it promises."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Progressivism:** often prizes participation, procedure, and local input as safeguards;
  abundance liberalism sees them as the main obstacle to delivery (_Majoritarian vs.
  consensus_, _Representation vs. participation_, _Local vs. central_).
- **Degrowth:** the opposite answer on growth: a sufficient economy within limits
  (_Growth_).
- **Ecomodernism:** shares the build-clean-energy program, but starts from an
  environmental philosophy about decoupling humanity from nature (_Standing of nature_;
  Reasons).
- **Classical liberalism:** shares the deregulatory instinct, but resists the public
  investment and state capacity abundance liberals want (_Economic coordination_,
  _Welfare and social insurance_).
- **Neo-Brandeisianism:** the rival on concentration: breaks up big firms, where abundance
  liberalism tolerates scale that delivers (_Economic scale and concentration_).

**Sources.** Edward Glaeser, _Triumph of the City_ (2011); Nicholas Bagley, "The
Procedure Fetish" (2019); Ezra Klein & Derek Thompson, _Abundance_ (2025); Marc J.
Dunkelman, _Why Nothing Works_ (2025); Yoni Appelbaum, _Stuck_ (2025).

## Market liberalism

- **Type:** Ideology
- **Parent:** Liberalism
- **Also called:** the Washington Consensus (its 1990s development-policy form);
  critics: "neoliberalism"
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                                         | Range                                                                                                        | Central |
| ------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------- |
| Trade and economic openness                       | Strongly _Open exchange_                                                                                     | Yes     |
| Economic coordination                             | Leans _Market coordination_ – Strongly _Market coordination_                                                 | Yes     |
| Money and public finance                          | Leans _Rules and fiscal discipline_ – Strongly _Rules and fiscal discipline_                                 | Yes     |
| Sovereignty vs. pooled governance                 | Strongly _Pooled, rule-bound international governance_ – Leans _Pooled, rule-bound international governance_ | Yes     |
| Popular judgment vs. expertise                    | Strongly _Rule by the knowledgeable_ – Leans _Rule by the knowledgeable_                                     | No      |
| Property in capital and goods                     | Strongly _Robust private ownership_ – Leans _Robust private ownership_                                       | No      |
| Distribution                                      | Strongly _Market entitlement_ – Middle                                                                       | No      |
| Welfare and social insurance                      | Leans _Self-reliance and voluntary provision_ – Middle                                                       | No      |
| Immigration and borders                           | Middle – Leans _Freedom of movement_                                                                         | No      |
| Zero-sum or positive-sum                          | Leans _Gains can be shared_ – Strongly _Gains can be shared_                                                 | No      |
| Popular sovereignty vs. constitutional constraint | Strongly _Entrenched rights and judicial review_ – Leans _Entrenched rights and judicial review_             | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Open trade, sound money, competitive markets, and secure property
have lifted more people out of poverty than any other policy in history. Countries
prosper when they keep inflation low, budgets sustainable, and markets open, and when
they bind themselves to international rules that make those commitments credible and
protect them from short-term political pressure. Global economic integration, managed
by rule-bound institutions, is the surest path to shared prosperity.

**Key concepts.**

- **Globalization:** the integration of markets across borders, a positive-sum process
  that spreads growth and specialization.
- **Sound money:** low, predictable inflation, safeguarded by independent central banks.
- **Credible commitment:** binding rules (trade agreements, fiscal rules, central-bank
  independence) protect good policy from political cycles.
- **Privatization:** markets usually deliver goods and services more efficiently than
  state ownership.

**Signature reasons.**

- "Free trade has done more for the world's poor than any aid program."
- "Inflation is a tax on the poor; central banks must be independent."
- "Countries that open up get richer."
- "Governments shouldn't run businesses."
- "International rules keep politicians from wrecking the economy for short-term gain."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Classical liberalism:** the older, broader philosophy of limited government and
  natural rights; market liberalism is a policy program built around international
  economic institutions and independent central banks (_Sovereignty vs. pooled
  governance_, _Popular judgment vs. expertise_; Reasons).
- **Ordoliberalism:** puts breaking up concentrated private power at the center
  (_Economic scale and concentration_).
- **New Right:** pairs market economics with nation, order, and family, and is often
  wary of supranational bodies (_Sovereignty vs. pooled governance_, _Security and civil
  liberties_).
- **Third Way:** accepts the market framework, but adds social investment and active
  welfare (_Welfare and social insurance_, _Distribution_).
- **Liberal internationalism:** pursues peace, security, and democracy through
  institutions, not mainly economic policy (_Foreign-policy aims_; Reasons).

**Sources.** Mont Pelerin Society, "Statement of Aims" (1947); Milton Friedman,
_Capitalism and Freedom_ (1962); John Williamson, "What Washington Means by Policy
Reform" (1990); Thomas Friedman, _The Lexus and the Olive Tree_ (1999); Jagdish
Bhagwati, _In Defense of Globalization_ (2004); Martin Wolf, _Why Globalization Works_
(2004); Quinn Slobodian, _Globalists_ (2018).

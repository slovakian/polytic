# Socialism and social democracy: Label cards

Cards for this family, written to the template in `../label-library.md`, in the order
of the list. Every card here is written with the same dispassion as any other: the
self-description is the tradition's own self-understanding, steelmanned, and never
Polytic's verdict (`../principles.md`).

## Socialism

- **Type:** Ideology
- **Parent:** —
- **Also called:** the socialist tradition; ethical socialism (its non-Marxist moral
  strand)
- **Variants:** Utopian socialism
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                     | Range                                                                                  | Central |
| ----------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------- |
| Property in capital and goods | Leans _Socially conditioned ownership_ – Strongly _Socially conditioned ownership_     | Yes     |
| Distribution                  | Leans _Egalitarian redistribution_ – Strongly _Egalitarian redistribution_             | Yes     |
| Economic coordination         | Strongly _Public direction_ – Middle                                                   | No      |
| Workplace governance          | Strongly _Worker voice and self-management_ – Leans _Worker voice and self-management_ | No      |
| Welfare and social insurance  | Leans _Public social insurance_ – Strongly _Public social insurance_                   | No      |
| Moral limits of markets       | Strongly _Protected non-market spheres_ – Leans _Protected non-market spheres_         | No      |
| Hierarchy or equality         | Leans _Equality is basic_ – Strongly _Equality is basic_                               | No      |
| Causes of fortune             | Leans _Luck and structure_ – Strongly _Luck and structure_                             | No      |
| Individuals or wholes         | Middle – Leans _Groups and relations are real_                                         | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Wealth is produced by everyone's work together, yet under
capitalism those who own the means of production command both the economy and the
working lives of everyone else. Socialism puts productive wealth under common control,
whether through the state, co-operatives, or communities, so that economic life answers
to human need, equality, and solidarity rather than to private profit. Freedom means
little to people who lack the means to use it.

**Key concepts.**

- **Property:** productive property (land, factories, capital) is a social product and
  a source of power over others, not a purely private matter; personal possessions are
  another thing.
- **Equality:** equal standing and a roughly equal share in what society produces, not
  only equal opportunity to compete for unequal rewards.
- **Freedom:** freedom from domination by employers and from want, which requires
  security and a voice at work, not only freedom from state interference.
- **Solidarity:** mutual obligation among those who work, expressed in shared
  institutions rather than charity.
- **Class:** the divide between those who own capital and those who must sell their
  labour, which shapes life chances more than individual effort.

**Signature reasons.**

- "Production should be for need, not for profit."
- "Wealth is created together, so it should be shared together."
- "Owning capital shouldn't give anyone command over another person's working life."
- "Freedom means nothing without the means to use it."
- "A society is judged by how it treats those with the least."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Social liberalism:** shares the welfare and redistribution Positions, but keeps
  private ownership of capital and grounds them in individual autonomy rather than
  common ownership and class (_Property in capital and goods_; Reasons).
- **Marxism:** a branch of socialism with a specific theory of history and class
  struggle, and a revolutionary horizon (_Direction of history_, _Tempo of change_);
  socialism as such need not hold either.
- **Anarchism:** many anarchists share the economic goal but reject the state as its
  instrument (_Political authority_).
- **Distributism:** also rejects concentrated capitalist ownership, but answers with
  widely held private property, not common ownership (_Property in capital and goods_,
  _Economic scale and concentration_).
- **Christian socialism:** the same economic shape, grounded in the Gospel (_Transcendence_;
  Reasons).

**Sources.** Robert Owen, _A New View of Society_ (1813–16); R. H. Tawney, _Equality_
(1931); G. A. Cohen, _Why Not Socialism?_ (2009); Axel Honneth, _The Idea of Socialism_
(2015; trans. 2017); Michael Newman, _Socialism: A Very Short Introduction_ (2005);
Donald Sassoon, _One Hundred Years of Socialism_ (1996); Richard Arnold, "Socialism",
_Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy_.

## Democratic socialism

- **Type:** Ideology
- **Parent:** Socialism
- **Also called:** socialism (in much current US usage)
- **Variants:** Eurocommunism
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                        | Range                                                                                        | Central |
| -------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------- |
| Property in capital and goods    | Leans _Socially conditioned ownership_ – Strongly _Socially conditioned ownership_           | Yes     |
| Economic coordination            | Strongly _Public direction_ – Middle                                                         | Yes     |
| Pluralism vs. unity              | Strongly _Legitimate pluralism and opposition_ – Leans _Legitimate pluralism and opposition_ | Yes     |
| Channels of change               | Strongly _Within institutions_ – Middle                                                      | Yes     |
| Distribution                     | Leans _Egalitarian redistribution_ – Strongly _Egalitarian redistribution_                   | No      |
| Workplace governance             | Strongly _Worker voice and self-management_ – Leans _Worker voice and self-management_       | No      |
| Welfare and social insurance     | Leans _Public social insurance_ – Strongly _Public social insurance_                         | No      |
| Tempo of change                  | Leans _Fundamental transformation_ – Leans _Incremental reform_                              | No      |
| Leadership                       | Leans _Dispersed, checked authority_ – Strongly _Dispersed, checked authority_               | No      |
| Representation vs. participation | Leans _Direct and participatory self-rule_ – Middle                                          | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Capitalism concentrates economic power in a few hands, and
democracy stops at the office and factory door. Democratic socialists aim to extend
democracy into the economy, replacing private ownership of the major enterprises with
public, social, and worker ownership. That has to be won through elections, unions, and
mass movements, keeping civil liberties and competing parties, because socialism
imposed without democracy betrays its own purpose.

**Key concepts.**

- **Democracy:** self-government that should reach economic life, not stop at elections.
- **Socialism:** a goal beyond welfare capitalism: social ownership of the commanding
  heights, not only taxes and transfers.
- **Reform:** "non-reformist reforms" that shift power to working people and build
  toward a different system, rather than only cushioning the existing one.
- **Freedom:** civil liberties and political pluralism are non-negotiable, in the
  movement and after it wins.

**Signature reasons.**

- "Democracy shouldn't stop at the office door."
- "The goal isn't to tame capitalism. It's to move beyond it."
- "Socialism without democracy isn't socialism."
- "Health care, housing and energy should be public goods, not commodities."
- "Win elections and build the movement; you need both."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Social democracy:** aims to tame capitalism within a mixed economy; democratic
  socialism aims to replace it over time (_Property in capital and goods_, _Economic
  coordination_).
- **Marxism–Leninism:** seeks socialism through a vanguard party and one-party rule;
  democratic socialism's defining commitment is pluralist, constitutional change
  (_Pluralism vs. unity_, _Channels of change_, _Leadership_).
- **Market socialism:** keeps markets as the main coordinator under social ownership;
  democratic socialists more often favour planning and public provision (_Economic
  coordination_).
- **Libertarian socialism:** pursues self-management without the state; democratic
  socialism uses the democratic state as its main instrument (_Political authority_).

**Sources.** Michael Harrington, _Socialism: Past and Future_ (1989); André Gorz,
_Strategy for Labor_ (1964; trans. 1967); Ralph Miliband, _Socialism for a Sceptical
Age_ (1994); Santiago Carrillo, _Eurocommunism and the State_ (1977); Erik Olin Wright,
_How to Be an Anticapitalist in the Twenty-First Century_ (2019); Bhaskar Sunkara, _The
Socialist Manifesto_ (2019).

## Social democracy

- **Type:** Ideology
- **Parent:** Socialism
- **Also called:** the Nordic model (for its Scandinavian form); revisionism
  (historical, Bernstein)
- **Variants:** Fabianism
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                     | Range                                                                                        | Central |
| ----------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------- |
| Distribution                  | Leans _Egalitarian redistribution_ – Strongly _Egalitarian redistribution_                   | Yes     |
| Welfare and social insurance  | Leans _Public social insurance_ – Strongly _Public social insurance_                         | Yes     |
| Economic coordination         | Middle – Leans _Market coordination_                                                         | Yes     |
| Channels of change            | Strongly _Within institutions_ – Leans _Within institutions_                                 | Yes     |
| Pluralism vs. unity           | Strongly _Legitimate pluralism and opposition_ – Leans _Legitimate pluralism and opposition_ | Yes     |
| Tempo of change               | Leans _Incremental reform_ – Strongly _Incremental reform_                                   | No      |
| Workplace governance          | Leans _Worker voice and self-management_ – Middle                                            | No      |
| Property in capital and goods | Middle – Leans _Socially conditioned ownership_                                              | No      |
| Reciprocity in support        | Leans _Unconditional support_ – Leans _Conditional (reciprocal) support_                     | No      |
| Political authority           | Strongly _Legitimate state authority_ – Leans _Legitimate state authority_                   | No      |
| Leadership                    | Leans _Dispersed, checked authority_ – Strongly _Dispersed, checked authority_               | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Markets are powerful engines of prosperity, but left to
themselves they produce insecurity and inequality that make freedom hollow for most
people. Through democratic politics, strong unions, and a universal welfare state, a
society can keep the dynamism of a market economy while giving everyone real security
and a fair share of what they help produce. Change comes by winning elections and
building institutions, not by revolution.

**Key concepts.**

- **Freedom:** the real ability to live your own life, which needs security against
  illness, unemployment, and old age, not only freedom from interference.
- **Equality:** equal life chances and narrow class differences, not identical outcomes.
- **Solidarity:** mutual obligation across classes, built into universal programs that
  everyone pays into and everyone uses.
- **Democracy:** the primary instrument of change, extended where possible into working
  life (collective bargaining, co-determination).

**Signature reasons.**

- "The market is a good servant and a bad master."
- "Universal welfare makes freedom real: you are not free if one illness can ruin you."
- "Strong unions and collective bargaining are how workers get a fair share."
- "We change society by winning elections, not by tearing it down."
- "High taxes are the price of a decent society, and they're worth it."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Democratic socialism:** wants to move beyond capitalism over time (more public or
  social ownership); social democracy aims to tame capitalism, not replace it
  (_Economic coordination_, _Property in capital and goods_).
- **Social liberalism:** can share the welfare Positions, but grounds them in individual
  autonomy and fair opportunity rather than solidarity and class, and gives less weight
  to unions and worker voice (_Workplace governance_; Reasons).
- **Third Way:** more market-led, and makes support conditional on contribution and
  "social investment" (_Reciprocity in support_ toward _Conditional_, _Economic
  coordination_ toward _Market coordination_).
- **Christian democracy:** also builds a generous welfare state, but through subsidiarity,
  family, and church, with a public role for religion (_Civil society vs. the state_,
  _Religion and state_).
- **Marxism–Leninism:** revolutionary, one-party, and planned; social democracy's defining
  break with it is pluralist, parliamentary reform (_Pluralism vs. unity_, _Channels of
  change_).

**Sources.** Eduard Bernstein, _Evolutionary Socialism_ (1899); SPD, Godesberg Program
(1959); Anthony Crosland, _The Future of Socialism_ (1956); Gøsta Esping-Andersen, _The
Three Worlds of Welfare Capitalism_ (1990); Sheri Berman, _The Primacy of Politics_
(2006); Donald Sassoon, _One Hundred Years of Socialism_ (1996).

## Third Way

- **Type:** Ideology
- **Parent:** Social democracy
- **Also called:** die Neue Mitte (the "new centre", its German form),
  New Labour (its British form), New Democrats (its US form); critics: "neoliberal"
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                         | Range                                                                                  | Central |
| --------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------- |
| Economic coordination             | Leans _Market coordination_                                                            | Yes     |
| Reciprocity in support            | Leans _Conditional (reciprocal) support_ – Strongly _Conditional (reciprocal) support_ | Yes     |
| Welfare and social insurance      | Middle – Leans _Public social insurance_                                               | No      |
| Distribution                      | Middle – Leans _Egalitarian redistribution_                                            | No      |
| Property in capital and goods     | Leans _Robust private ownership_ – Middle                                              | No      |
| Workplace governance              | Middle – Leans _Owner and managerial authority_                                        | No      |
| Trade and economic openness       | Strongly _Open exchange_ – Leans _Open exchange_                                       | No      |
| Sovereignty vs. pooled governance | Strongly _Pooled, rule-bound international governance_ – Middle                        | No      |
| Security and civil liberties      | Middle – Leans _Collective security and order_                                         | No      |
| Popular judgment vs. expertise    | Leans _Rule by the knowledgeable_ – Middle                                             | No      |
| Channels of change                | Strongly _Within institutions_                                                         | No      |
| Tempo of change                   | Leans _Incremental reform_ – Strongly _Incremental reform_                             | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Globalisation and the knowledge economy have made nationalisation
and passive welfare obsolete, but the centre-left's values (social justice, opportunity,
community) matter as much as ever. Government should invest in people rather than
replace markets: education, skills, and work-centred welfare that is a springboard, not
a safety net, in a bargain where rights come with responsibilities. What matters is
what works.

**Key concepts.**

- **Equality:** equal opportunity and inclusion, overcoming exclusion from work and
  society, more than equal outcomes.
- **Responsibility:** "no rights without responsibilities"; support carries obligations
  to work, train, and contribute.
- **Social investment:** spending on education, childcare, and skills that raises
  people's capacity to earn, rather than only compensating them.
- **Modernisation:** the centre-left must adapt its means to a global market economy to
  keep its ends.

**Signature reasons.**

- "What matters is what works."
- "A hand up, not a handout."
- "No rights without responsibilities."
- "Tough on crime, and tough on the causes of crime."
- "Globalisation is a fact; the job is to equip people to thrive in it."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Social democracy:** keeps a larger role for public provision, unions, and
  unconditional universal benefits (_Reciprocity in support_, _Economic coordination_,
  _Workplace governance_).
- **Social liberalism:** can land on very similar Positions; the Third Way frames them
  through community, reciprocity, and the centre-left's labour roots rather than
  individual autonomy (Reasons).
- **Market liberalism:** shares open trade and market coordination, but the Third Way
  keeps an active, investing state and moderate redistribution (_Welfare and social
  insurance_, _Distribution_).
- **Blue Labour:** shares reciprocity, but rejects the Third Way's embrace of
  globalisation, open markets, and high migration (_Trade and economic openness_,
  _Immigration and borders_, _Moral limits of markets_).

**Sources.** Anthony Giddens, _The Third Way: The Renewal of Social Democracy_ (1998)
and _The Third Way and Its Critics_ (2000); Tony Blair & Gerhard Schröder, "Europe: The
Third Way / Die Neue Mitte" (1999); Will Marshall & Martin Schram (eds.), _Mandate for
Change_ (1993); Gøsta Esping-Andersen et al., _Why We Need a New Welfare State_ (2002).

## Market socialism

- **Type:** Ideology
- **Parent:** Socialism
- **Also called:** economic democracy (David Schweickart's model)
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                     | Range                                                                                        | Central |
| ----------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------- |
| Property in capital and goods | Leans _Socially conditioned ownership_ – Strongly _Socially conditioned ownership_           | Yes     |
| Economic coordination         | Middle – Leans _Market coordination_                                                         | Yes     |
| Workplace governance          | Strongly _Worker voice and self-management_ – Middle                                         | No      |
| Distribution                  | Leans _Egalitarian redistribution_ – Strongly _Egalitarian redistribution_                   | No      |
| Welfare and social insurance  | Leans _Public social insurance_ – Strongly _Public social insurance_                         | No      |
| Pluralism vs. unity           | Strongly _Legitimate pluralism and opposition_ – Leans _Legitimate pluralism and opposition_ | No      |
| Channels of change            | Strongly _Within institutions_ – Middle                                                      | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Markets do what planners do badly: they use dispersed information,
respond to what people want, and reward innovation. What turns markets into engines of
inequality and domination is private ownership of capital. So keep the market and
socialise the ownership: firms owned by their workers or by the public compete with one
another, and the returns on capital are shared by everyone instead of a small class of
shareholders.

**Key concepts.**

- **Market:** a coordinating tool, separable from capitalism, and the best one
  available for most goods.
- **Ownership:** the decisive question; social or worker ownership of firms, with
  investment answerable to the public.
- **Equality:** equal shares in the returns on capital, which under capitalism flow to
  the few.
- **Feasibility:** socialism must learn from the failures of central planning and offer
  a model that works.

**Signature reasons.**

- "The problem with capitalism isn't markets. It's who owns the firms."
- "Central planning failed; markets work; ownership is the question."
- "Let worker-owned firms compete, and the profits go to the people who do the work."
- "Investment should answer to the public, not to a handful of shareholders."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Social democracy:** keeps capital in private hands and redistributes afterwards
  (_Property in capital and goods_).
- **Democratic socialism:** gives more weight to planning and public provision
  (_Economic coordination_).
- **Co-operativism:** grows co-operatives voluntarily within the existing economy;
  market socialism designs an economy-wide system, usually through the state
  (_Civil society vs. the state_; Reasons).
- **Mutualism:** also pairs markets with non-capitalist ownership, but without a state
  (_Political authority_).
- **Socialism with Chinese characteristics:** combines markets and public ownership
  under one-party rule (_Pluralism vs. unity_).

**Sources.** Oskar Lange, "On the Economic Theory of Socialism" (1936–37); Alec Nove,
_The Economics of Feasible Socialism_ (1983); David Miller, _Market, State, and
Community_ (1989); Pranab Bardhan & John Roemer (eds.), _Market Socialism: The Current
Debate_ (1993); John Roemer, _A Future for Socialism_ (1994); David Schweickart, _After
Capitalism_ (2002).

## Co-operativism

- **Type:** Ideology
- **Parent:** Socialism
- **Also called:** the co-operative movement; the co-operative commonwealth (its aim
  for the whole economy)
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                        | Range                                                                                                          | Central |
| -------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------- |
| Workplace governance             | Strongly _Worker voice and self-management_ – Leans _Worker voice and self-management_                         | Yes     |
| Civil society vs. the state      | Strongly _Family, community, and voluntary association_ – Leans _Family, community, and voluntary association_ | Yes     |
| Property in capital and goods    | Middle – Leans _Socially conditioned ownership_                                                                | No      |
| Economic scale and concentration | Middle – Leans _Dispersed ownership and small scale_                                                           | No      |
| Economic coordination            | Middle – Leans _Market coordination_                                                                           | No      |
| Local vs. central                | Strongly _Local and regional self-rule_ – Leans _Local and regional self-rule_                                 | No      |
| Channels of change               | Strongly _Within institutions_ – Leans _Within institutions_                                                   | No      |
| Tempo of change                  | Leans _Incremental reform_ – Strongly _Incremental reform_                                                     | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** People can meet their common needs by owning and running
enterprises together, one member, one vote, instead of waiting for capitalists to employ
them or the state to provide for them. Co-operatives put self-help, mutual
responsibility, democracy, and equity into practice now, in shops, banks, farms,
housing, and workplaces. As they grow, they show that an economy can run on co-operation
rather than on profit for absent owners.

**Key concepts.**

- **Ownership:** held by the members who use or work in the enterprise, not by outside
  investors.
- **Democracy:** one member, one vote, whatever each member has put in.
- **Self-help:** people organising to meet their own needs, rather than relying on
  employers or the state.
- **Concern for community:** surpluses serve members and their communities, and
  co-operatives co-operate with one another.

**Signature reasons.**

- "One member, one vote, not one share, one vote."
- "We don't need a boss or a minister to run this. We need each other."
- "Profits should go to the people who use the business or work in it."
- "Every successful co-op proves ordinary people can run an enterprise."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Market socialism:** an economy-wide model, usually set up by the state; co-operativism
  grows voluntarily from below (_Civil society vs. the state_, _Tempo of change_).
- **Mutualism:** shares member ownership and mutual credit, but aims at a society
  without a state (_Political authority_).
- **Anarcho-syndicalism:** also wants workers to run production, but through
  revolutionary unions and a general strike (_Channels of change_, _Tempo of change_).
- **Distributism:** welcomes co-operatives, but centres family property and small private
  firms (_Property in capital and goods_).
- **Social democracy:** relies on the state and unions rather than member-owned
  enterprises (_Civil society vs. the state_).

**Sources.** Rochdale Society of Equitable Pioneers, _Laws and Objects_ (1844);
Beatrice Potter (Webb), _The Co-operative Movement in Great Britain_ (1891); G. D. H.
Cole, _A Century of Co-operation_ (1944); William Foote Whyte & Kathleen King Whyte,
_Making Mondragón_ (1988); International Co-operative Alliance, _Statement on the
Co-operative Identity_ (1995); Jessica Gordon Nembhard, _Collective Courage_ (2014).

## Blue Labour (left conservatism)

- **Type:** Ideology
- **Parent:** Socialism / Post-liberalism
- **Also called:** left conservatism; postliberal Labour
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                        | Range                                                                                                          | Central |
| -------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------- |
| Reciprocity in support           | Leans _Conditional (reciprocal) support_ – Strongly _Conditional (reciprocal) support_                         | Yes     |
| Workplace governance             | Strongly _Worker voice and self-management_ – Leans _Worker voice and self-management_                         | Yes     |
| Moral limits of markets          | Strongly _Protected non-market spheres_ – Leans _Protected non-market spheres_                                 | Yes     |
| Civil society vs. the state      | Strongly _Family, community, and voluntary association_ – Leans _Family, community, and voluntary association_ | Yes     |
| Local vs. central                | Strongly _Local and regional self-rule_ – Leans _Local and regional self-rule_                                 | No      |
| Immigration and borders          | Strongly _Community control over membership_ – Leans _Community control over membership_                       | No      |
| Citizenship: rights vs. duties   | Strongly _Citizenship as shared obligation_ – Leans _Citizenship as shared obligation_                         | No      |
| Distribution                     | Middle – Leans _Egalitarian redistribution_                                                                    | No      |
| Economic scale and concentration | Middle – Leans _Dispersed ownership and small scale_                                                           | No      |
| Trade and economic openness      | Middle – Leans _Economic protection and self-reliance_                                                         | No      |
| Basis of nationhood              | Middle – Leans _Shared heritage and culture_                                                                   | No      |
| Individuals or wholes            | Leans _Groups and relations are real_ – Strongly _Groups and relations are real_                               | No      |
| Tradition or reason              | Strongly _Inherited practice carries wisdom_ – Leans _Inherited practice carries wisdom_                       | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** The labour movement began not as a project of the state or the
market but as working people organising themselves: friendly societies, co-operatives,
unions, and chapels, rooted in family, work, and place. The free-market right and the
managerial left have both hollowed these out, treating people as consumers or as
clients. Politics should rebuild the relationships and institutions (local, vocational,
and reciprocal) that give working people dignity and power, and stop money from ruling
everything.

**Key concepts.**

- **Reciprocity:** "something for something"; entitlement follows contribution and
  belonging.
- **Relationship:** the basic unit of politics; people are members of families,
  workplaces, and places, not isolated individuals.
- **Work:** a source of dignity and vocation, to be protected by worker
  representation and vocational institutions.
- **Paradox:** the labour tradition is both conservative and radical, guarding inherited
  goods (family, faith, place) against the market's churn.
- **Commodification:** labour, land, and money are not ordinary commodities (Polanyi).

**Signature reasons.**

- "Labour was built on family, faith and work, not on Whitehall."
- "The market and the state both treat people as units. We need relationships."
- "Contribution should come before entitlement."
- "Workers should sit on the boards of the companies they work for."
- "A good life is rooted in a place."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Social democracy:** builds a central, universal welfare state; Blue Labour prefers
  local, mutual, and reciprocal institutions (_Civil society vs. the state_, _Local vs.
  central_, _Reciprocity in support_).
- **Third Way:** embraced globalisation and open markets; Blue Labour defines itself
  against them (_Trade and economic openness_, _Immigration and borders_, _Moral limits of
  markets_).
- **Post-liberalism:** shares the critique of liberalism, but Blue Labour starts from the
  labour movement and worker power (_Workplace governance_, _Distribution_; Reasons).
- **One-nation conservatism:** a Tory duty of the privileged to the nation; Blue Labour
  comes from working people's own self-organisation (Reasons).
- **Communitarianism:** shares the focus on community and responsibility, without Blue
  Labour's class politics and union roots (_Workplace governance_; Reasons).

**Sources.** Karl Polanyi, _The Great Transformation_ (1944); Maurice Glasman, Jonathan
Rutherford, Marc Stears & Stuart White (eds.), _The Labour Tradition and the Politics of
Paradox_ (2011); Ian Geary & Adrian Pabst (eds.), _Blue Labour: Forging a New Politics_
(2015); Jon Cruddas, _The Dignity of Labour_ (2021); Maurice Glasman, _Blue Labour: The
Politics of the Common Good_ (2022).

## Libertarian socialism

- **Type:** Ideology
- **Parent:** Socialism / Anarchism
- **Also called:** anti-authoritarian socialism; libertarian (its original
  nineteenth-century sense, still current in Europe)
- **Variants:** Guild socialism
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                        | Range                                                                                      | Central |
| -------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------- |
| Workplace governance             | Strongly _Worker voice and self-management_                                                | Yes     |
| Political authority              | Middle – Strongly _Voluntary order_                                                        | Yes     |
| Property in capital and goods    | Leans _Socially conditioned ownership_ – Strongly _Socially conditioned ownership_         | Yes     |
| Social hierarchy                 | Strongly _Equality of status_                                                              | Yes     |
| Representation vs. participation | Strongly _Direct and participatory self-rule_ – Leans _Direct and participatory self-rule_ | No      |
| Local vs. central                | Strongly _Local and regional self-rule_ – Leans _Local and regional self-rule_             | No      |
| Economic coordination            | Strongly _Public direction_ – Middle                                                       | No      |
| Leadership                       | Strongly _Dispersed, checked authority_                                                    | No      |
| Distribution                     | Leans _Egalitarian redistribution_ – Strongly _Egalitarian redistribution_                 | No      |
| Channels of change               | Middle – Strongly _Beyond institutions_                                                    | No      |
| Hierarchy or equality            | Strongly _Equality is basic_                                                               | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Socialism and freedom are inseparable. Socialism without liberty
becomes a new tyranny of party and bureaucracy, and liberty without socialism leaves
people under the command of bosses and landlords. Every authority has to justify itself,
and most cannot. The aim is a society of free association, in which workers and
communities run their own affairs directly through councils, federations, and
co-operatives, with no ruling state or party above them.

**Key concepts.**

- **Authority:** carries the burden of proof; hierarchy that cannot justify itself
  should be dismantled.
- **Self-management:** workers and communities make the decisions that govern them,
  rather than owners, managers, or officials.
- **Socialism:** social ownership through self-management, not nationalisation, which
  only swaps one boss for another.
- **Freedom:** both freedom from domination and the real power to shape one's life
  together with others.

**Signature reasons.**

- "Any authority has to justify itself, and most can't."
- "Nationalisation just swaps a private boss for a state one."
- "Liberty without socialism is privilege; socialism without liberty is slavery."
- "Workers can run their workplaces without managers or commissars."
- "Change starts with people organising themselves, not with capturing the state."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Anarcho-communism** and **Anarcho-syndicalism:** its anarchist children, shown
  instead of it when they fit (a moneyless economy of communes; revolutionary unions).
  Libertarian socialism is the fit for self-managing socialists outside them, including
  those who would still use a democratic state for now (_Political authority_,
  _Reciprocity in support_; Reasons).
- **Council communism:** shares self-management through councils, from within Marxism's
  theory of history and class (_Direction of history_; Reasons).
- **Democratic socialism:** uses the democratic state as its main instrument
  (_Political authority_).
- **Marxism–Leninism:** the vanguard party and party-state that libertarian socialism
  defines itself against (_Pluralism vs. unity_, _Leadership_).
- **Left-libertarianism (Steiner–Vallentyne):** a similar name for a different tradition,
  built on self-ownership and markets (_Property in capital and goods_, _Economic
  coordination_).

**Sources.** Mikhail Bakunin, _Federalism, Socialism, Anti-Theologism_ (1867); G. D. H.
Cole, _Guild Socialism Restated_ (1920); Cornelius Castoriadis, "On the Content of
Socialism" (1955–58); Daniel Guérin, _Anarchism: From Theory to Practice_ (1965; trans.
1970); Noam Chomsky, "Notes on Anarchism" (1970) and _Government in the Future_ (1970;
publ. 2005); Colin Ward, _Anarchy in Action_ (1973).

## African socialism (incl. Ujamaa)

- **Type:** Ideology
- **Parent:** Socialism
- **Also called:** Ujamaa ("familyhood", Julius Nyerere's Tanzanian form); Zambian
  Humanism (Kenneth Kaunda's form)
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                   | Range                                                                                            | Central |
| --------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------- |
| Land and natural resources  | Strongly _Common inheritance_ – Leans _Common inheritance_                                       | Yes     |
| Economic coordination       | Strongly _Public direction_ – Leans _Public direction_                                           | Yes     |
| Pluralism vs. unity         | Leans _Unified common purpose_ – Strongly _Unified common purpose_                               | Yes     |
| Distribution                | Leans _Egalitarian redistribution_ – Strongly _Egalitarian redistribution_                       | No      |
| Trade and economic openness | Leans _Economic protection and self-reliance_ – Strongly _Economic protection and self-reliance_ | No      |
| Leadership                  | Leans _Strong, decisive leadership_ – Middle                                                     | No      |
| Individuals or wholes       | Leans _Groups and relations are real_ – Strongly _Groups and relations are real_                 | No      |
| Work                        | Strongly _Work is a source of worth_ – Leans _Work is a source of worth_                         | No      |
| Tradition or reason         | Leans _Inherited practice carries wisdom_ – Middle                                               | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** Socialism is not something Africa has to import from Europe. It is
an attitude of mind already present in the traditional African family and village,
where land belonged to the community, everyone worked, and no one hoarded while others
went hungry. Newly independent nations should build on that inheritance (communal land,
village co-operation, and self-reliance) rather than on class war or capitalist
individualism, and a young nation still building itself needs unity more than
divisive party conflict.

**Key concepts.**

- **Ujamaa:** familyhood; society as an extended family whose members care for one
  another and share what they produce.
- **Land:** a common gift that belongs to the community or nation, used by those who work
  it, and not a commodity to be bought and sold.
- **Self-reliance:** development through the nation's own effort and its own people,
  not through foreign aid or foreign capital.
- **Class:** traditional African society had no classes, so socialism means restoring
  communal values, not class struggle.
- **Democracy:** consensus within one national movement, where competing parties would
  only reproduce ethnic and factional division.

**Signature reasons.**

- "We have no more need of being converted to socialism than we have of being taught
  democracy: both are rooted in our own past."
- "Land is a common gift. It should not be sold for profit."
- "Self-reliance, not aid, is the road to development."
- "A nation still being built needs unity, not factions."
- "In the traditional village everyone worked and no one exploited anyone."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Marxism–Leninism:** builds socialism through class struggle and a vanguard party;
  African socialism rejects class struggle as foreign to African society and roots
  socialism in communal tradition, often in religion too (_Tradition or reason_;
  Reasons).
- **Democratic socialism:** keeps competing parties (_Pluralism vs. unity_).
- **Pan-Africanism:** a Thin Label for continental unity and self-determination, often
  held alongside African socialism.
- **Ubuntu:** an ethic of personhood and consensus, not an economic programme (_Economic
  coordination_, _Land and natural resources_).
- **Gandhism:** shares village self-reliance, but relies on voluntary trusteeship of
  wealth rather than public ownership (_Economic coordination_, _Property in capital and
  goods_).

**Sources.** Julius K. Nyerere, "Ujamaa: The Basis of African Socialism" (1962) and
_Ujamaa: Essays on Socialism_ (1968); TANU, _The Arusha Declaration_ (1967); Léopold
Sédar Senghor, _On African Socialism_ (1964); Kwame Nkrumah, _Consciencism_ (1964);
Republic of Kenya, Sessional Paper No. 10, _African Socialism and Its Application to
Planning in Kenya_ (1965); Kenneth Kaunda, _A Humanist in Africa_ (1966); William H.
Friedland & Carl G. Rosberg (eds.), _African Socialism_ (1964).

## Bolivarianism (socialism of the 21st century)

- **Type:** Ideology
- **Parent:** Socialism
- **Also called:** socialism of the 21st century; chavismo (its Venezuelan movement);
  critics: "Castro-chavismo"
- **Variants:** —
- **Centered group:** —

### Positions

| Dimension                                         | Range                                                                                      | Central |
| ------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------- |
| Leadership                                        | Strongly _Strong, decisive leadership_ – Leans _Strong, decisive leadership_               | Yes     |
| Representation vs. participation                  | Strongly _Direct and participatory self-rule_ – Leans _Direct and participatory self-rule_ | Yes     |
| Economic coordination                             | Strongly _Public direction_ – Leans _Public direction_                                     | Yes     |
| Land and natural resources                        | Strongly _Common inheritance_ – Leans _Common inheritance_                                 | No      |
| Distribution                                      | Leans _Egalitarian redistribution_ – Strongly _Egalitarian redistribution_                 | No      |
| Welfare and social insurance                      | Leans _Public social insurance_ – Strongly _Public social insurance_                       | No      |
| Popular sovereignty vs. constitutional constraint | Leans _Popular and legislative supremacy_ – Strongly _Popular and legislative supremacy_   | No      |
| Popular judgment vs. expertise                    | Leans _Equal popular judgment_ – Strongly _Equal popular judgment_                         | No      |
| Pluralism vs. unity                               | Middle – Leans _Unified common purpose_                                                    | No      |
| Sovereignty vs. pooled governance                 | Middle – Strongly _Sovereign self-government_                                              | No      |
| Trade and economic openness                       | Middle – Leans _Economic protection and self-reliance_                                     | No      |
| Tempo of change                                   | Strongly _Fundamental transformation_ – Leans _Fundamental transformation_                 | No      |

### Reasons

**Self-description.** For two centuries Latin America's wealth served foreign powers and
a local oligarchy while the poor majority was shut out. Bolivarianism takes up Simón
Bolívar's unfinished revolution: national sovereignty over oil and land, Latin American
unity against imperialism, and a protagonistic democracy in which the people govern
directly through communal councils and referendums. It is led by a leader who answers
to the people rather than to the old parties and elites.

**Key concepts.**

- **Democracy:** participatory and "protagonistic": the people deciding directly, not
  only choosing representatives every few years.
- **Sovereignty:** national control of natural resources and freedom from US and
  financial domination.
- **The people:** the poor majority, set against an oligarchy that captured the old
  two-party system.
- **Leadership:** a leader who embodies and mobilises the popular will, joined in a
  civic-military alliance.
- **Socialism of the 21st century:** socialism built through elections, a new
  constitution, and communal power, not by copying the Soviet model.

**Signature reasons.**

- "The oil belongs to the people, not to foreign companies."
- "Democracy isn't voting every few years. It's the people governing in their communal
  councils."
- "Latin America must unite or stay someone's backyard."
- "Only the people can save the people."

**Telling it apart.**

- **Democratic socialism:** keeps power dispersed and checked, with institutions above
  any leader (_Leadership_, _Pluralism vs. unity_).
- **Marxism–Leninism:** relies on a vanguard party and Marxist theory; Bolivarianism came
  to power through elections and draws on Bolívar's nationalism and Christianity as much
  as Marx (_Channels of change_; Reasons).
- **Peronism:** also a leader-centred popular movement, but corporatist and built on the
  unions rather than socialist (_Property in capital and goods_, _Economic
  coordination_).
- **Left populism:** a Thin Label for "the people against the oligarchy" that can ride
  on Bolivarianism or on other hosts.

**Sources.** Constitution of the Bolivarian Republic of Venezuela (1999); Hugo Chávez,
address to the World Social Forum, Porto Alegre (2005); Heinz Dieterich, _El socialismo
del siglo XXI_; Marta Harnecker, _A World to Build: New Paths toward Twenty-First Century
Socialism_ (2015); George Ciccariello-Maher, _We Created Chávez_ (2013); Kirk A.
Hawkins, _Venezuela's Chavismo and Populism in Comparative Perspective_ (2010).

# Foundation Dimensions that bear on politics: a literature survey

> **Research ticket:** [Survey the literature for Foundation Dimensions that bear on politics](https://github.com/slovakian/polytic/issues/20)
> **Feeds:** "Lock the Dimension set: Foundation and Political" (this survey proposes candidates; that ticket decides).
> **Builds on:** `docs/framework.md`, `docs/glossary.md`, `docs/principles.md`, and the earlier survey
> [`docs/research/belief-system-models.md`](https://github.com/slovakian/polytic/blob/research/belief-system-models/docs/research/belief-system-models.md).
> **Date:** 2026-10-26

## TL;DR (plain English)

Polytic's framework says people have two layers of belief: **Political** ("what should society or the law
do?") and **Foundation** (the philosophy underneath: what they think is real, knowable, and good, including
how they think they themselves should live). A Foundation Dimension only earns a place if it helps explain
someone's politics. This survey asked: which deep philosophical questions actually do that?

The short answer:

- **Twenty-five deep questions survive as candidates; about a dozen have a strong track record of
  shaping politics.** The strongest evidence (large surveys, experiments, or both) is for: whether people have **free will**; whether success and
  poverty come mainly from **effort or circumstance**; whether **life is zero-sum**; **how wide the moral
  circle** should be (family and nation first, or everyone equally); whether **hierarchy is natural or
  equality basic**; whether some things are **sacred and not for trade**; **religious belief** and where
  **moral authority** comes from; **how far to trust experts**; and whether human traits are **given by
  nature or made by society**.
- **A second group has moderate evidence**: what human nature is like (fixed and flawed, or improvable),
  whether morality is objective, outcomes versus rules in ethics, how much nature itself matters morally,
  what grounds a being's moral status, whether history is progressing, what a good life looks like for
  oneself, and what work is for.
- **A third group is argued by philosophers but barely tested**: whether rights are natural or man-made,
  whether societies are "just individuals" or real wholes, whether values can all be ranked on one scale,
  how much to trust tradition over fresh reasoning, and what well-being is.
- **Many classic philosophy questions don't make the cut**: philosophy of mind, personal identity,
  mathematics, time, language, and (mostly) aesthetics. They are fascinating but show little or no
  connection to politics, so including them would turn Polytic into a general philosophy quiz.
- **Several popular psychology measures are not Foundation Dimensions**: need for closure, dogmatism,
  fear of a dangerous world, and mortality anxiety are traits or moods, not views a person holds and can
  defend. Measuring them would read as diagnosis, which breaks "map, don't grade."
- **Two surprises matter for the design.** First, many of these deep beliefs cut across left and right:
  zero-sum thinking predicts left-wing views on redistribution and right-wing views on immigration; people
  credit "nature" for traits on one issue and "society" on another depending on their side; both sides
  ascribe more free will to people they want to blame. This is good news: it means the Foundation layer
  adds real resolution rather than restating left/right, and it gives the grill natural coherence probes.
  Second, most evidence is correlational and US-heavy, and the links are tighter among politically
  engaged people. Foundation Beliefs help _explain_ and _test_ political Beliefs; they should not be
  presented as their _cause_.

A suggested shortlist and the open questions for the locking ticket are in [Synthesis](#synthesis).

## Method and sources

**Scope.** We surveyed metaethics, normative ethics, epistemology (including social epistemology and
the ethics of belief), metaphysics of agency, philosophical anthropology (human nature), philosophy of
religion, theories of well-being and the good life, social ontology, philosophy of history, environmental
ethics, theories of desert, and the moral circle/partiality debate. For each, we asked whether there is
(a) a philosophical lineage with a clear two-sided trade-off, and (b) evidence or serious argument that
the question shapes political positions.

**Sources, in order of trust.**

1. **Primary philosophical works** for lineage (authors, works, dates).
2. **The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy (SEP)** as the reference map of each debate. Every SEP entry
   cited below was confirmed to exist at the given URL during this survey.
3. **Peer-reviewed empirical work** in political psychology, social psychology, sociology, and
   economics. Bibliographic details (authors, journal, volume, pages, DOI) and abstract-level findings
   were checked against Crossref records, publisher pages, or institutional repositories.
4. **The PhilPapers 2020 Survey** (Bourget & Chalmers 2023), which asked about 2,000 professional
   philosophers 100 questions, including political ones ("Politics: capitalism or socialism?",
   "Political philosophy: communitarianism, egalitarianism, or libertarianism?", "Capital punishment:
   permissible or impermissible?"). The question list was confirmed on Chalmers's survey discussion page.
   The survey site and paper were behind bot protection during this research, so the correlation figures
   quoted below come from search-engine extractions of the paper and the survey's correlation pages. They
   are **flagged as unverified**: treat them as indicative only.

**Evidence ratings.** Each candidate gets one rating for its bearing on politics:

| Rating          | Meaning                                                                                                                                                  |
| --------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Strong**      | Replicated empirical associations with specific political positions, ideally in large or representative samples, ideally with some experimental support. |
| **Moderate**    | Some empirical support (single studies, student samples, or indirect links via a close proxy), plus a strong philosophical argument.                     |
| **Argued-only** | A well-developed philosophical argument that the question bears on politics, but little or no empirical testing.                                         |

**Two caveats apply throughout.**

- **Correlation, not cause.** Nearly all the evidence is correlational. Where experiments exist, they
  sometimes fail to show downstream effects: a meta-analysis of 145 experiments found that manipulating
  free-will belief does lower it, but found no evidence of downstream effects on behavior (Genschow et
  al. 2023). Polytic should treat Foundation Beliefs as _explanations a person gives and can test_, not as
  hidden causes it has detected.
- **Engagement moderates everything.** Links between deep beliefs and political positions are tighter
  among politically engaged people. Religiosity, for example, predicts conservatism strongly among the
  engaged and weakly or not at all among the disengaged (Malka et al. 2012), echoing Converse (1964) and
  the earlier survey. Polytic will often meet people whose Foundation and Political layers are loosely
  connected; that is a finding, not a failure.

## How each candidate is written up

Each candidate below has the same parts:

- **Poles**: the two ends, named so neither sounds like the "smart" one.
- **Definition**: the trade-off in one or two sentences, with what each pole protects.
- **Lineage**: where the debate comes from in philosophy.
- **Political bearing**: which political positions it shapes, the evidence, and a rating.
- **Candidate Foundation Labels**: established named positions that could become Labels.
- **Notes**: overlaps, in-principle/in-practice Bridges, and cautions.

The numbering (1 to 25) is for reference only and does not rank the candidates.

## Family A: agency, responsibility, and fortune

These three form a chain the grill can walk: _Do people freely choose?_ (metaphysics) → _Do they deserve
what follows?_ (ethics) → _What actually drives who succeeds?_ (social theory). People often hold
positions at each link that pull against each other, which makes this the richest source of coherence
probes in the whole Foundation layer.

### 1. Free will and moral responsibility

**Poles:** _Self-authored agency_ ↔ _Caused behavior_

**Definition.** Are people's choices ultimately up to them in a way that makes them truly responsible
(and so properly praised and blamed), or are choices the product of causes (genes, upbringing, brain,
circumstance) that the person did not choose? The first pole protects responsibility, dignity, and the
meaning of blame and praise; the second protects compassion, and resistance to blaming people for what
they could not help.

**Lineage.** Ancient roots in Epicurus and the Stoics; Augustine, _On Free Choice of the Will_ (c. 388–395).
Compatibilism in Hobbes, _Leviathan_ (1651) and Hume, _An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding_ (1748),
§8. Kant, _Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals_ (1785): we must act "under the idea of freedom" (the
classic in-principle/in-practice Bridge). Hard determinism in d'Holbach, _System of Nature_ (1770).
Modern debate: P. F. Strawson, "Freedom and Resentment" (1962); Frankfurt, "Alternate Possibilities and
Moral Responsibility" (1969); van Inwagen, _An Essay on Free Will_ (1983); Dennett, _Elbow Room_ (1984);
Fischer & Ravizza, _Responsibility and Control_ (1998); Smilansky, _Free Will and Illusion_ (2000);
Pereboom, _Living Without Free Will_ (2001); Caruso, _Rejecting Retributivism_ (2021); Dennett & Caruso,
_Just Deserts_ (2021). SEP: "Free Will", "Compatibilism", "Incompatibilist (Nondeterministic) Theories of
Free Will", "Skepticism About Moral Responsibility".

**Political bearing: strong (correlational); weak (causal).**

- **Punishment.** Weaker free-will belief predicts less _retributive_ (but not less consequentialist)
  punishment; learning about the neural basis of behavior, in the lab or in a neuroscience course,
  reduced support for retribution (Shariff et al. 2014, four studies).
- **Conservatism.** Free-will belief is associated with authoritarianism, religiosity (specifically
  intrinsic religiosity), belief in a just world, the "binding" moral foundations, and retributive
  punishment; the authors see "a strong sense of personal responsibility" as the common element (Carey &
  Paulhus 2013, three studies, using the FAD-Plus scale). They also found that _scientific_ determinism
  and _fatalistic_ determinism have different correlates and must be measured separately.
- **Large-scale replication with a twist.** Fourteen studies, including a meta-analysis of five with
  n = 308,499, found conservatives report more free-will belief, largely because they moralize more
  behaviors; and **both liberals and conservatives attribute more free will to actors they are motivated to
  blame**, including political opponents (Everett et al. 2021).
- **Causal caution.** A meta-analysis of 145 experiments found free-will manipulations work on the belief
  but found no evidence of downstream consequences (Genschow et al. 2023).
- **Philosophers.** In the PhilPapers 2020 survey, libertarian free will is reported to correlate with
  theism (r ≈ 0.45) and with moral realism (r ≈ 0.27). _Unverified; see Method._
- **Measurement exists:** the Free Will Inventory measures free will, determinism, and dualism beliefs
  separately (Nadelhoffer et al. 2014).

Positions shaped: criminal justice (retribution vs. rehabilitation, death penalty), welfare and market
outcomes (via desert), addiction policy, blame in public life.

**Candidate Foundation Labels:** libertarian free will (agent causation); compatibilism; semi-compatibilism;
hard determinism; hard incompatibilism / free will skepticism; illusionism (Smilansky); revisionism;
Strawsonian (reactive-attitudes) view; Kantian practical standpoint.

**Notes.** The textbook Bridge case: "determinism is true, but we must act as if we choose" (Kant;
Smilansky's illusionism). Everett et al.'s finding gives the grill a ready, symmetric coherence probe:
"You credit free will for X's choices; does the same hold for Y, whom you sympathize with?" Distinguish
scientific from fatalistic determinism (Carey & Paulhus 2013).

### 2. Desert

**Poles:** _Earned_ ↔ _Unearned_

**Definition.** Do people morally deserve the rewards and burdens that flow from their talents, effort,
and choices, so that taking them away needs special justification? Or are talents and even the capacity
for effort largely gifts of luck, so that nobody deserves their place in the distribution "all the way
down"? The first pole protects the moral meaning of effort and achievement; the second protects against
treating good fortune as virtue and bad fortune as vice.

**Lineage.** Aristotle, _Nicomachean Ethics_ V (justice as proportion to merit). Feinberg, "Justice and
Personal Desert" in _Doing and Deserving_ (1970). Rawls, _A Theory of Justice_ (1971), §§17 and 48: the
"natural lottery" and the claim that we do not deserve our natural endowments, replaced by legitimate
expectations. Nozick, _Anarchy, State, and Utopia_ (1974): entitlement without desert. Michael Young, _The
Rise of the Meritocracy_ (1958); Sher, _Desert_ (1987); Miller, _Principles of Social Justice_ (1999);
luck egalitarianism (Dworkin 1981; Cohen 1989; Arneson 1989); Sandel, _The Tyranny of Merit_ (2020).
Desert skepticism follows from free will skepticism (Pereboom 2001; Caruso 2021). SEP: "Desert",
"Distributive Justice", "Moral Luck".

**Political bearing: moderate to strong.**

- **Fairness ideals are plural and measurable.** In incentivized experiments, people split among
  strict-egalitarian, liberal-egalitarian (reward effort, not luck), and libertarian (reward all
  production) fairness ideals (Cappelen et al. 2007).
- **Cross-national redistribution.** Americans and Scandinavians were compared in a large international
  experiment; differences in how much people hold others responsible for luck-driven inequality help
  explain differences in redistribution (Almås, Cappelen & Tungodden 2020).
- **Beliefs about fairness of the market** shape the welfare state (Alesina & Angeletos 2005, a formal
  model in which beliefs that effort determines income sustain low redistribution).
- **Meritocratic belief** rises with inequality across countries (Mijs 2021, 25 years of ISSP data).

Positions shaped: taxation and redistribution, inheritance tax, affirmative action, welfare
conditionality, executive pay.

**Candidate Foundation Labels:** desert-based justice; meritocracy; luck egalitarianism; Rawlsian
anti-desert (legitimate expectations); Nozickian entitlement theory; desert skepticism.

**Notes.** Philosophically separate from free will (a compatibilist can deny desert for talents, as Rawls
does) and from causes of fortune (#3: someone can think effort drives success yet deny that effort is
deserved). Keep the chain explicit so the grill can probe it.

### 3. Causes of fortune: agency versus circumstance

**Poles:** _Effort and choice_ ↔ _Luck and structure_

**Definition.** When people succeed or fail (become rich, stay poor, get sick, commit crimes), what mostly
explains it: their own effort and choices, or circumstances they did not control (birth, luck,
discrimination, economic structure)? This is a belief about how the social world works, not about desert.
The first pole protects agency and the dignity of self-help; the second protects attention to forces no
individual can overcome alone.

**Lineage.** Weber, _The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism_ (1905); the theodicy tradition
(Leibniz, _Theodicy_, 1710) and its secular cousin, the "just world" (Lerner, _The Belief in a Just World_,
1980). Sociology's structure/agency debate (Giddens, _The Constitution of Society_, 1984). Feagin's
triad of poverty explanations: individualistic, structural, fatalistic (Feagin 1975).

**Political bearing: strong.** One of the best-replicated findings in public opinion.

- Kluegel & Smith, _Beliefs About Inequality_ (1986): individualist explanations of poverty and wealth
  underpin opposition to redistribution in the US.
- Conservatism correlates with individualistic explanations of poverty; controllability attributions lead
  to blame and anger rather than pity, and lower support for welfare (Zucker & Weiner 1993). A later review
  frames help for the poor as driven by perceived responsibility of the recipient, a view the authors
  describe as dominant among conservatives (anger, neglect) versus liberals (sympathy, help) (Weiner,
  Osborne & Rudolph 2011).
- In economics: beliefs that effort rather than luck determines income predict opposition to
  redistribution (Fong 2001; Alesina & Angeletos 2005; Piketty 1995 on social-mobility beliefs).
- **Just-world belief** is associated with admiring the fortunate and derogating victims (Rubin & Peplau 1975) and with conservatism and free-will belief (Carey & Paulhus 2013).

Positions shaped: welfare, minimum wage, criminal justice, health policy, reparations, affirmative action.

**Candidate Foundation Labels:** individualist / structural / fatalist attribution (Feagin); belief in a
just world; meritocratic belief; structuralism.

**Notes.** Unlike most Foundation Dimensions this is partly empirical: the facts can be studied. Polytic's
"objective realism" principle allows it to note what evidence a person relies on, but the Dimension
records _their_ view, never a correct answer. Strong overlap with #2 (desert) and #25 (work); see
[Overlaps](#overlaps-and-possible-merges).

## Family B: human nature

### 4. Human nature: constrained or improvable

**Poles:** _Fixed and flawed_ ↔ _Malleable and improvable_

**Definition.** Is human nature essentially fixed, with permanent limits of selfishness, knowledge, and
virtue that institutions must work around? Or is it largely shaped by circumstances, so better
institutions, education, or culture can make people substantially better? Sowell's terms: the
_constrained_ versus the _unconstrained_ vision. The first pole protects against utopian projects that
override real people; the second protects against resignation to avoidable wrongs.

**Lineage.** Augustine, _City of God_ (426) on original sin; Hobbes, _Leviathan_ (1651); Madison,
_Federalist_ No. 51 (1788: "If men were angels, no government would be necessary"); Smith, _The Theory
of Moral Sentiments_ (1759); Burke, _Reflections on the Revolution in France_ (1790). Against: Rousseau,
_Discourse on Inequality_ (1755); Godwin, _Political Justice_ (1793); Condorcet, _Sketch for a Historical
Picture of the Progress of the Human Mind_ (1795); Owen; Marx's "species-being" (1844). Syntheses:
Passmore, _The Perfectibility of Man_ (1970); Sowell, _A Conflict of Visions_ (1987); Pinker, _The Blank
Slate_ (2002), who restates it as "Tragic" versus "Utopian" visions. SEP: "Human Nature".

**Political bearing: moderate.**

- **Lay theories of malleability** (Dweck's entity versus incremental theories; Dweck, Chiu & Hong 1995)
  bear on morality and politics: people who see the social-moral order as fixed favor duty-based morality;
  those who see it as malleable favor rights-based morality (Chiu et al. 1997). Believing everyone can
  become highly intelligent predicts support for policies that distribute resources more equally (Rattan
  et al. 2012).
- **Experimental evidence from conflict.** Inducing the belief that groups can change improved attitudes
  toward the adversary and increased willingness to compromise, among Israeli Jews and Palestinians
  (Halperin et al. 2011, _Science_).
- **Belief in pure evil** (that some people are irredeemably evil) predicts harsher, more retributive
  responses to wrongdoing; belief in pure good predicts more empathy and less support for torture (Webster
  & Saucier 2013).
- **Sowell's framework itself** is argued, not tested: we found no validated survey measure of
  "constrained versus unconstrained vision."

Positions shaped: criminal justice (rehabilitation), education policy, revolution versus reform,
constitutional design (checks and balances), foreign policy, utopian programs.

**Candidate Foundation Labels:** constrained / unconstrained vision (Sowell); tragic / utopian vision
(Pinker); Augustinian; Hobbesian; Rousseauian (natural goodness); perfectibilism; entity / incremental
theorist (Dweck).

**Notes.** Overlaps with #5 (the _source_ of traits) and #20 (Sowell's constrained vision is as much about
limits of _knowledge_ as of virtue). Also pairs with #22 (perfectibility and progress travel together in
Condorcet).

### 5. Given or made: nature, nurture, and social construction

**Poles:** _Given by nature_ ↔ _Made by society_

**Definition.** Are important human differences (between sexes, between individuals' abilities, between
groups) mainly given by biology, or mainly produced by upbringing, culture, and social arrangements? And
are categories like gender or race natural kinds or social constructions? The first pole protects respect
for real differences and limits on social engineering; the second protects against treating contingent
arrangements as inevitable.

**Lineage.** Plato's myth of the metals (_Republic_, c. 375 BCE); Locke's _tabula rasa_ (_Essay Concerning
Human Understanding_, 1689); Mill, _The Subjection of Women_ (1869: "what is now called the nature of
women is an eminently artificial thing"); Darwin, _The Descent of Man_ (1871); Galton; Beauvoir, _The Second
Sex_ (1949); Berger & Luckmann, _The Social Construction of Reality_ (1966); Hacking, _The Social
Construction of What?_ (1999); Pinker, _The Blank Slate_ (2002). The PhilPapers 2020 survey asks
"Race: biological, social, or unreal?" and "Gender: biological, psychological, social, or unreal?"

**Political bearing: strong, but domain-specific.**

- Conservatives scored higher on genetic explanations of race and class differences; liberals scored
  higher on genetic explanations of sexual orientation; for individual traits like intelligence the gap
  disappeared (Suhay & Jayaratne 2013, _Public Opinion Quarterly_). The authors argue people adopt causal
  explanations selectively to fit political views.
- Belief in genetic determinism (the biological component of essentialism) has been linked to motivated
  social cognition, including prejudice and system justification (Keller 2005; findings not re-checked
  beyond the bibliographic record).
- Essentialist beliefs about social categories are a measurable construct (Haslam, Rothschild & Ernst
  2000).
- Among philosophers, "Politics: capitalism" is reported to correlate with "Race: biological" at
  r ≈ 0.37 (n = 808), one of the strongest politics correlations in the survey. _Unverified; see Method._

Positions shaped: gender policy, transgender policy, affirmative action, education, sexual-orientation
rights, immigration and integration, explanations of group inequality.

**Candidate Foundation Labels:** biological essentialism / nativism; social constructionism;
interactionism; blank slate (Lockean); evolutionary psychology; naturalism about kinds.

**Notes.** The flip by domain is a feature: the grill can ask a person who credits nature on one issue and
society on another what distinguishes them, without implying either answer is wrong. Handle with care;
this is the Dimension most at risk of sliding into contested empirical claims. Polytic records the
person's view and reasons, not a verdict on the science. Overlaps with #4 (malleability).

## Family C: the foundations of morality

### 6. Moral objectivity: realism and anti-realism

**Poles:** _Objective moral truth_ ↔ _Human-made morality_

**Definition.** Are some moral claims true regardless of what anyone believes or feels (as "2 + 2 = 4" is),
or is morality ultimately a product of human attitudes, agreements, or cultures? The first pole protects
the possibility of moral knowledge and of saying a whole society is wrong; the second protects humility,
tolerance of difference, and suspicion of moral dogma.

**Lineage.** Plato's Form of the Good (_Republic_); Hume, _A Treatise of Human Nature_ (1739–40, Book III,
the is/ought gap); Moore, _Principia Ethica_ (1903); Ayer, _Language, Truth and Logic_ (1936; emotivism);
Mackie, _Ethics: Inventing Right and Wrong_ (1977; error theory); Harman, "Moral Relativism Defended"
(1975); Rawls, "Kantian Constructivism in Moral Theory" (1980); Korsgaard, _The Sources of Normativity_
(1996); Blackburn, _Ruling Passions_ (1998; quasi-realism); Wong, _Natural Moralities_ (2006); Parfit, _On
What Matters_ (2011); Enoch, _Taking Morality Seriously_ (2011). Dewey's moral pragmatism, _Human Nature
and Conduct_ (1922). SEP: "Moral Realism", "Moral Anti-Realism", "Moral Relativism", "Moral Cognitivism vs.
Non-Cognitivism".

**Political bearing: moderate, mostly indirect.**

- **Tolerance.** More objectivist intuitions about an issue go with lower tolerance of people who disagree,
  a link a review describes as stably replicated (Pölzler & Wright 2019 review, citing Goodwin & Darley
  2008, 2012; Wright, Grandjean & McWhite 2013; Wright, McWhite & Grandjean 2014).
- **Religion.** Moral subjectivism correlates negatively with religious belief; religious priming raises
  objectivism, and arguments for subjectivism lower confidence in God (Yilmaz & Bahçekapili 2015).
- **Compromise.** Holding an attitude as a moral conviction (closely related, though not identical to
  objectivism) makes people oppose compromise and punish compromising politicians, on economic and
  non-economic issues alike (Skitka, Bauman & Sargis 2005; Ryan 2017).
- **Pragmatism.** Framing moral values as solutions to specific problems, rather than universal truths,
  increased tolerance of dissenters without increasing immoral behavior; relativism priming did increase
  it (Zhang, Tong & Peng 2024; Chinese online sample, no political measure).
- **No direct left/right link was found** in the folk-objectivism review (Pölzler & Wright 2019). Both
  sides hold some views as objective; the political effect runs through _which_ issues and _how_ the
  person handles disagreement.
- Among philosophers, moral realism is reported to correlate with theism (r ≈ 0.3). _Unverified._

Positions shaped: tolerance and free speech, compromise, universal human rights versus cultural
relativism, interventionism.

**Candidate Foundation Labels:** moral realism (naturalist, non-naturalist); constructivism (Kantian,
Rawlsian); expressivism / quasi-realism; error theory; moral relativism; moral pragmatism.

**Notes.** "Moral pragmatism" is a natural in-principle/in-practice Bridge ("there may be no moral facts,
but we must reason as if some answers are better"). Strong overlap with #7 and #21; the PhilPapers
cluster (realism, theism, libertarian free will) suggests a "temperament" that the grill can test for
coherence without assuming it.

### 7. Source of moral authority: given or human-made

**Poles:** _Given from beyond us_ ↔ _Worked out by us_

**Definition.** Where does moral authority ultimately come from: something given that we must discover
and submit to (God, nature's order, sacred tradition), or something humans construct and revise through
reason, experience, agreement, or sentiment? The first pole protects moral stability and limits on human
pride; the second protects moral learning and the ability to revise inherited wrongs.

**Lineage.** Divine command (Ockham; Adams, _Finite and Infinite Goods_, 1999); natural law (Aquinas,
_Summa Theologiae_ I–II q. 94, c. 1270; Finnis, _Natural Law and Natural Rights_, 1980); Kant's autonomy
(_Groundwork_, 1785); sentimentalism (Hume 1739–40; Smith 1759); contractarianism (Hobbes 1651; Gauthier,
_Morals by Agreement_, 1986); contractualism (Scanlon, _What We Owe to Each Other_, 1998); evolutionary
naturalism (Darwin 1871; Joyce, _The Evolution of Morality_, 2006). Plato's _Euthyphro_ frames the core
dilemma. SEP: "Theological Voluntarism", "The Natural Law Tradition in Ethics", "Contractualism",
"Kant's Moral Philosophy", "Hume's Moral Philosophy".

**Political bearing: strong, but entangled with religiosity.**

- Hunter, _Culture Wars_ (1991), argues the defining US cultural divide is between _orthodoxy_ (commitment
  to "an external, definable, and transcendent authority") and _progressivism_ (moral authority shaped by
  contemporary experience and reason). Jensen (1998) found this orthodox/progressive divide within both
  India and the United States, using Shweder's ethics.
- Caution: DiMaggio, Evans & Bryson (1996) found less mass attitude polarization than the culture-war
  thesis implies; the divide is clearer among engaged and religious activists.

Positions shaped: abortion, sexuality and marriage, bioethics, education, the role of religion in law.

**Candidate Foundation Labels:** divine command theory; natural law; Kantian autonomy; sentimentalism;
contractarianism; contractualism; evolutionary naturalism; orthodox / progressive (Hunter).

**Notes.** Overlaps heavily with #6 (objectivity: a realist can be secular) and #21 (transcendence: a
theist can be a natural-law rationalist). It is the "bridge" concept Hunter used, which may make it the
most useful of the three for describing people; the locking ticket should decide whether to keep all
three or merge.

### 8. Outcomes or constraints

**Poles:** _Best outcomes_ ↔ _Inviolable rules_

**Definition.** Is the right act the one that produces the best overall results, or are there things you
must never do (or must always do) even when breaking the rule would produce better results? The first pole
protects concern for real consequences and against squeamish rule-worship; the second protects persons
from being sacrificed for the greater good.

**Lineage.** Bentham, _Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation_ (1789); Mill,
_Utilitarianism_ (1861); Sidgwick, _The Methods of Ethics_ (1874). Kant, _Groundwork_ (1785); Ross, _The
Right and the Good_ (1930; prima facie duties); Nozick (1974; rights as side-constraints). Virtue ethics as
a third tradition: Aristotle, _Nicomachean Ethics_; Anscombe, "Modern Moral Philosophy" (1958); Hursthouse,
_On Virtue Ethics_ (1999). SEP: "Consequentialism", "Deontological Ethics", "Virtue Ethics".

**Political bearing: moderate.**

- Religiosity and conservatism are each associated with resisting outcome-based reasoning about
  transgressions, independent of disgust sensitivity (Piazza & Sousa 2014, three studies).
- But use of principles is motivated: political liberals were more willing to sacrifice "Chip" than
  "Tyrone" in a trolley dilemma, i.e. their consequentialism shifted with the victim's race (Uhlmann et al.
  2009). Utilitarian psychology has two separable parts, impartial beneficence and acceptance of
  instrumental harm (Kahane et al. 2018), which may relate to politics differently (not tested here).

Positions shaped: torture and security, criminal justice, health rationing, cost-benefit regulation,
bioethics, war.

**Candidate Foundation Labels:** act / rule consequentialism (utilitarianism); deontology (Kantian,
Rossian pluralism); rights as side-constraints; threshold deontology; virtue ethics.

**Notes.** Virtue ethics does not sit naturally on this axis; it is better captured in #24 (personal
ethic). Uhlmann et al. give the grill a symmetric coherence probe.

### 9. Value structure: one master value or many

**Poles:** _Unified values_ ↔ _Plural, conflicting values_

**Definition.** Do all genuine values ultimately fit into one coherent scheme (one master value, or a
single correct ranking), or are there many genuine values that can conflict tragically with no common
measure? The first pole protects the hope of a principled answer to every dilemma; the second protects
against sacrificing real goods to a single ideal.

**Lineage.** Plato; Bentham's monism of pleasure; Dworkin, _Justice for Hedgehogs_ (2011; the unity of
value). Against: Berlin, "Two Concepts of Liberty" (1958) and _Four Essays on Liberty_ (1969); Williams;
Hampshire; Raz, _The Morality of Freedom_ (1986; incommensurability); Galston, _Liberal Pluralism_ (2002).
SEP: "Value Pluralism".

**Political bearing: argued, with some empirical support.**

- Berlin argued that monism underwrites utopian and totalitarian politics and pluralism underwrites
  liberal toleration.
- Tetlock's _value pluralism model_ (1986) predicts more integratively complex reasoning when a person's
  ideology commits them to conflicting values; early work found moderate socialists more complex than
  moderate conservatives in the British Commons. Later work found complexity is domain-specific: each side
  is simpler on its own core issues (Conway et al. 2016).

**Candidate Foundation Labels:** value pluralism (Berlin); value monism; utilitarian monism; Rossian
pluralism; unity of value (Dworkin).

**Notes.** Framework already records value conflict as Pull on both Poles of each Dimension, so this may
be redundant as a Dimension. It could survive as a _meta_-Belief ("I think my values genuinely conflict"),
or merge into #10 (incommensurability is the philosophical core of sacred values). Recommend merge or drop.

### 10. Sacred or tradeable

**Poles:** _Some things are beyond price_ ↔ _Everything can be weighed_

**Definition.** Are some things (life, land, faith, honor, the body, the nation) sacred, so that putting
them up for trade is itself a wrong; or can every value in principle be weighed against others, including
money? The first pole protects things that lose their meaning once priced; the second protects honest
trade-offs and against taboos that cost lives or freedom.

**Lineage.** Durkheim, _The Elementary Forms of Religious Life_ (1912); Eliade, _The Sacred and the
Profane_ (1957); Kant's distinction between price and dignity (_Groundwork_, 1785); Radin, _Contested
Commodities_ (1996); Sandel, _What Money Can't Buy_ (2012). The opposite pole: Becker, _The Economic
Approach to Human Behavior_ (1976).

**Political bearing: strong.**

- People treat some values as protected or sacred and react with moral outrage to "taboo trade-offs"
  (Tetlock et al. 2000; Tetlock 2003; Baron & Spranca 1997 on protected values).
- In experiments with Israelis and Palestinians, offering material incentives to compromise over sacred
  issues _increased_ violent opposition, while symbolic concessions on the adversary's own sacred values
  decreased it (Ginges et al. 2007, _PNAS_).
- Moralized attitudes lead citizens to oppose compromise and to forgo material gains to avoid it (Ryan
  2017).

Positions shaped: organ and surrogacy markets, sex work, land and territory, flag and symbol laws,
religious freedom, cost-benefit valuation of life, peace negotiations.

**Candidate Foundation Labels:** sacred values / protected values; anti-commodification (Sandel, Radin);
market universalism; Kantian dignity.

**Notes.** Sanctity of life (#13) is the paradigm sacred value; incommensurability (#9) is its
philosophical core. A natural home for merging both.

## Family D: who counts

### 11. The moral circle: partial or impartial

**Poles:** _Near and dear first_ ↔ _Everyone counts equally_

**Definition.** Do we owe more to those close to us (family, community, nation, our own kind), or should
everyone's interests (strangers, foreigners, perhaps animals and future generations) count equally? The
first pole protects loyalty, special relationships, and the communities that make obligation real; the
second protects the equal worth of every life wherever it is.

**Lineage.** Mozi's impartial care against Confucian graded love (5th–4th c. BCE); Godwin (1793; the
Archbishop Fénelon case); Lecky, _History of European Morals_ (1869; the "expanding circle"); Singer,
"Famine, Affluence, and Morality" (1972) and _The Expanding Circle_ (1981); Williams, "Persons, Character
and Morality" (1981; "one thought too many"); MacIntyre, "Is Patriotism a Virtue?" (1984); Nussbaum,
"Patriotism and Cosmopolitanism" (1994); Scheffler, _Boundaries and Allegiances_ (2001); Parfit, _Reasons
and Persons_ (1984), Part IV (future generations). SEP: "Impartiality", "Cosmopolitanism", "Nationalism".

**Political bearing: strong.**

- Liberals express moral concern for wider, less structured circles (universalism); conservatives for
  tighter, better-defined ones (parochialism), across seven studies (Waytz et al. 2019, _Nature
  Communications_).
- **Moral universalism** (treating strangers as you treat in-group members) is measurable and predicts
  voting: universalists vote left, communal moralists vote right, in US presidential elections (Enke 2020,
  _Journal of Political Economy_; measure validated in Enke, Rodríguez-Padilla & Zimmermann 2022).
- A validated moral-expansiveness scale exists (Crimston et al. 2016).

Positions shaped: immigration, foreign aid, trade, nationalism versus cosmopolitanism, animal welfare,
climate policy (future generations), welfare chauvinism.

**Candidate Foundation Labels:** impartialism; utilitarian cosmopolitanism; effective altruism;
cosmopolitanism (Nussbaum); moral partialism / special obligations (Scheffler, Williams); communitarian
particularism; Confucian graded love; Mohist impartial care; longtermism; sentientism.

**Notes.** This is the Foundation layer under the Political nationalism/cosmopolitanism Dimension; keep
them distinct ("I owe more to my compatriots" versus "borders should be open"). Ethical egoism is the
extreme partial pole (the self as the nearest circle); see #24 for the personal-ethic version.

### 12. The moral standing of nature

**Poles:** _Human-centered_ ↔ _Nature-centered_

**Definition.** Does nature matter morally only because of what it means for humans (now and in future),
or do animals, species, and ecosystems have value of their own? The first pole protects human needs,
development, and the special status of persons; the second protects what cannot speak for itself against
being used up.

**Lineage.** Genesis 1:28 ("dominion"); Aristotle, _Politics_ I.8; Descartes; Kant's indirect duties.
Against: Leopold, _A Sand County Almanac_ (1949; the land ethic); White, "The Historical Roots of Our
Ecologic Crisis" (1967); Routley, "Is There a Need for a New, an Environmental, Ethic?" (1973; the "last
man" argument); Naess, "The Shallow and the Deep, Long-Range Ecology Movement" (1973); Singer, _Animal
Liberation_ (1975); Norton, "Environmental Ethics and Weak Anthropocentrism" (1984); Taylor, _Respect for
Nature_ (1986); Rolston, _Environmental Ethics_ (1988). Sociology: Catton & Dunlap's "human exemptionalism"
versus the "new ecological paradigm" (1978). SEP: "Environmental Ethics", "The Moral Status of Animals".

**Political bearing: moderate.**

- Ecocentric and anthropocentric attitudes are separable and predict different environmental attitudes
  and behavior (Gagnon Thompson & Barton 1994).
- Social dominance orientation predicts willingness to exploit the environment, via a belief in human
  dominance over nature (Milfont et al. 2013).
- The New Ecological Paradigm scale (Dunlap et al. 2000) is the standard measure and shows partisan
  differences in many samples; direct tests of _anthropocentrism_ against left/right are thinner than we
  expected.
- Note that a green position can be argued on human-centered grounds, so anthropocentrism does not imply
  anti-environmentalism (Norton 1984).

Positions shaped: climate and conservation policy, land use, animal agriculture, hunting, development
versus preservation.

**Candidate Foundation Labels:** strong / weak anthropocentrism; stewardship; sentientism; biocentrism;
ecocentrism (land ethic); deep ecology.

**Notes.** Mary Douglas's cultural theory of risk ("myths of nature": benign, fragile, tolerant,
capricious) is a related worldview with strong evidence on risk perception (Kahan et al. 2007), but its
axes (hierarchy, individualism) map onto #14 and #16 rather than onto nature's moral standing.

### 13. Grounds of moral status: sanctity of life or quality of life

**Poles:** _Life itself is inviolable_ ↔ _What matters is the life's quality and capacities_

**Definition.** What gives a being full moral status and protection: being a living human (or having a
soul, or the potential to become a person), or having certain capacities (consciousness, the ability to
suffer, self-awareness, interests)? Relatedly, is human life inviolable at every stage, or does its value
depend on its quality? The first pole protects the weakest and least capable from being judged unworthy;
the second protects against prolonging suffering and lets status track what a being can experience.

**Lineage.** Hippocratic tradition; Catholic natural law and the doctrine of the sanctity of life; Kant on
dignity. Thomson, "A Defense of Abortion" (1971); Tooley, "Abortion and Infanticide" (1972); Warren, "On
the Moral and Legal Status of Abortion" (1973); Kuhse, _The Sanctity-of-Life Doctrine in Medicine_ (1987);
Marquis, "Why Abortion Is Immoral" (1989); Singer, _Rethinking Life and Death_ (1994). SEP: "Grounds of
Moral Status".

**Political bearing: moderate (strong face validity; we found little direct psychometric work).** These
are the explicit premises of the abortion, euthanasia, embryo-research, and disability debates, and of
part of the animal-welfare debate. Empirically, attitudes on these issues are strongly tied to religiosity
and to beliefs about when life or personhood begins, but we did not find and verify a study that measures
"grounds of moral status" as a standalone belief and links it to politics. Flagged for the locking ticket.

**Candidate Foundation Labels:** sanctity of life; quality of life; personhood (capacity) views;
potentiality / "future like ours" (Marquis); species-membership (human dignity) views; gradualism.

**Notes.** Could merge into #10 (sanctity as a sacred value) plus #11 (who is in the circle). Kept separate
here because it is the direct philosophical premise of several high-salience Political Beliefs.

## Family E: the shape of the social world

### 14. Natural hierarchy or basic equality

**Poles:** _Hierarchy is natural_ ↔ _Equality is basic_

**Definition.** Are people naturally ranked (by ability, virtue, role, or strength), so that well-ordered
hierarchy is part of the good; or are all persons fundamentally equal in moral worth, so that every
hierarchy needs justification? The first pole protects excellence, order, and differentiated roles; the
second protects against domination and arbitrary rank.

**Lineage.** Plato, _Republic_ (the metals); Aristotle, _Politics_ I (natural rule); the medieval "great
chain of being"; Nietzsche, _On the Genealogy of Morality_ (1887). Against: Locke, _Second Treatise_
(1689), §4 ("a state also of equality"); Rousseau (1755); Kant; Waldron, _One Another's Equals_ (2017).
SEP: "Equality", "Egalitarianism".

**Political bearing: strong.**

- Social dominance orientation (a preference for group-based hierarchy) predicts a wide range of social
  and political attitudes (Pratto et al. 1994); it splits into dominance and anti-egalitarianism facets
  (Ho et al. 2015).
- The "hierarchical world" belief (that the world is naturally ranked) is reported to relate far more
  strongly to political ideology than dangerous-world belief; conservatives score higher (Clifton & Kerry
  2023; the "20 times" figure is from a popular summary and not re-checked).
- In Kahan's cultural cognition work, hierarchical versus egalitarian worldviews predict risk perceptions
  on guns, climate, and more (Kahan et al. 2007).

Positions shaped: redistribution, affirmative action, gender roles, immigration, meritocratic selection,
democracy versus rule by the best.

**Candidate Foundation Labels:** natural hierarchy (Aristotelian); aristocratic / Nietzschean; meritocratic
hierarchy; basic moral equality (Lockean, Kantian); egalitarianism.

**Notes.** **Disguise risk.** SDO items include political prescriptions ("we should not push for group
equality"). The Foundation version must ask what is _natural_ or _true of persons_, leaving "what society
should do about it" to the Political layer. See [Political Dimensions in disguise](#political-dimensions-in-disguise).

### 15. Zero-sum or positive-sum

**Poles:** _One's gain is another's loss_ ↔ _Gains can be shared_

**Definition.** In social and economic life, does one person's or group's gain generally come at another's
expense, or can everyone gain together through cooperation, trade, and growth? The first pole protects
attention to exploitation and real conflicts of interest; the second protects attention to cooperation and
the possibility of mutual benefit.

**Lineage.** Montaigne, _Essays_ Book I ("The profit of one man is the damage of another," 1580);
mercantilism; Hobbes; Adam Smith, _The Wealth of Nations_ (1776) against mercantilism; Marx's theory of
exploitation; Foster, "Peasant Society and the Image of Limited Good" (1965); Thurow, _The Zero-Sum
Society_ (1980).

**Political bearing: strong, and cross-cutting.**

- In a survey of about 20,400 US residents, more zero-sum thinking is associated with **more** support for
  redistribution and race- and gender-based affirmative action **and** with **more restrictive**
  immigration views; the mindset traces to ancestral experiences of mobility, immigration, and enslavement
  (Chinoy, Nunn, Sequeira & Stantcheva 2026, _American Economic Review_).
- Liberals and conservatives both think in zero-sum terms, selectively: conservatives about social
  inequalities (where the status quo is challenged), liberals about economic inequality (Davidai & Ongis
  2019, six studies).
- A cross-cultural scale exists (Różycka-Tran, Boski & Wojciszke 2015, 37 nations).

Positions shaped: redistribution, immigration, trade, affirmative action, attitudes to growth.

**Candidate Foundation Labels:** zero-sum worldview; positive-sum worldview; "image of limited good";
mercantilist; Smithian / commercial-society view; exploitation theory.

**Notes.** One of the best demonstrations that a Foundation Dimension adds resolution: it predicts
positions that left/right splits apart. Domain-specificity (Davidai & Ongis) is again a coherence probe.

### 16. Social ontology: individuals or wholes

**Poles:** _Only individuals are real_ ↔ _Groups and relations are real_

**Definition.** Is society nothing over and above the individuals who make it up (so explanations,
responsibility, and value ultimately rest with individuals), or are communities, nations, classes, and
institutions real entities, with properties, responsibilities, and goods of their own, that shape who
individuals are? The first pole protects individual responsibility and rights against collectives; the
second protects belonging, shared goods, and the ability to name structural causes and collective duties.

**Lineage.** Hobbes (1651); Bentham, _Introduction to the Principles_ (1789: "The community is a fictitious
body"); Weber's methodological individualism (_Economy and Society_, 1922); Popper, _The Open Society and
Its Enemies_ (1945); Hayek. Against: Hegel, _Elements of the Philosophy of Right_ (1820); Durkheim, _The
Rules of Sociological Method_ (1895; social facts); Marx; Taylor, "Atomism" (1985); Sandel, _Liberalism and
the Limits of Justice_ (1982; the "unencumbered self"); MacIntyre, _After Virtue_ (1981); Young,
_Responsibility for Justice_ (2011; structural injustice). SEP: "Methodological Individualism", "Social
Ontology", "Communitarianism".

**Political bearing: moderate, mostly argued; empirical support comes through proxies.** The
liberal–communitarian debate of the 1980s is explicitly about this question. Empirical proxies: Kahan's
individualist versus communitarian worldviews predict risk perceptions (Kahan et al. 2007); structural
versus individual attributions (#3) are the explanatory side of the same question. We found no validated
measure of lay social ontology as such.

Positions shaped: collective responsibility and reparations, group rights and multiculturalism (Kymlicka,
_Multicultural Citizenship_, 1995), hate-speech law, communitarian versus liberal policy, national
identity.

**Candidate Foundation Labels:** methodological / ontological individualism; holism; relational ontology;
communitarianism; structuralism; the "unencumbered self" versus "embedded self".

**Notes.** Overlaps with #3 (structure versus agency as explanation) and #11 (communities as objects of
loyalty). The famous political paraphrase ("there is no such thing as society," Thatcher, 1987 interview)
shows how easily it slides into the Political layer.

### 17. Rights: natural or conventional

**Poles:** _Rights exist before the state_ ↔ _Rights are created by society_

**Definition.** Do people have rights (to life, liberty, property, or their own bodies) simply by being
persons, before and independent of any law; or are rights rules that societies create and can reshape for
good reasons? The first pole protects individuals against majorities and states; the second protects the
ability to design rights around human welfare and changing needs.

**Lineage.** Locke, _Second Treatise_ (1689), §§4–6, 27 (self-ownership and property); the US Declaration
(1776) and French Declaration (1789). Against: Hume, _Treatise_ Book III (justice as convention); Bentham,
"Anarchical Fallacies" (written 1796; rights as "nonsense upon stilts"); Burke (1790; prescriptive
"rights of Englishmen"). Hart, "Are There Any Natural Rights?" (1955); Nozick (1974); Cohen,
_Self-Ownership, Freedom, and Equality_ (1995); Murphy & Nagel, _The Myth of Ownership_ (2002: pre-tax
income is not a natural baseline). SEP: "Rights", "Natural Law Theories".

**Political bearing: argued-only.** It is the explicit premise of libertarian arguments about taxation and
property (Nozick) and of their critics (Murphy & Nagel), and of universal human rights versus national
sovereignty. We found no direct empirical study of lay beliefs about rights' origin and political
positions; libertarian moral psychology (Iyer et al. 2012) is adjacent but measures valuing liberty, not
beliefs about rights' source.

**Candidate Foundation Labels:** natural rights (Lockean); self-ownership; left-libertarianism;
conventionalism about rights (Humean, Benthamite); legal positivism; human-rights universalism.

**Notes.** Close to #6 (natural rights are a realist claim) and to the Political property Dimension.
Candidate for merge into #6 as a facet, unless the locking ticket wants the libertarian tradition to have
a dedicated Foundation hook.

## Family F: knowledge and authority

These three are where Polytic's principle 2 ("objective realism, not relativism") applies most directly:
political philosophies really do rest on different epistemic foundations. They are also where neutral pole
naming is hardest, because one pole tends to sound like "rational" and the other like "irrational." Each
pole below is named and defined by what it _values_.

### 18. Grounds for belief: evidence or conviction

**Poles:** _Belief proportioned to evidence_ ↔ _Belief rightly held on other grounds_

**Definition.** Should you believe things only to the degree the public evidence supports them, or are
some beliefs rightly held on other grounds (faith, intuition, lived experience, commitment, revelation),
especially where evidence cannot settle the question? The first pole protects against self-deception and
manipulation; the second protects the legitimacy of commitments no experiment can test, and of knowledge
that is personal or situated.

**Lineage.** Locke, _Essay_ (1689), IV.xix; Hume, "Of Miracles" (1748); Clifford, "The Ethics of Belief"
(1877: "it is wrong always, everywhere, and for anyone, to believe anything upon insufficient evidence").
Against: Pascal's wager (_Pensées_, 1670); Kierkegaard (_Concluding Unscientific Postscript_, 1846);
James, "The Will to Believe" (1896); Wittgenstein, _On Certainty_ (1969; hinge propositions); Plantinga,
"Reason and Belief in God" (1983; reformed epistemology); standpoint epistemology (Harding, _Whose Science?
Whose Knowledge?_, 1991; Collins, _Black Feminist Thought_, 1990). Peirce's and Popper's fallibilism sit
between. SEP: "Faith", "Pragmatism", "Rationalism vs. Empiricism".

**Political bearing: moderate.**

- Three epistemic beliefs (faith in intuition for facts, need for evidence, and "truth is political")
  predicted misperceptions and conspiracy beliefs across three nationally representative US surveys;
  ideology rarely moderated the effects (Garrett & Weeks 2017).
- The belief that opinions should change with evidence (AOT-E) is associated with political liberalism,
  rejecting traditional moral values, and accepting science, and is less common among the religious
  (Pennycook et al. 2020).
- **Measurement caution:** questionnaire measures of open-minded thinking may be biased against religious
  respondents through item wording (Stanovich & Toplak 2019). The pole definitions above try to avoid this
  by giving the second pole its strongest philosophical form.

Positions shaped: science policy, public health, education (curriculum), the role of religious and
experiential claims in public argument.

**Candidate Foundation Labels:** evidentialism (Clifford); fallibilism (Peirce, Popper); pragmatist belief
(James); fideism; reformed epistemology; intuitionism; standpoint epistemology.

**Notes.** "Truth is political" (Garrett & Weeks) is close to a social-constructionist epistemology and
links to #5. The in-principle/in-practice split is common here ("I go by evidence, except in matters of
faith").

### 19. Epistemic trust: deference or independent judgment

**Poles:** _Defer to expert institutions_ ↔ _Judge for yourself_

**Definition.** When you cannot check something yourself, should you defer to experts and their
institutions (science, academia, the professions, official bodies), or keep final judgment for yourself
and your community, treating institutional claims with independent scrutiny? The first pole protects the
division of epistemic labor that complex societies depend on; the second protects against captured,
mistaken, or self-serving authorities.

**Lineage.** Plato's philosopher-rulers; Locke (1689) and Kant, "What Is Enlightenment?" (1784, _sapere
aude_) for epistemic autonomy; Mill, _On Liberty_ (1859), ch. 2; Lippmann, _Public Opinion_ (1922) versus
Dewey, _The Public and Its Problems_ (1927); Feyerabend, _Science in a Free Society_ (1978); Hardwig,
"Epistemic Dependence" (1985); Goldman, "Experts: Which Ones Should You Trust?" (2001); Nguyen, "Echo
Chambers and Epistemic Bubbles" (2020). SEP: "Social Epistemology", "Epistemological Problems of
Testimony".

**Political bearing: strong (and time-varying).**

- US conservatives began 1974 with the highest trust in science of any group and ended 2010 with the
  lowest; other groups were stable (Gauchat 2012, General Social Survey).
- Anti-intellectualism (general distrust of experts) predicts opposition to expert consensus on climate,
  nuclear power, GMOs, and fluoridation; expert-consensus cues _backfire_ among the anti-intellectual, and
  populist rhetoric activates it (Merkley 2020).
- The partisan gap widened sharply after the COVID-19 pandemic (Pew Research Center 2022).

Positions shaped: public health mandates, climate policy, technocracy versus populism, regulation,
education, trust in media.

**Candidate Foundation Labels:** epistemic deference / dependence (Hardwig); epistemic autonomy (Kantian,
Lockean); technocratic; populist epistemology; Millian open inquiry; scientific realism (as a reason for
deference).

**Notes.** Distinct from #18: a strict evidentialist can distrust institutions (a "do your own research"
evidentialist), and a person of faith can trust science fully. Its political counterpart (epistocracy or
technocracy versus democracy, e.g. Brennan, _Against Democracy_, 2016) belongs in the Political layer.

### 20. Tradition or reason

**Poles:** _Inherited practice carries wisdom_ ↔ _Reason should audit inheritance_

**Definition.** When deciding how society or life should be arranged, how much authority should inherited
practices and institutions have? Do they embody knowledge no individual can reconstruct, so the burden of
proof lies on change; or should practices be judged by explicit reasoning, with no authority from age
alone? The first pole protects tacit knowledge and against unintended consequences; the second protects
against preserving injustice simply because it is old.

**Lineage.** Burke, _Reflections_ (1790); Hume on custom; Hayek, "The Use of Knowledge in Society" (1945)
and _The Constitution of Liberty_ (1960); Oakeshott, _Rationalism in Politics_ (1962); Chesterton, _The
Thing_ (1929; the "fence"); Quinton, _The Politics of Imperfection_ (1978). Against: Descartes, _Discourse
on Method_ (1637, Part 2: the city designed by one architect); Paine, _Rights of Man_ (1791); Godwin
(1793); Bentham. Popper's "piecemeal social engineering" (1945, 1957) is a middle position. SEP:
"Conservatism", "Edmund Burke".

**Political bearing: argued, with indirect support.** This is the philosophical core of conservatism as
SEP and Quinton define it. Empirically, valuing tradition (Schwartz's tradition and conformity values)
reliably predicts conservatism (see the earlier survey), and AOT-E correlates negatively with respect for
tradition (Pennycook et al. 2020). We found no direct measure of the _epistemic_ claim ("tradition
encodes knowledge") as distinct from valuing tradition.

**Candidate Foundation Labels:** Burkean traditionalism; Hayekian evolutionary epistemology; Oakeshottian
anti-rationalism; Chesterton's fence; Enlightenment rationalism; Popperian piecemeal reform.

**Notes.** **Disguise risk:** the Political "change tempo" Dimension (reform versus revolution) is its
downstream expression. Overlaps with #4 (Sowell's constrained vision includes limits on knowledge).

## Family G: ultimate reality and the direction of history

### 21. Transcendence: theism or naturalism

**Poles:** _A transcendent order exists_ ↔ _Nature is all there is_

**Definition.** Is there a reality beyond the natural world (God, gods, a divine order, an afterlife) that
gives existence its meaning and sets its standards; or is the natural world all there is, with meaning made
by humans? The first pole protects meaning, hope, and standards beyond human will; the second protects
human self-reliance and a single, testable picture of reality.

**Lineage.** Aquinas's five ways (_Summa Theologiae_ I q. 2); Pascal (1670); Hume, _Dialogues Concerning
Natural Religion_ (1779); Feuerbach (1841); Nietzsche, _The Gay Science_ (1882: "God is dead"); James, _The
Varieties of Religious Experience_ (1902); Russell, "Why I Am Not a Christian" (1927); Plantinga. SEP:
"Philosophy of Religion", "Naturalism", "Religion and Political Theory".

**Political bearing: strong, but moderated by engagement and by country.**

- Religiosity is among the strongest predictors of cultural conservatism across societies (Norris &
  Inglehart, _Sacred and Secular_, 2004).
- In US data from 1996 to 2008, religiosity predicted conservative attitudes and values strongly among
  the politically engaged and weakly or not at all among the disengaged (Malka et al. 2012). Immigration
  and the death penalty were reported exceptions, where religiosity predicted more liberal views among
  the engaged (per a BPS Research Digest summary; not re-checked in the paper).
- Belief, practice, and belonging have different political effects (Putnam & Campbell, _American Grace_,
  2010).

Positions shaped: abortion, sexuality and marriage, end-of-life policy, education, church–state relations,
family policy.

**Candidate Foundation Labels:** classical theism; deism; pantheism; non-theistic spirituality;
agnosticism; atheism; naturalism; secular humanism.

**Notes.** As a Dimension it needs Pull on both poles to read as a trade-off (an agnostic drawn by both;
a "spiritual but not religious" person). Separate from #7 (source of moral authority) and from the
Political secularism Dimension (religion's place in public life; see Disguise).

### 22. The direction of history

**Poles:** _History is progressing_ ↔ _Progress is not assured_

**Definition.** Is human history, on the whole, a story of moral and material progress (so the future
will likely be better and today's reformers are on "the right side of history"), or is progress fragile,
cyclical, or illusory, with real goods often lost in change? The first pole protects hope and confidence
in reform; the second protects against discounting what past generations knew and built.

**Lineage.** Augustine's providential history (_City of God_); Polybius and Ibn Khaldun (_Muqaddimah_, 1377) on cycles; Vico, _The New Science_ (1725); Turgot (1750); Kant, "Idea for a Universal History"
(1784); Condorcet (1795); Hegel, _Lectures on the Philosophy of History_ (1837); Marx; Spengler, _The
Decline of the West_ (1918–22); Butterfield, _The Whig Interpretation of History_ (1931); Berlin,
"Historical Inevitability" (1954); Popper, _The Poverty of Historicism_ (1957); Nisbet, _History of the
Idea of Progress_ (1980); Lasch, _The True and Only Heaven_ (1991); Fukuyama, _The End of History and the
Last Man_ (1992); Gray, _Straw Dogs_ (2002); Pinker, _Enlightenment Now_ (2018). SEP: "Progress",
"Philosophy of History".

**Political bearing: moderate.**

- In opinion polls spanning about 70 years and many countries, people widely perceive moral decline;
  conservatives perceive a larger decline, though liberals share the belief (Mastroianni & Gilbert 2023,
  _Nature_; the paper's inference that the decline is illusory is contested by critics).
- "Nostalgic deprivation" (the sense of having lost status relative to the past) predicts radical-right
  support among white Americans and Britons (Gest, Reny & Mayer 2018).
- The very name "progressive," and the reactionary tradition's sense of a lost golden age (Lilla, _The
  Shipwrecked Mind_, 2016), show how central this is to political identity.

Positions shaped: attitudes to social change, technology, immigration, national narratives, education
(history curricula).

**Candidate Foundation Labels:** Whig / progressive history; Enlightenment optimism; historical
materialism; providentialism; cyclical history; declinism; anti-historicism (Popper, Berlin); historical
pessimism (Gray).

**Notes.** A related question, _what drives history_ (ideas versus material interests; great individuals
versus impersonal forces), is philosophically important for Marxism but has little evidence of mass
political bearing; treat it as a facet or Off-map (see [Rejected](#rejected-candidates)).

## Family H: the good life (the person's own ethic)

The framework says the Foundation layer includes "the person's own ethic for living," which makes "I'd
never drink, but alcohol should be legal" describable. These candidates capture that personal layer. Their
Political counterparts (state neutrality versus perfectionism; paternalism) live in the Political layer.

### 23. What makes a life go well: subjective or objective

**Poles:** _It depends on the person's own experience and wants_ ↔ _Some things are good for anyone_

**Definition.** Is a person's life going well a matter of their own pleasure, satisfaction, or desires
being fulfilled; or are some things (knowledge, friendship, achievement, virtue, faith) good for a person
whether or not they want them? The first pole protects individuals' authority over their own lives; the
second protects the possibility that people can be wrong about their own good, and the goods worth
encouraging.

**Lineage.** Epicurus; Bentham (1789); Mill, _Utilitarianism_ (1861; "higher pleasures"); Sidgwick (1874);
Aristotle, _Nicomachean Ethics_ (eudaimonia); Finnis (1980; basic goods); Parfit, _Reasons and Persons_
(1984), Appendix I (hedonism, desire-fulfilment, objective list); Hurka, _Perfectionism_ (1993); Sen,
_Development as Freedom_ (1999); Nussbaum, _Women and Human Development_ (2000; capabilities). The
PhilPapers 2020 survey asks "Well-being: hedonism, desire satisfaction, or objective list?" SEP:
"Well-Being", "Perfectionism in Moral and Political Philosophy".

**Political bearing: argued-only.** It is the explicit foundation of the Political neutrality-versus-
perfectionism debate (Rawls, _Political Liberalism_, 1993; Raz 1986), of paternalism (Mill 1859; Thaler &
Sunstein, _Nudge_, 2008), and of the capability approach in development policy. We found no study linking
lay theories of well-being to political positions.

**Candidate Foundation Labels:** hedonism; desire-satisfaction theory; objective-list theory;
perfectionism; eudaimonism; capability approach.

**Notes.** Its political force comes through the gap between "for me" and "for society": an objective-list
person can still be a political neutralist. That gap is a recorded fact, not a Tension, unless the person
feels the pull.

### 24. Self-authorship or a given role

**Poles:** _Author your own life_ ↔ _Live up to a given role_

**Definition.** Is the good life one you author yourself, by discovering and expressing who you are and
choosing your own commitments; or one in which you faithfully fill roles and duties you did not choose
(family, community, faith, vocation, nation), finding yourself through them? The first pole protects
authenticity and freedom from imposed roles; the second protects belonging, fidelity, and the wisdom of
inherited forms of life.

**Lineage.** For self-authorship: Rousseau; Herder; Mill, _On Liberty_ (1859), ch. 3 ("individuality");
Nietzsche; Sartre, "Existentialism Is a Humanism" (1946); Taylor, _The Ethics of Authenticity_ (1991).
For given roles: Confucius, _Analects_; Epictetus (Stoic role ethics); Aristotle; Aquinas; Bradley, "My
Station and Its Duties" (_Ethical Studies_, 1876); MacIntyre, _After Virtue_ (1981). Shweder's "big three"
ethics of autonomy, community, and divinity (Shweder et al. 1997). SEP: "Authenticity", "Communitarianism",
"Virtue Ethics".

**Political bearing: moderate.**

- Using Shweder's three ethics, Jensen (1998) found progressives reason more in the ethic of autonomy and
  the orthodox more in the ethics of community and divinity, in both India and the US.
- At the societal level, the shift from survival to self-expression values tracks liberalizing attitudes
  on gender, sexuality, and authority (Inglehart & Welzel, _Modernization, Cultural Change, and Democracy_,
  2005).

Positions shaped: family policy, gender roles, sexuality, education, national service, religious freedom.

**Candidate Foundation Labels:** authenticity / self-creation (Romantic, Millian, existentialist);
Confucian role ethics; Stoic role ethics; Aristotelian virtue in community; "station and its duties"
(Bradley); ethics of autonomy / community / divinity (Shweder).

**Notes.** The personal-ethic counterpart of #7 and #16. Ethical egoism versus altruism (Stirner, _The Ego
and Its Own_, 1844; Rand, _The Virtue of Selfishness_, 1964; versus Comte's altruism) could be a facet
here or of #11.

### 25. Work: calling or means

**Poles:** _Work is a source of worth_ ↔ _Work is a means to living_

**Definition.** Is work a central source of a person's dignity, character, and meaning, so a good life
involves working hard and well; or is work mainly a means to the things that make life worth living, to
be minimized where possible? The first pole protects the dignity of contribution and self-reliance; the
second protects leisure, family, and freedom from the demand to be productive.

**Lineage.** Luther's "calling"; Weber (1905); Locke's labor theory of property (1689). Marx, _Economic and
Philosophic Manuscripts_ (1844): unalienated labor as self-realization. Against: Aristotle on leisure as
the aim of work; Lafargue, _The Right to Be Lazy_ (1883); Russell, _In Praise of Idleness_ (1935);
Graeber, _Bullshit Jobs_ (2018); Weeks, _The Problem with Work_ (2011).

**Political bearing: moderate.**

- The Protestant work ethic and humanitarian-egalitarian values form separate value clusters that pull
  white Americans in opposite directions on race-related policy (Katz & Hass 1988).
- Work-ethic beliefs are associated with conservatism and opposition to welfare (Furnham, _The Protestant
  Work Ethic_, 1990).

Positions shaped: welfare conditionality and work requirements, universal basic income, working hours,
retirement age, automation.

**Candidate Foundation Labels:** Protestant work ethic; vocation / calling; Marxian self-realization
through labor; anti-work; Aristotelian leisure ideal.

**Notes.** A good non-partisan illustration: the "work as self-realization" pole is shared by Weber's
Puritans and the young Marx, who reach opposite Political conclusions from it. Overlaps with #2 and #3.

## Synthesis

### What the literature supports

1. **There is plenty of real material.** At least a dozen Foundation questions have strong or moderate
   evidence of bearing on specific political positions, and each has a deep philosophical lineage with
   established named positions to anchor Foundation Labels.
2. **The best-evidenced Foundation Dimensions are not just left/right in disguise.** Zero-sum thinking,
   nature-versus-society attributions, free-will attributions, and moral-objectivity intuitions all flip
   by issue or by target. That means the Foundation layer adds resolution, and gives the grill symmetric
   coherence probes ("you said X here; what's different there?") that challenge coherence without
   nudging toward a side.
3. **Evidence is uneven by family.** Strongest for agency/fortune, who counts, the competitive social
   world, religion, and epistemic trust. Weakest for theories of the good, social ontology, and rights,
   which are central in political philosophy but barely studied in the public.
4. **Evidence is mostly correlational, mostly American, and mostly about engaged people.** The global
   skeleton should not assume US patterns (e.g. religiosity's political meaning varies by country), and
   the Result Card should present Foundation–Political links as the person's own reasoning, never as
   causes Polytic has detected.

### A suggested shortlist (not a decision)

If the locking ticket wants a core set with the best evidence and least overlap, about **16** stand out:

> Free will (1), Desert (2), Causes of fortune (3), Human nature (4, possibly absorbing 5), Given or made
> (5), Moral objectivity (6), Sacred or tradeable (10, absorbing 9), Moral circle (11), Standing of nature
> (12), Hierarchy or equality (14), Zero-sum or positive-sum (15), Grounds for belief (18), Epistemic trust
> (19), Transcendence (21), Direction of history (22), Self-authorship or given role (24).

The **second tier** (7, 8, 13, 16, 17, 20, 23, 25) is defensible under the framework's "comprehensive, not
minimal" rule; each either needs a merge decision or has thinner evidence.

### Trade-offs for the locking ticket

- **Comprehensiveness versus session time.** Twenty-five Foundation Dimensions plus a comprehensive
  Political set is a lot for one session. Coverage handles this (most Beliefs will be Not asked or Thin),
  but comparison only counts Dimensions with more than Thin Coverage on both sides, so a large set means
  sparser overlap between a person and a Reference Figure.
- **Distinctness versus probing value.** Merging the responsibility chain (#1–#3) would tidy the set but
  lose the most productive coherence probe Polytic has.
- **Evidence versus tradition.** Political philosophy's central Foundation questions (well-being, rights,
  social ontology) have the least empirical support. Dropping them would leave the Intellectual Bridge
  thin for Rawlsians, communitarians, and libertarians, whose positions are defined by those questions.
- **Neutral naming versus recognizability.** Neutral pole names (e.g. "Belief proportioned to evidence ↔
  Belief rightly held on other grounds") are less familiar than "reason versus faith," but the familiar
  names make one pole sound like the smart one.

### Open questions for "Lock the Dimension set: Foundation and Political"

1. **How many Foundation Dimensions?** About 16 core, all 25, or something in between?
2. **Merges.** Decide the clusters in [Overlaps](#overlaps-and-possible-merges), especially #6/#7/#21,
   #4/#5, and #9/#10/#13.
3. **Do empirical-world beliefs count as Foundation?** #3 (causes of fortune), #15 (zero-sum), and #22
   (history) are partly factual. The framework's definition ("what the person takes to be real") admits
   them, but the Result Card must present them as the person's view, never as right or wrong.
4. **Domain-specific Foundation Beliefs.** Several Foundation Beliefs flip by issue. Should the model
   record that as a Tension, as an in-principle/in-practice split, or as facets of one Belief?
5. **The disguise boundary for scale-based constructs.** SDO and cultural-cognition items mix Foundation
   and Political content. The locking ticket should write the Foundation question for #14 and #16 in
   "what is true" form.
6. **Personal ethic Dimensions.** Is #24 (plus #23 and #25 as facets) enough to capture "the person's own
   ethic for living," or should there be more (e.g. egoism versus altruism; striving versus acceptance)?
7. **Global portability.** Most evidence is from the US. Which Dimensions are likely to be globally
   meaningful (moral circle, transcendence, zero-sum likely are; the specific left/right mapping of
   several is not)?
8. **Measurement.** Few of these have validated instruments. Diagnostic Dilemmas will need to be written
   for each; existing scales (Free Will Inventory, BZSG, moral universalism, NEP, primals) can inform
   them but should not be administered.
